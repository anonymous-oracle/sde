### Calculus and differential equations

- **Limiting values and vanishing fractions**: factorising and rationalising 0/0 forms; standard limits sin x / x and (1 + x)^(1/x); limits by series expansion; L'Hôpital's rule for 0/0 and ∞/∞, proved from the Cauchy mean value theorem; the forms 0·∞, ∞ − ∞, 0⁰ and 1^∞ reduced to them
- **Implicit and parametric differentiation**: the chain rule on F(x, y) = 0 giving dy/dx = −F_x / F_y; parametric curves with dy/dx = y′(t) / x′(t); second derivatives of implicit and parametric curves; tangents and normals to such curves
- **Higher-order derivatives**: the nth derivative of a product by Leibniz's rule; nth derivatives of exponential, sinusoidal and rational functions; Taylor polynomials as derivative-matching approximations
- **Tangents, normals and rates of change**: related rates with respect to time; tangent and normal lines at a point; subtangent and subnormal lengths; the slope of a curve as an instantaneous rate; the derivative from first principles as lim (f(x + h) − f(x))/h, worked for x² and sin x; differentiability at a point as the existence of that limit, which implies continuity but not conversely, as |x| at 0 shows
- **Local and absolute extrema**: critical points; the first- and second-derivative tests; extrema on closed intervals by comparing endpoints and critical points; concavity and inflection points; optimisation problems set up from constraints; the first-derivative test, under which a point c with f′(c) = 0 is a local maximum when f′ changes sign from positive to negative at c and a local minimum when it changes from negative to positive; the second-derivative test, under which f″(c) < 0 at a critical point gives a local maximum and f″(c) > 0 a local minimum, with no conclusion when f″(c) = 0
- **Indefinite integrals and integration techniques**: antiderivatives as inverses of differentiation; substitution; integration by parts and the tabular method; reduction formulas; trigonometric and rational substitutions
- **Partial fractions**: linear, repeated and irreducible quadratic factors; coefficients by equating and by substituting roots; integrating each resulting term; reducing a rational function to a polynomial plus a proper fraction; algebraic simplification of rational expressions by cancelling common factors, with the domain excluding the zeros of the denominator, and the rules for multiplying and dividing them
- **Undetermined coefficients**: matching coefficients for polynomial, exponential and sinusoidal forcing; the resonance case and the multiply-by-x rule; particular integrals of linear constant-coefficient equations; the zero-input response (the homogeneous solution fixed by the initial values) superposed on the zero-state response (the response to the forcing alone, from rest)
- **Integration in elementary terms**: which antiderivatives have closed forms in elementary functions; Liouville's criterion; standard non-elementary examples e^(−x²) and sin x / x; numerical integration when no closed form exists; the composite trapezoid rule, with error −(b − a)h²f″(ξ)/12, and composite Simpson's rule, with error −(b − a)h⁴f⁽⁴⁾(ξ)/180, so that their errors fall as h² and h⁴ for step h
- **Definite integrals and their techniques**: linearity, additivity over intervals, comparison and symmetry; substitution and parts for definite integrals with changed limits; Wallis's reduction formula for powers of sine and cosine; the even–odd rule for symmetric limits, under which ∫₋ₐᵃ f(x) dx = 0 for odd f and ∫₋ₐᵃ f(x) dx = 2∫₀ᵃ f(x) dx for even f
- **Fundamental theorem of calculus**: the two parts; proof of the first from the mean value theorem; the derivative of an integral with variable limits; differentiation under the integral sign (Leibniz's rule); the second part as ∫ₐᵇ f(x) dx = F(b) − F(a) for any antiderivative F of f
- **Improper integrals**: infinite limits and unbounded integrands; comparison and limit-comparison tests; p-integrals; absolute versus conditional convergence; the Gamma integral as a worked example
- **Area between curves and symmetry**: area as the integral of the difference of two curves; intersection points as limits of integration; exploiting symmetry about an axis or the origin; area in parametric form
- **Volumes of revolution**: a solid generated by revolving the region between y = f(x) and the axis about the x-axis has the disc method volume V = π∫ₐᵇ f(x)² dx; a washer with outer radius R(x) and inner radius r(x) has V = π∫ₐᵇ (R² − r²) dx; the shell method about the y-axis gives V = 2π∫ₐᵇ x f(x) dx for a region between y = f(x) and the x-axis with 0 ≤ a < b; choose the method whose integrand is a single function of the variable of integration, and for an axis other than a coordinate axis shift the radii by the distance to that axis
- **Series convergence tests**: partial sums and the Cauchy criterion; comparison, integral, ratio and root tests with the inconclusive case; the alternating series test; absolute and conditional convergence; summation by parts (Abel's formula) with Abel's and Dirichlet's tests; the Cauchy product of two series, with Mertens' theorem on its convergence; rearrangements, under which an absolutely convergent series keeps its sum while a conditionally convergent one can be rearranged to any real sum (Riemann's theorem)
- **Multiple integrals**: double and triple integrals over regions; iterated integrals and Fubini's theorem; change of variables with the Jacobian determinant (polar, cylindrical, spherical); area, volume and centroid; cylindrical coordinates (r cos θ, r sin θ, z) with Jacobian r, and spherical coordinates (ρ sin φ cos θ, ρ sin φ sin θ, ρ cos φ) with Jacobian ρ² sin φ
- **Vector calculus: divergence and curl**: the gradient as the direction of steepest ascent; divergence as local flux density; curl as local rotation; div curl = 0 and curl grad = 0; line integrals and conservative fields
- **Differential forms and Stokes' theorem**: exterior derivative and wedge product; the generalised Stokes theorem, the integral of dω over M equal to the integral of ω over the boundary; recovering the divergence, Green's and classical Stokes theorems; closed and exact forms; the wedge rule dy ∧ dx = −dx ∧ dy, under which the exterior derivative of the 1-form P dx + Q dy is (Q_x − P_y) dx ∧ dy, recovering Green's theorem; the Poincaré lemma, under which a closed form is locally exact
- **Partial differential equations**: classification into elliptic, parabolic and hyperbolic; the heat, wave and Laplace equations; separation of variables; Fourier-series solutions on bounded intervals; boundary and initial conditions; explicit finite-difference schemes for the heat equation u_t = αu_xx, stable only when r = αΔt/Δx² ≤ 1/2; the heat equation derived from Fourier's law of conduction q = −k u_x with conservation of energy, giving u_t = αu_xx with α = k/(ρc), and the wave equation derived from Newton's second law for a small element of a string under tension T, giving u_tt = c²u_xx with c² = T/ρ
- **Second-order linear ODEs**: the Wronskian and linear independence; constant-coefficient solutions by the characteristic equation, with repeated and complex roots; reduction of order; variation of parameters; first-order linear systems x′ = Ax and the second-order equation rewritten as a system of two first-order equations, solved through the matrix exponential e^(At); higher-order linear equations a_n y⁽ⁿ⁾ + ⋯ + a_1 y′ + a_0 y = 0 with constant coefficients, whose characteristic polynomial a_n r^n + ⋯ + a_0 has degree n, each root r giving a solution e^(rt) (a repeated root of multiplicity m gives t^k e^(rt) for k < m, and a complex pair α ± iβ gives e^(αt) cos βt and e^(αt) sin βt), so that n linearly independent solutions span the general solution, independence being tested by the Wronskian
- **Frobenius series, Bessel and Legendre functions**: ordinary and regular singular points; the indicial equation and the Frobenius series; Bessel's equation and Bessel functions of integer order with their zeros; Legendre's equation, the Legendre polynomials and their orthogonality on [−1, 1]; Hermite polynomials as solutions of y″ − 2xy′ + 2ny = 0, orthogonal on the whole line with weight e^(−x²)
- **Gamma and Beta functions**: Γ(s) as the integral of t^(s−1) e^(−t) extending the factorial; Γ(n + 1) = n! and Γ(1/2) = √π; the Beta integral and B(p, q) = Γ(p)Γ(q) / Γ(p + q)
- **First-order ODE solution methods**: separable equations; homogeneous equations by the substitution y = vx; linear equations by the integrating factor; Bernoulli equations reduced to linear form; first-order equations of higher degree solved for the derivative; the order (highest derivative present) and degree (power of that derivative once the equation is free of radicals and fractions) of a differential equation, and forming an equation by eliminating the arbitrary constants of a family
- **Transcendental functions**: the exponential as Σ xⁿ/n! with e = lim (1 + 1/n)ⁿ, and the natural logarithm as the inverse of exp, defined by ∫₁ˣ dt/t, with log(1 + x) = Σ (−1)ⁿ⁺¹ xⁿ/n for |x| < 1; sin and cos by their power series, with sin² + cos² = 1 and derivatives cos and −sin; tan; the inverse trigonometric functions as integrals, such as arctan x = ∫₀ˣ dt/(1 + t²); sinh and cosh with their identities; the complex exponential and logarithm, where log z = ln|z| + i arg z is multivalued up to 2πik and needs a branch cut
- **Direction fields and phase-plane analysis**: the slope field of y′ = f(x, y) and its isoclines f(x, y) = c; autonomous systems x′ = F(x, y), y′ = G(x, y) and their nullclines; equilibria where F = G = 0; linearisation at an equilibrium with the Jacobian matrix, whose eigenvalues classify it: real of opposite sign give a saddle, real of the same sign a node, complex a spiral, and purely imaginary a centre, with the stability of each type; Lyapunov's direct method for a nonlinear system x′ = F(x) with an equilibrium at the origin, where a function V that is positive definite near the origin with V̇ = ∇V · F ≤ 0 proves stability, and V̇ < 0 away from the origin proves asymptotic stability, without solving the system; limit cycles as isolated closed orbits of a planar system, with the Poincaré–Bendixson theorem, under which a bounded trajectory in the plane whose limit set contains no equilibrium is a periodic orbit or spirals onto one
- **Central-force motion and Kepler's laws**: for a force directed toward a fixed origin the angular momentum h = r²θ′ is constant, so the position vector sweeps equal areas in equal times (Kepler's second law, the areal velocity ½h); with u = 1/r and an inverse-square force of strength GM, the orbit equation u″ + u = GM/h² has solutions u = (GM/h²)(1 + e cos(θ − θ₀)), that is r = ℓ/(1 + e cos(θ − θ₀)) with ℓ = h²/GM, a conic with focus at the origin (Kepler's first law); the period of an elliptic orbit with semi-major axis a is T = 2πa^(3/2)/√(GM), so T² is proportional to a³ (Kepler's third law)
- **Numerical ODE solvers**: Euler's method yₙ₊₁ = yₙ + h f(tₙ, yₙ), with local error O(h²) and global error O(h); the classical Runge–Kutta method RK4 with slopes k₁ to k₄ and global error O(h⁴); convergence plots, where the slope of log(error) against log h equals the order; step-size failure; and the stability of explicit Euler on y′ = λy, stable only when |1 + hλ| ≤ 1, with stiff equations as the case where that bound forces tiny steps
- **Boundary value problems and Sturm–Liouville theory**: a second-order problem with conditions at two ends, with y″ + λy = 0 and y(0) = y(π) = 0 as the model case, whose eigenvalues λₙ = n² and eigenfunctions sin(nx) are found; the Sturm–Liouville form (p y′)′ + (q + λw) y = 0 with separated boundary conditions; real eigenvalues forming an increasing unbounded sequence, with eigenfunctions orthogonal under the weight w; the Rayleigh quotient; expansion of a function in eigenfunctions as a generalised Fourier series
- **Potential theory and harmonic functions**: harmonic functions with Δu = u_xx + u_yy = 0; the mean value property, under which u at a point equals the average of u over any circle about it; the maximum principle; the Dirichlet problem and its Poisson integral on the unit disc, u(r, θ) = (1/2π) ∫ (1 − r²) f(φ) / (1 − 2r cos(θ − φ) + r²) dφ; the harmonic conjugate v of u, so that u + iv is holomorphic; Green's function as the solution for a point source
- **Differentiation in several variables**: partial derivatives and the directional derivative D_u f = ∇f · u; the total derivative as the linear map given by the Jacobian matrix, with the chain rule as the product of Jacobians; equality of mixed partials when they are continuous (Schwarz's theorem); the Hessian matrix of second partials, and the second-derivative test at a critical point by the signs of the Hessian's eigenvalues
- **Arc length and rectifiable curves**: the length of a curve as the supremum of the lengths of its inscribed polygons, the curve being rectifiable exactly when that supremum is finite; L = ∫ₐᵇ √(x′(t)² + y′(t)²) dt for parametric curves and ∫ √(1 + f′(x)²) dx for graphs; the arc-length function s(t) with ds/dt = |γ′(t)|; independence of the length from the parametrisation

### Coordinate geometry and conics

- **Straight lines in polar and oblique forms**: the normal form x cos α + y sin α = p; the polar equation of a line r = p sec(θ − α); the oblique form y = mx + c; the distance from a point to a line; the angle between two lines; the family of lines through the intersection of two lines
- **Pair of straight lines**: the homogeneous second-degree equation ax² + 2hxy + by² = 0 as two lines through the origin; the angle between them from tan θ = 2√(h² − ab) / (a + b); the perpendicularity condition a + b = 0; the joint equation of the angle bisectors
- **Transformation of coordinates**: translation and rotation of axes; invariance of distance and of the quadratic invariants; removing the xy term by a rotation through the angle given by tan 2θ = B / (A − C) for Ax² + Bxy + Cy²; affine maps x ↦ Ax + b as a linear map followed by a translation, and affine combinations Σ λᵢxᵢ with Σ λᵢ = 1, which describe the points on lines and planes
- **Systems of circles**: orthogonal circles with d² = r₁² + r₂²; the radical axis as the locus of equal power; the radical centre of three circles; coaxal systems; the pencil of circles S + λL = 0 through the intersections of two circles
- **Tangents, normals and diameters of conics**: the tangent at a point as the polar of that point; the chord of contact; the tangency condition c² = a²m² + b² for the line y = mx + c and the ellipse x²/a² + y²/b² = 1; normal equations; diameters as loci of midpoints of parallel chords; pole and polar; for the parabola y² = 4ax, the line y = mx + c is tangent exactly when c = a/m, touching it at (a/m², 2a/m); the normal at the point (at², 2at) is y = −tx + 2at + at³, which with slope m = −t reads y = mx − 2am − am³; and the diameter of the parabola bisecting chords of slope m is the line y = 2a/m, parallel to the axis
- **Ellipse: auxiliary circle, eccentric angle and conjugate diameters**: the parametric point (a cos θ, b sin θ); the auxiliary circle of radius a; the eccentric angle and the tangent at it; conjugate diameters and the relation between their semi-lengths; the four normals, since from a point (h, k) there are four normals to the ellipse, real or imaginary, and four of them are real exactly when (h, k) lies inside the evolute astroid (ax)^(2/3) + (by)^(2/3) = (a² − b²)^(2/3), two being real when it lies outside
- **Asymptotes of a hyperbola**: asymptotes y = ±(b/a)x; the asymptotic pair x²/a² − y²/b² = 0 as the limit of the hyperbola; the rectangular hyperbola xy = c²; a hyperbola fixed by its asymptotes and one point on it
- **Polar equation of a conic**: the focus–directrix definition with e = PS / PM; the polar form r = l / (1 + e cos θ); the semi-latus rectum l; eccentricity separating parabola, ellipse and hyperbola; focal chords and their lengths
- **General second-degree equation and tracing of conics**: classification by the sign of B² − 4AC and the invariant determinant; rotation to remove the cross term, then translation to the centre; tracing parabolas, ellipses and hyperbolas from the reduced form
- **Lines and planes in three dimensions**: the parametric line x = p + t·d; the plane n·(x − p) = 0 with normal n given by the cross product of two direction vectors; the distance from a point q to a plane, |n·(q − p)| / ‖n‖; the angle between two planes from the angle between their normals; the cross product u × v = (u₂v₃ − u₃v₂, u₃v₁ − u₁v₃, u₁v₂ − u₂v₁), with magnitude ‖u‖‖v‖ sin θ, which is the area of the parallelogram spanned by u and v, a direction perpendicular to both by the right-hand rule, zero for parallel vectors, and anticommutative, u × v = −(v × u); the triple product (u × v) · w as the signed volume of the parallelepiped spanned by u, v and w
- **Quadric surfaces**: the central quadrics, namely the ellipsoid x²/a² + y²/b² + z²/c² = 1, the hyperboloid of one sheet x²/a² + y²/b² − z²/c² = 1 and the hyperboloid of two sheets −x²/a² − y²/b² + z²/c² = 1; the elliptic paraboloid z = x²/a² + y²/b², the hyperbolic paraboloid z = x²/a² − y²/b², the elliptic cone and the cylinders; plane sections of each; reduction of a general quadric by a rotation that diagonalises the symmetric matrix of its quadratic part, with the type read from the signs of its eigenvalues
- **Polar coordinates and polar curves**: x = r cos θ, y = r sin θ, r = √(x² + y²), with θ read by quadrant from tan θ = y / x; the polar curve r = f(θ) and its tangent slope dy/dx = (r′ sin θ + r cos θ) / (r′ cos θ − r sin θ); the area of a polar sector ½ ∫ r² dθ; the area element r dr dθ in double integrals

### Real analysis

- **Construction of the real numbers**: Dedekind cuts and Cauchy-sequence completion of Q; equivalence classes of sequences; the arithmetic and order of the constructed reals; uniqueness of a complete ordered field up to isomorphism
- **Field axioms and ordered-field properties**: the field axioms for addition and multiplication; the order axioms; consequences such as uniqueness of inverses and non-negativity of squares; the Archimedean property; why Q is an ordered field without the least upper bound property
- **Supremum and the completeness axiom**: least upper bound and greatest lower bound; the approximation property; the monotone convergence theorem; the nested interval property; the Archimedean property derived from completeness; the extended real line R ∪ {−∞, +∞}, where the supremum of a set unbounded above is +∞, and the forms ∞ − ∞, 0·∞ and ∞/∞ are left undefined
- **Epsilon-delta limits and continuity**: the formal definition of a limit of a function; limit laws proved from the definition; the sequential criterion for continuity; uniform continuity and how it differs from continuity on an interval; uniform continuity as the requirement that, for each ε > 0, one δ > 0 works at every pair of points, |x − y| < δ implying |f(x) − f(y)| < ε, so that δ depends only on ε and not on the point; the theorem that a continuous function on a closed bounded interval is uniformly continuous (Heine–Cantor), proved by bisection or from the Bolzano–Weierstrass theorem, with 1/x on (0, 1] as a continuous function that is not uniformly continuous
- **Intermediate value and extreme value theorems**: proof by bisection from the completeness axiom; continuous functions on closed bounded intervals attain a maximum and a minimum; consequences for roots and fixed points
- **Infinite limits and limits at infinity**: lim as x → a of f(x) = ∞ means that for every M there is δ > 0 with f(x) > M whenever 0 < |x − a| < δ; lim as x → ∞ of f(x) = L means that for every ε > 0 there is N with |f(x) − L| < ε for all x > N; the sequential criterion and the limit laws carry over, and a rational function's limit at infinity is read from the leading terms of numerator and denominator, with degree comparison deciding between 0, a finite nonzero ratio and ∞
- **Monotone functions and discontinuities**: monotone functions have one-sided limits; their discontinuities are countable; removable, jump and essential discontinuities; continuity of the inverse of a strictly monotone continuous function
- **Metric spaces**: metric axioms; open and closed sets; interior, closure and limit points; Cauchy sequences and completeness; continuous maps between metric spaces; R^n with the Euclidean and maximum metrics; bounded continuous functions with the supremum metric
- **Compactness and connectedness**: sequential compactness and the finite subcover property; the Heine–Borel theorem in R^n; compact sets are closed and bounded; continuous images of compact sets; connected sets and the intermediate value theorem
- **Cauchy sequences, subsequences, limsup and liminf**: the ε–N definition of a convergent sequence and uniqueness of limits; algebra of limits proved from the definition; bounded and monotone sequences and the monotone convergence theorem; Bolzano–Weierstrass; Cauchy sequences converge in R (completeness of the reals); limsup and liminf as extreme subsequential limits; a sequence converges exactly when its limsup equals its liminf; the special limits n^(1/n) → 1, since writing n^(1/n) = 1 + hₙ and expanding gives n(n − 1)hₙ²/2 ≤ n, so hₙ → 0, and (1 + x/n)ⁿ → eˣ, since n·log(1 + x/n) → x
- **Uniform convergence and power series**: pointwise versus uniform convergence; the Weierstrass M-test; preservation of continuity, integrability and differentiability under uniform limits; radius of convergence by the Cauchy–Hadamard formula; power series as differentiable functions inside their radius
- **Rolle, mean value and Taylor theorems**: Rolle's theorem; the Lagrange and Cauchy mean value theorems; monotonicity from the sign of the derivative; Taylor's theorem with the Lagrange remainder and its error bound
- **Continuity of derivatives**: f is continuously differentiable (C¹) when f′ exists at every point and is continuous; differentiability does not imply C¹, since f(x) = x² sin(1/x) with f(0) = 0 has f′(0) = 0 while f′(x) = 2x sin(1/x) − cos(1/x) oscillates without a limit at 0; a derivative cannot have a jump discontinuity, since every derivative has the intermediate value property (Darboux's theorem), so any discontinuity of f′ is of the oscillatory kind
- **Stone–Weierstrass theorem**: density of polynomials in C[a, b] (Weierstrass approximation); Bernstein polynomials as a constructive proof sketch; the lattice and point-separating form of the theorem; approximation of a continuous function by polynomials to a stated error
- **Inverse and implicit function theorems**: the derivative as a linear map and the Jacobian; the inverse function theorem in R^n by the contraction principle; the implicit function theorem giving a local graph of a level set; the rank theorem in outline; the constant-rank theorem says that if a C^r map F has constant rank k near a point, there are local coordinates in which F takes the normal form (x₁, …, xₙ) ↦ (x₁, …, xₖ, 0, …, 0), so every constant-rank map looks like a projection in suitable charts; the inverse function theorem is the case of full rank n for a map between spaces of equal dimension, and the implicit function theorem is the case of a surjective derivative, where the level set is locally the graph of a function and so a submanifold
- **Riemann and Riemann–Stieltjes integration**: Darboux upper and lower sums; integrability criteria; Riemann sums converging to the integral; the Stieltjes integral with respect to a monotone integrator; existence for continuous integrands; integration by parts for Stieltjes integrals
- **Vector-valued integrals and rectifiable curves**: for f : [a, b] → ℝᵏ the Riemann or Riemann–Stieltjes integral is taken componentwise, ∫ f dα = (∫ f₁ dα, …, ∫ fₖ dα), with ‖∫f‖ ≤ ∫‖f‖; the length of a curve γ is the supremum over partitions of Σ‖γ(tᵢ) − γ(tᵢ₋₁)‖, finite exactly for a rectifiable curve, and equal to ∫ₐᵇ ‖γ′(t)‖ dt when γ is continuously differentiable
- **Lebesgue measure and integration**: outer measure and measurable sets; construction of Lebesgue measure on R; measurable and simple functions; the Lebesgue integral with the monotone and dominated convergence theorems; comparison with the Riemann integral; σ-algebras of measurable sets, closed under complements and countable unions, and the Borel σ-algebra generated by the open sets
- **Complex-valued functions in Lebesgue theory**: f = u + iv is measurable exactly when its real part u and imaginary part v are, and integrable when |f| is; ∫f = ∫u + i∫v, with |∫f| ≤ ∫|f|; the space L² of complex functions with ∫|f|² < ∞ carries the Hermitian inner product ⟨f, g⟩ = ∫ f ḡ
- **Irrationality and transcendence proofs**: irrationality of √2 and of e (Fourier's proof); Niven's proof of the irrationality of π; Liouville's bound on the approximation of algebraic numbers by rationals; the transcendence of e by Hermite's method, stated as a theorem
- **Equicontinuity and the Arzelà–Ascoli theorem**: a family F is equicontinuous when, for each ε > 0, one δ works for every f in F at every pair of points closer than δ; pointwise boundedness; Arzelà–Ascoli, under which a pointwise bounded, equicontinuous family of functions on a compact set is relatively compact in the sup norm, so every sequence in it has a uniformly convergent subsequence
- **Perfect sets and the Cantor set**: a perfect set is a closed set with no isolated points; the Cantor set, obtained by removing open middle thirds, is perfect, uncountable, nowhere dense and of measure zero; every non-empty perfect subset of R has the cardinality of the continuum
- **Partitions of unity**: for an open cover {Uᵢ}, continuous functions φᵢ ≥ 0 with support inside Uᵢ, locally finite, summing to 1; existence for open covers of metric spaces, built from the smooth bump function e^(−1/x) for x > 0 and 0 otherwise; their use in gluing local constructions into a global one
- **Separation axioms, quotient and product topology**: the separation axioms T0 to T4, namely T1 (points are closed), T2 (Hausdorff: distinct points have disjoint neighbourhoods), T3 (regular) and T4 (normal: disjoint closed sets have disjoint open neighbourhoods), with every metric space normal; the quotient topology as the finest topology that makes a quotient map continuous; the product topology with the products of open sets as a basis, and Tychonoff's theorem at statement level, that a product of compact spaces is compact

### Complex and functional analysis

- **Complex number algebra**: the field C and complex conjugation; modulus and principal argument; the Argand plane; polar and Euler form e^(iθ); triangle inequalities; de Moivre's theorem and the n-th roots of unity as the vertices of a regular polygon; the fundamental theorem of algebra, under which every non-constant polynomial over C has a root, so that C is algebraically closed (proof by Liouville's theorem or by the minimum-modulus argument)
- **Complex functions**: holomorphic functions and the Cauchy–Riemann equations; power series as holomorphic functions; the exponential and logarithm with branch cuts; Möbius maps and conformal geometry; conformal maps as holomorphic maps with nonzero derivative, which preserve angles; the Riemann mapping theorem, under which every simply connected open subset of C other than C and the empty set is conformally equivalent to the unit disc; complex power series Σ aₙ(z − z₀)ⁿ with radius of convergence 1/limsup |aₙ|^(1/n) (Cauchy–Hadamard), holomorphic inside that disc, with aₙ = f⁽ⁿ⁾(z₀)/n!
- **Cauchy's theorem and residues**: Cauchy's theorem for closed contours; Cauchy's integral formula; Taylor and Laurent series; classification of singularities; residues and the residue theorem; real integrals evaluated by contour integration
- **Hilbert spaces and bounded linear operators**: inner product spaces and the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality; completeness; orthonormal bases and Parseval's identity; the orthogonal projection theorem; bounded operators with their norms and adjoints
- **Hahn–Banach and dual spaces**: the dual space of a normed space; the Hahn–Banach extension theorem in analytic and geometric forms; separation of convex sets; the Riesz representation theorem for the dual of a Hilbert space; the analytic form, under which a linear functional f on a subspace that satisfies f ≤ p for a sublinear functional p extends to the whole space with f ≤ p still holding (by Zorn's lemma, or by one-dimensional extension steps); the geometric form, under which a convex set and a disjoint open convex set are separated by a closed hyperplane {x : φ(x) = c} with φ continuous and linear, so that φ ≤ c on one set and φ ≥ c on the other
- **Self-adjoint operators and the spectral theorem**: self-adjoint operators and their real spectrum; orthogonality of eigenvectors for distinct eigenvalues; the spectral theorem for compact self-adjoint operators; the finite-dimensional matrix case as the special instance
- **Banach spaces**: complete normed spaces; ℓᵖ, Lᵖ and C[a, b] with the supremum norm as examples; bounded linear operators between normed spaces with the operator norm ‖T‖ = sup over ‖x‖ ≤ 1 of ‖Tx‖; the space of bounded operators from one Banach space to another being itself a Banach space when the target is complete
- **Open mapping and closed graph theorems**: a surjective bounded linear map between Banach spaces is an open map, proved from the Baire category theorem, so a bounded linear bijection between Banach spaces has a bounded inverse; a linear map between Banach spaces whose graph is closed is bounded

### Number theory

- **Primitive roots and the index**: the order of an element modulo n; primitive roots exist exactly for n = 1, 2, 4, pᵏ and 2pᵏ with p an odd prime; the index (discrete logarithm) and its laws; testing for a primitive root through the prime divisors of φ(n)
- **Continued fractions**: finite and infinite expansions of rationals and irrationals; convergents from the recurrences and the determinant identity; best rational approximation; periodic expansions of quadratic irrationals (Lagrange's theorem)
- **Diophantine approximation**: Dirichlet's approximation theorem, |x − p/q| < 1/q²; Liouville's bound for algebraic numbers; convergents as best approximations; the irrationality measure
- **Linear and second-degree Diophantine equations**: solvability of ax + by = c by the gcd; the general solution from Bézout's identity; second-degree equations reduced to forms x² − Dy² = N and to sums of squares
- **Systems of linear Diophantine equations**: Ax = b over ℤ is solvable exactly when, writing the Smith normal form A = UDV with U and V unimodular and D = diag(d₁, …, d_r, 0, …) with d₁ | ⋯ | d_r, the transformed vector c = U⁻¹b satisfies dᵢ | cᵢ for i ≤ r and cᵢ = 0 for i > r; the general solution is x = Vy with y ∈ ℤⁿ, yᵢ = cᵢ / dᵢ for i ≤ r and the remaining yᵢ free; the same test is run on a Hermite normal form, an upper-triangular form reached by column operations, whose back-substitution gives the solution
- **Pell's equation**: x² − Dy² = 1 for non-square D; the fundamental solution from the period of the continued fraction of √D; generating all solutions from the fundamental one; the equation x² − Dy² = N and its bounded solutions
- **Pythagorean triples and rational points on the unit circle**: the primitive parametrisation (m² − n², 2mn, m² + n²) with coprime m and n of opposite parity; rational points on x² + y² = 1 from lines through (−1, 0)
- **Sums of higher powers**: Faulhaber's formulas for sums of k-th powers; Bernoulli numbers from their generating function; the polynomial form of the sum, checked by induction
- **Polygonal numbers**: triangular, square and k-gonal numbers and their closed forms; Fermat's polygonal number theorem that every positive integer is a sum of k k-gonal numbers, with the k = 4 case as Lagrange's four-square theorem
- **Fibonacci numbers**: Binet's formula; gcd(F_m, F_n) = F_gcd(m,n); the divisibility F_m | F_mn; Zeckendorf's representation; the golden ratio as the limit of ratios
- **Möbius inversion and arithmetic functions**: multiplicative functions τ, σ and φ; the Möbius function μ; the Möbius inversion formula; Dirichlet convolution of arithmetic functions; the Möbius function μ(n) = 1 for n = 1, (−1)ᵏ when n is a product of k distinct primes, and 0 when n has a squared prime factor; the inversion, under which f(n) = Σ_(d|n) g(d) for all n if and only if g(n) = Σ_(d|n) μ(d) f(n/d); the Euler totient by its product formula φ(n) = n·∏_(p|n)(1 − 1/p) over the primes p dividing n; and the divisor functions τ(n) = Σ_(d|n) 1 and σ(n) = Σ_(d|n) d
- **Partitions and generating functions**: the partition function p(n) and the product generating function of 1/(1 − xᵏ); Euler's pentagonal number theorem; partitions into distinct parts equal to partitions into odd parts
- **Perfect, Mersenne and amicable numbers**: the Euclid–Euler theorem linking even perfect numbers to Mersenne primes; the divisor-sum function σ(n); amicable pairs and their search; why odd perfect numbers remain open; the Mersenne numbers Mₚ = 2ᵖ − 1, where Mₚ can be prime only when the exponent p is prime (since 2^(ab) − 1 has 2^a − 1 as a factor), though the converse fails (M₁₁ = 23 · 89); and the Lucas–Lehmer test for an odd prime p, in which s₀ = 4 and sᵢ₊₁ = sᵢ² − 2 (mod Mₚ), with Mₚ prime exactly when s_(p−2) ≡ 0 (mod Mₚ)
- **Quadratic reciprocity and the Jacobi symbol**: the Legendre symbol and Euler's criterion; Gauss's lemma; the quadratic reciprocity law; the Jacobi symbol and its evaluation by reciprocity without factoring; the quadratic reciprocity law, under which for distinct odd primes p and q, (p/q)(q/p) = (−1)^((p−1)(q−1)/4), so (p/q) = (q/p) unless both p and q are ≡ 3 (mod 4); the supplementary laws (−1/p) = (−1)^((p−1)/2) and (2/p) = (−1)^((p²−1)/8); Euler's criterion, (a/p) ≡ a^((p−1)/2) (mod p) for an odd prime p not dividing a; and the Jacobi symbol (m/n) = ∏ (m/pᵢ)^(eᵢ) for odd n = ∏ pᵢ^(eᵢ), which satisfies (m/n)(n/m) = (−1)^((m−1)(n−1)/4) for odd positive coprime m and n
- **Wilson's theorem and Gauss's lemma**: (p − 1)! ≡ −1 (mod p) and its converse; Gauss's lemma counting least residues in the upper half; reading the Legendre symbol from that count
- **Wolstenholme's and von Staudt's theorems**: the von Staudt–Clausen theorem on the denominators of Bernoulli numbers; Wolstenholme's congruence C(2p − 1, p − 1) ≡ 1 (mod p³) for primes p ≥ 5; the p-adic valuation of binomial coefficients
- **Decimal expansions of rationals**: the period of 1/n and the order of 10 modulo n; purely and mixed periodic expansions; the period of 1/p dividing p − 1; full-reptend primes
- **Gauss sums and constructible regular polygons**: the quadratic Gauss sum and its evaluation; ruler-and-compass constructibility of the regular n-gon exactly when n is 2ᵏ times a product of distinct Fermat primes (Gauss–Wantzel); the Fermat numbers Fₙ = 2^(2ⁿ) + 1, which satisfy F₀F₁⋯F_(n−1) = Fₙ − 2, so that any two distinct Fermat numbers are coprime, giving a second proof that there are infinitely many primes; the known Fermat primes F₀ = 3, F₁ = 5, F₂ = 17, F₃ = 257 and F₄ = 65537, with F₅ = 641 · 6700417 composite
- **Farey series and Minkowski's theorem**: the Farey sequence of order n and the determinant-1 property of neighbouring fractions; the mediant property; Minkowski's theorem that a symmetric convex body of volume above 2ⁿ contains a non-zero lattice point (statement and proof sketch)
- **Lifting the exponent lemma and Hensel's lemma**: the p-adic valuation of aⁿ − bⁿ for odd primes p; Hensel's lemma lifting simple roots of a polynomial congruence from mod p to mod pᵏ; prime-power congruences
- **Quadratic forms and sums of squares**: the sum of two squares theorem; Lagrange's four-square theorem; binary quadratic forms with their discriminant and equivalence; representation numbers of binary forms; Gauss's three-square theorem, under which n is a sum of three integer squares exactly when n is not of the form 4ᵃ(8b + 7); the reduction theory of positive definite binary forms, under which every form is equivalent to a reduced form ax² + bxy + cy² with |b| ≤ a ≤ c, and b ≥ 0 when |b| = a or a = c; the class number h(D), the number of classes of forms of discriminant D; and the sum of two squares criterion, that n = x² + y² has an integer solution exactly when every prime ≡ 3 (mod 4) divides n to an even power
- **Algebraic structures: groups, rings and fields**: the group of units of Z/nZ and Euler's totient; Lagrange's theorem for finite groups; rings and fields of residues; Fermat's little theorem as a consequence of group order
- **Finite fields**: existence and uniqueness of the field with pⁿ elements; the multiplicative group of a finite field is cyclic; irreducible polynomials over Fₚ; the Frobenius map x ↦ xᵖ
- **Rational points on curves**: parametrising rational points on a conic from one known rational point; Fermat's method of descent; Mordell's theorem at statement level (the rational points of an elliptic curve form a finitely generated group)
- **Elliptic curves in Weierstrass form**: y² = x³ + ax + b with discriminant −16(4a³ + 27b²) ≠ 0, so the curve is nonsingular, together with a point O at infinity; the chord-and-tangent group law, in which P + Q is the reflection in the x-axis of the third point where the line PQ meets the curve (the tangent at P when P = Q), with O as the identity, so that −(x, y) = (x, −y); the resulting group is abelian, and Mordell's theorem makes its rational points finitely generated (see the landmark results below)
- **Primality testing**: the Fermat test and Carmichael numbers; the Miller–Rabin strong probable-prime test with error at most 1/4 per base; deterministic witness sets for 64-bit integers; the AKS test as a polynomial-time deterministic test (statement level)
- **Integral quadratic forms of discriminant ±1**: for an integral form ax² + bxy + cy² the discriminant b² − 4ac is congruent to 0 or 1 mod 4, so discriminant −1 never occurs; a form of discriminant 1 has rational roots, and since it is primitive Gauss's lemma factors it over ℤ as a product of two integer linear forms (px + qy)(rx + sy) with ps − qr = ±1, because the discriminant equals (ps − qr)²; the unimodular substitution then makes it equivalent under GL₂(ℤ) to xy
- **Landmark results (statement level)**: Goldbach's conjecture (every even integer above 2 is a sum of two primes); Fermat's Last Theorem (no positive integers satisfy xⁿ + yⁿ = zⁿ for n ≥ 3, proved by Wiles with Taylor's help); the ABC conjecture (for each ε > 0, only finitely many coprime a + b = c have rad(abc) < c^(1−ε)); elliptic curves (rational points form a finitely generated abelian group, the Mordell–Weil theorem); modular forms (holomorphic functions transforming under the modular group, with theta and Eisenstein series as examples); the prime number theorem (π(x) ~ x / ln x); the Riemann zeta function (ζ(s) = Σ n^(−s) for Re s > 1, its analytic continuation, and the open Riemann hypothesis on its zeros); Dirichlet series (Σ aₙ n^(−s), with ζ as the basic case); Dirichlet's theorem (infinitely many primes in each reduced residue class mod m); the Hasse–Minkowski theorem (a rational quadratic form represents zero exactly when it does so over R and over every Qₚ, using the Hilbert symbol); p-adic numbers (the completion of Q for the p-adic absolute value, containing Z_p)
- **p-adic absolute value and expansions**: for a nonzero rational x, v_p(x) is the exponent of the prime p in x, and |x|_p = p^(−v_p(x)), with |0|_p = 0; the ultrametric (strong triangle) inequality |x + y|_p ≤ max(|x|_p, |y|_p), with equality whenever |x|_p ≠ |y|_p; ℤ_p = {x : |x|_p ≤ 1} as the p-adic integers; and every nonzero x in ℚ_p has a unique expansion x = Σ_(k ≥ k₀) aₖ pᵏ with digits 0 ≤ aₖ < p and a_(k₀) ≠ 0
- **Modular forms and cusp forms**: a modular form of weight k is holomorphic on the upper half-plane and satisfies f((aτ + b)/(cτ + d)) = (cτ + d)ᵏ f(τ) for all integer matrices with ad − bc = 1, and it is holomorphic at the cusp, with q-expansion f(τ) = Σ_(n ≥ 0) aₙ qⁿ where q = e^(2πiτ); Eisenstein series are examples, and a cusp form is one with a₀ = 0, the basic cusp form being Δ = q∏(1 − qⁿ)²⁴ = Σ τ(n) qⁿ of weight 12
- **Dirichlet series and Euler products**: D(s) = Σ aₙ n^(−s) converges for Re s > σ_c and diverges for Re s < σ_c, its abscissa of convergence σ_c, and converges absolutely for Re s > σ_a, with σ_c ≤ σ_a ≤ σ_c + 1; for a multiplicative sequence aₙ with a₁ = 1, the Euler product D(s) = ∏_p Σ_(k ≥ 0) a_(p^k) p^(−ks) holds where the series converge absolutely, so that ζ(s) = ∏_p (1 − p^(−s))^(−1) for Re s > 1
- **Prime number theorem formulations**: π(x) ~ x / ln x, equivalently π(x) ~ li(x) = ∫₂ˣ dt / ln t, and equivalently, for the Chebyshev function ψ(x) = Σ_(n ≤ x) Λ(n) with Λ the von Mangoldt function, ψ(x) ~ x; the analytic statement that ζ(s) has no zeros on the line Re s = 1, which implies the theorem (Hadamard and de la Vallée Poussin)
- **Infinitude of primes**: Euclid's argument that a finite list p₁, …, pₖ of primes misses a prime, since any prime factor of N = p₁⋯pₖ + 1 leaves remainder 1 on division by each pᵢ, so it is a prime outside the list
- **Greatest integer function and Legendre's formula**: ⌊x⌋ is the largest integer not exceeding x, with ⌊x + n⌋ = ⌊x⌋ + n for integers n and Hermite's identity ⌊x⌋ + ⌊x + ½⌋ = ⌊2x⌋; the exponent of a prime p in n! is Legendre's formula v_p(n!) = Σ_(k ≥ 1) ⌊n / pᵏ⌋ = (n − s_p(n)) / (p − 1), where s_p(n) is the sum of the base-p digits of n
- **Factoring integers**: trial division up to √n; Pollard's rho method, which iterates x ↦ x² + c (mod n) and finds a factor as gcd(|xᵢ − x_(2i)|, n) by cycle detection, expected in about √p steps for the least prime factor p; and the quadratic sieve, which collects congruences x² ≡ y (mod n) with y smooth over a factor base and solves the resulting linear system over GF(2) for a difference of squares, with running time exp((1 + o(1))√(ln n · ln ln n))
- **Ordinary and exponential generating functions**: the formal power series A(x) = Σ aₙxⁿ, with products giving convolutions of coefficients and the coefficient [xⁿ] read off by expansion; rational generating functions and the linear recurrences they encode through the denominator's characteristic polynomial, with the Fibonacci series x / (1 − x − x²) as the case; the Catalan generating function C(x) = 1 + xC(x)² and its closed form (1 − √(1 − 4x)) / (2x); exponential generating functions Σ aₙxⁿ/n! for labelled structures, where products give binomial convolutions
- **Trigonometric sums over roots of unity**: Σ from k = 0 to n − 1 of e^(2πik/n) = 0 for n ≥ 2; the Dirichlet kernel Σ from k = −n to n of e^(ikx) = sin((n + ½)x) / sin(x/2); the value cos(2π/5) = (√5 − 1)/4, from 1 + 2cos(2π/5) + 2cos(4π/5) = 0 and cos(4π/5) = 2cos²(2π/5) − 1
- **Hilbert symbol**: for nonzero a, b in Qₚ, (a, b)ₚ = 1 when z² = ax² + by² has a non-trivial solution in Qₚ and −1 otherwise; symmetric and multiplicative in each argument; for an odd prime p, with a = pᵅu and b = pᵝv for units u and v, (a, b)ₚ = (−1)^(αβ(p − 1)/2) (u/p)ᵝ (v/p)ᵅ, with (·/p) the Legendre symbol; Hilbert's reciprocity, under which the product of (a, b)ᵥ over all places v, including the real place, is 1
- **Sylow's theorems and the structure theorem for finite abelian groups**: Cauchy's theorem, that a prime p dividing |G| gives an element of order p; Sylow's theorems: for |G| = pⁿm with p not dividing m, Sylow p-subgroups of order pⁿ exist, are all conjugate, and their number n_p divides m and satisfies n_p ≡ 1 (mod p), with a Sylow subgroup normal exactly when n_p = 1; the finite abelian structure theorem, that every such group is a direct product of cyclic groups of prime-power order, unique up to order, and equally a product Z/d₁ × … × Z/dₖ with d₁ | d₂ | … | dₖ
- **Rings, ideals and modules**: a ring with unit; ideals, quotient rings R/I and the first isomorphism theorem; prime ideals, where R/P is an integral domain, and maximal ideals, where R/M is a field; modules over a ring as abelian groups with a compatible scalar action, with submodules, quotients and homomorphisms, and abelian groups as Z-modules; the structure theorem for finitely generated modules over a principal ideal domain, a free part Rʳ plus torsion summands R/(d₁) ⊕ … ⊕ R/(dₖ) with d₁ | … | dₖ
- **Field extensions and Galois theory**: the tower law [L : K] = [L : M][M : K]; the degree of K(α) equal to the degree of the minimal polynomial of α; splitting fields; Galois extensions as normal and separable ones, with |Gal(L/K)| = [L : K]; the fundamental theorem of Galois theory, the inclusion-reversing bijection between subgroups H of Gal(L/K) and intermediate fields, with [L : K^H] = |H|; solvability by radicals as solvability of the Galois group, with the symmetric group on five letters as the Galois group of a general quintic, which is not solvable
