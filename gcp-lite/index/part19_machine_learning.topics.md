  - First-order logic: quantifiers, unification, lifted forward chaining and resolution; Prolog's execution model
- **Knowledge representation and expert systems**: ontologies, semantic networks, frames and rules; non-monotonic reasoning (default logic, truth maintenance); expert-system architecture (knowledge base, inference engine, explanation) and applications; AI programming languages (Lisp, Prolog, CLIPS)
- **Planning**: STRIPS and PDDL; forward and backward state-space planning; planning graphs and GraphPlan; partial-order planning; planning with scheduling and resources
- **Reasoning under uncertainty**
  - Probability for AI: conditional independence and Bayes' rule; A and B are conditionally independent given C when P(A, B | C) = P(A | C)P(B | C), which lets a joint distribution over many variables factor into small local tables; Bayes' rule P(A | B) = P(B | A)P(A) ÷ P(B) turns a likelihood and a prior into a posterior, so a test with 99% sensitivity and a 1% false-positive rate, applied to a condition with base rate 1 in 1,000, gives a positive result a posterior of only about 9%, since false positives from the large negative class outnumber the true cases
  - Bayesian networks: structure, factorization, d-separation; exact inference by enumeration and variable elimination; NP-hardness by reduction from 3-SAT
  - Approximate inference: rejection sampling, likelihood weighting, Markov chain Monte Carlo with Gibbs sampling; Metropolis–Hastings proposing x′ from q(x′ | x) and accepting with probability min(1, π(x′) q(x | x′) ÷ π(x) q(x′ | x)), which leaves the target π stationary by detailed balance; Gibbs sampling as the update of one variable at a time from its full conditional, which is Metropolis–Hastings with acceptance probability 1; convergence to π for an irreducible, aperiodic chain, with the ergodic theorem making time averages converge to expectations; burn-in before draws are kept; and the effective sample size N ÷ (1 + 2 Σₖ ρₖ) of N autocorrelated draws, with the split R̂ as the between-chain to within-chain variance check
  - Learning Bayesian networks: maximum likelihood, MAP and Bayesian parameter learning; structure learning; expectation–maximization for hidden variables; MAP as the mode of the posterior, argmax over θ of log p(D | θ) + log p(θ), where a Gaussian prior gives the ridge penalty and a Laplace prior gives the lasso penalty
  - Dynamic Bayesian networks; filtering; hidden Markov models as a special case; a dynamic Bayesian network repeats one network per time slice, with arcs from slice t to slice t + 1; filtering computes P(state at t | observations up to t) recursively, predicting the next state with the transition model and then updating with the likelihood of the new observation, which is the forward algorithm for an HMM and the Kalman filter for a linear-Gaussian model; an HMM is the special case with one hidden state variable and one observation per slice
- **Fuzzy logic**: fuzzy sets and membership functions; fuzzy operators and rules; fuzzy inference with fuzzification and defuzzification (centroid)
- **Multi-agent systems**: agent communication and negotiation; coordination (contract net); web-based and mobile agents
- **Robotics**: sensors and actuators; configuration space and motion planning; localization and mapping; the sense–plan–act loop; state estimation and control under uncertainty; learned manipulation and locomotion policies and their safety limits
- **Ethics of AI**: humans and robots; robustness and transparency; data bias and fairness; accountability; privacy; human oversight; alignment as the gap between the objective written down and the outcome intended, so that a proxy metric gets gamed (reward hacking); misuse as a use the builder did not intend, handled with a threat model and red-team tests before release
- **Data-science problem typology**: descriptive, diagnostic, predictive and prescriptive questions; a guided solution framework from framing through data, model and evaluation to deployment

### Classical machine learning

- **Learning paradigms**: supervised, unsupervised and reinforcement learning; regression, classification and clustering, and when to use which
- **Learning theory**
  - Empirical risk minimization: choosing the hypothesis h in H that minimizes the empirical risk R̂(h) = (1 ÷ m) Σᵢ ℓ(h(xᵢ), yᵢ) over m training examples, justified only when the empirical risk tracks the true risk R(h), which is the job of uniform convergence (below)
  - The bias–variance decomposition of squared error (bias² + variance + irreducible noise); overfitting and underfitting; double descent, where test error rises near the interpolation threshold (parameters roughly equal to training points) and falls again as the model grows past it
  - Regularization as a constraint: ridge (L2, a circular constraint region, closed-form solution, shrunken weights) and lasso (L1, a diamond-shaped region whose corners give sparse weights, fitted by coordinate descent); polynomial features with ridge in one pipeline; polynomial regression as linear least squares on the design matrix with columns 1, x, …, xᵈ, where the degree d is the bias–variance knob and the Vandermonde columns become ill-conditioned at high degree, so centring x or an orthogonal polynomial basis is used
  - Maximum likelihood; minimizing cross-entropy as maximum likelihood for a classifier
  - Train, validation and test splits; cross-validation as an estimator with its own variance; the test set touched once
  - Splits that give an honest estimate: transforms fitted on the training split only; stratified splits for skewed classes; time-based splits for temporal data; group-aware splits keeping one user, patient or device on one side; near-duplicates removed across splits
  - Generalization from a test set: Hoeffding's bound P(|test error − true error| > ε) ≤ 2e^(−2mε²) for a fixed classifier; reusing a test set for model selection spends its guarantee
  - Uniform convergence: with probability at least 1 − δ, sup over h in H of |R̂(h) − R(h)| ≤ ε once m ≥ ln(2|H| ÷ δ) ÷ (2ε²) for a finite class, by a union bound over Hoeffding's inequality for each hypothesis; a class with this property makes ERM generalize, and an infinite class needs a complexity measure instead of |H|
  - Bias–complexity tradeoff: the excess risk of ERM splits into an approximation error (the best risk achievable inside H) and an estimation error (the cost of choosing from H with finite data); the no-free-lunch theorem says that without a restriction on H, every learner fails on some distribution, so a richer H lowers the first term and raises the second
  - Structural risk minimization: a nested sequence of classes H₁ ⊂ H₂ ⊂ … ordered by a complexity measure such as VC dimension, choosing the class and hypothesis that minimize empirical risk plus a complexity penalty; the penalty must shrink as the sample size grows, which is what makes the choice of class itself learnable
  - Algorithmic stability: an algorithm is β-uniformly stable when replacing one training example changes its loss at every point by at most β; for such an algorithm the expected gap between true and empirical risk is at most β, and regularized ERM with a λ-strongly convex objective is stable with β of order L² ÷ (λm) for an L-Lipschitz loss
  - Convex learning problems: learning a convex loss over a bounded convex hypothesis set (norm at most B) with L-Lipschitz losses; stochastic gradient descent with step size ηₜ = B ÷ (L√t) reaches excess risk of order BL ÷ √T after T steps, so the sample complexity for accuracy ε is of order B²L² ÷ ε²
  - Online learning and regret: at each round the learner picks wₜ, then a convex loss fₜ is revealed; regret is Σₜ fₜ(wₜ) − minᵥ Σₜ fₜ(v) against the best fixed predictor in hindsight; online gradient descent attains regret of order √T, and the Perceptron makes at most (R ÷ γ)² mistakes on a stream separable with margin γ inside a ball of radius R
- **Linear models**
  - Least squares: minimizing ‖Xθ − y‖²; normal equations XᵀXθ = Xᵀy; convexity and convergence of gradient descent
  - Logistic regression with cross-entropy loss; generalized linear models; a GLM as an exponential-family response with a link function g(μ) = xᵀβ (identity for Gaussian, logit for Bernoulli, log for Poisson), fitted by iteratively reweighted least squares; the variance function V(μ) tied to the family (constant for Gaussian, μ(1 − μ) for Bernoulli, μ for Poisson), the canonical link under which the sufficient statistic enters linearly, deviance as the GLM analogue of residual sum of squares, and overdispersion in count data with the negative binomial as the usual remedy; softmax (multinomial) regression as the K-class form of logistic regression, with class probabilities softmax(Wx) and the cross-entropy gradient Xᵀ(P − Y) ÷ m, the same form as the binary case
- **Other supervised models**: k-nearest neighbours; naive Bayes; linear and quadratic discriminant analysis; support vector machines and margins; class weights; LDA's shared-covariance Gaussian class densities giving a linear boundary and QDA's class-specific covariances giving a quadratic one; the SVM dual, in which the data enter only through inner products, so a kernel k(x, x′) can replace them (the kernel trick), with the margin fixed by the support vectors; k-nearest neighbours as the majority vote (classification) or mean (regression) over the k closest training points under a distance metric, with k as the bias–variance knob (small k fits noise, large k oversmooths) and the Cover–Hart result that the asymptotic 1-NN error is at most twice the Bayes error for two classes; exact search at O(nd) per query, k-d and ball trees for low dimension, and approximate nearest neighbours (locality-sensitive hashing, HNSW graphs) at scale, where distances concentrate in high dimension and the neighbourhood loses meaning; the nearest-centroid (Rocchio) classifier that assigns a document to the class whose mean TF-IDF vector has the highest cosine similarity; the relevance vector machine, a Bayesian kernel model with a separate Gaussian prior precision for each weight, fitted by maximizing the marginal likelihood so that most precisions diverge and their weights vanish, which leaves fewer relevance vectors than an SVM has support vectors and gives predictive variances; distance weighting gives each neighbour a vote proportional to 1 ÷ distance (or a kernel of it), so near points dominate and the choice of k matters less, at the cost of sensitivity to how distances are scaled; a linear SVM on sparse TF-IDF vectors works well because text has far more distinct terms than documents, so the training documents are usually linearly separable and the margin, rather than the dimension, controls how well the classifier generalizes
- **Trees and ensembles**: decision trees and impurity (Gini, entropy); CART; random forests; gradient boosting and boosted stumps; feature importance; monotonic constraints; bagging as bootstrap samples of the training rows, one tree per sample, averaged over trees so that variance falls as the trees decorrelate, with about 37% of rows left out of each bootstrap giving an out-of-bag error estimate; random forests as bagging plus a random subset of features at each split to decorrelate the trees; nonmetric data handled without a distance, with categorical splits that send each category subset to one branch and rule sets read off the tree paths
- **Unsupervised learning and anomaly detection**: k-means; DBSCAN; Gaussian mixtures fitted by expectation–maximization; association rules (support, confidence, lift); robust statistics (z-score, median absolute deviation); isolation forests; reconstruction-error anomaly scores; EM for a Gaussian mixture alternating an E-step (responsibilities γᵢₖ ∝ πₖ N(xᵢ | μₖ, Σₖ)) with an M-step (weighted means, covariances and mixing weights from those responsibilities); EM in general, with the Q-function as the expected complete-data log-likelihood under the current posterior over hidden variables, the Jensen bound that makes it a lower bound on the observed log-likelihood, so each iteration never decreases the likelihood and the iteration converges to a stationary point, often a local maximum, which is why starting values matter
- **Dimensionality reduction**: the curse of dimensionality; PCA to compress features, with components chosen by explained variance; t-SNE for visualization only; PCA as the top eigenvectors of the covariance matrix (equivalently the leading right singular vectors of the centred data matrix), each maximizing captured variance wᵀΣw, with the explained-variance ratio λₖ ÷ Σⱼ λⱼ; independent component analysis, which recovers statistically independent sources by maximizing non-Gaussianity (kurtosis or negentropy) after whitening, with FastICA's fixed-point iteration as the usual algorithm and the cocktail-party problem as the classic case
- **Evaluation**
  - The confusion matrix; accuracy and why it misleads on imbalanced data (base rates)
  - Precision, recall, F1, macro-F1 for multiclass problems; choosing precision or recall by the cost of false positives against false negatives
  - ROC curves and AUC as the probability a random positive outranks a random negative; PR curves and PR-AUC
  - Calibration and reliability bins
  - Ranking measures: precision@k, recall@k, MAP, MRR, nDCG@k = DCG@k ÷ IDCG@k; average precision as the mean of the precision at each rank holding a relevant item, MAP as its mean over queries, and MRR as the mean over queries of 1 ÷ (rank of the first relevant item)
  - Forecast errors: MAE, percentage errors and their instability near zero, interval coverage
  - Business guardrail metrics beside model metrics; a north-star metric that the guardrails protect, a metric tree breaking it into the drivers a model can move, and launch criteria written as thresholds on each, fixed before the test starts
- **Feature engineering**
  - Scaling and normalization; encoding categoricals; the hashing trick; target encoding with time-aware folds
  - Missing values; imbalanced datasets (SMOTE, class weighting, focal loss, negative sampling) and the accuracy paradox, where always predicting the majority class scores high and detects nothing
  - Text, time, window, session and graph features; learned embeddings for the same entities as the alternative to these hand-built features, with the data and monitoring cost each route carries
  - Schema validation and golden tests for feature pipelines; a schema pins each feature's name, type, unit and allowed range, checked on every batch so that a renamed or retyped column fails the run instead of flowing into training; a golden test runs the pipeline on a small fixed input and compares its outputs with a stored expected file, so any change to a transform shows up as a reviewable diff rather than a silent shift in the model's scores
- **Regression in depth**: model assessment (R², adjusted R², residual plots, heteroscedasticity, leverage and influence); multiple regression and multicollinearity (variance inflation factor); variable and subset selection (best-subset, forward and backward stepwise, AIC and BIC); hat values hᵢᵢ as leverage, Cook's distance and studentized residuals for influence, the Breusch–Pagan test for heteroscedasticity with heteroscedasticity-robust standard errors as the fix, and the F test for comparing nested models
- **Computational learning theory**: hypothesis space and inductive bias; the PAC model; sample complexity for finite hypothesis classes; VC dimension and the growth function
- **Feature selection and agreement**: filter, wrapper and embedded selection versus feature extraction; Cohen's kappa as agreement beyond chance; filter scores (correlation, mutual information, Fisher score) computed once per feature, wrapper search by recursive feature elimination that refits the model at each step, embedded selection by an L1 penalty, and the stability of the selected set across resamples as the check that it is not noise; kappa computed as (p_o − p_e) ÷ (1 − p_e) with p_o the observed agreement and p_e the agreement expected by chance
- **Splines and basis expansions**: fixed basis functions (piecewise polynomials, B-splines) that turn a nonlinear fit into a linear one; knots and degrees of freedom; smoothing splines minimizing Σ(yᵢ − f(xᵢ))² + λ∫f''(t)² dt, whose solution is a natural cubic spline with knots at the data; the reproducing-kernel view; generalized additive models g(E[y]) = β₀ + Σⱼ fⱼ(xⱼ) with each fⱼ a spline fitted by backfitting, which give one interpretable curve per feature and no interactions unless they are added explicitly
- **Kernel smoothing**: local weighted averages with a kernel that decays with distance; the Nadaraya–Watson estimator; bandwidth as the bias–variance knob; local linear regression correcting boundary bias; kernel density estimation
- **Robust regression and errors-in-variables**: Huber and bisquare losses that downweight outliers; least absolute deviations; the breakdown point; errors-in-variables, where noise in the predictor attenuates the least-squares slope by the reliability ratio σ²ₓ ÷ (σ²ₓ + σ²ᵤ)
- **Model comparison and Bayes factors**: marginal likelihood as the evidence for a model; the Bayes factor P(D | M₁) ÷ P(D | M₂); Occam's factor penalizing flexible models; BIC as a large-sample approximation to the log evidence; the posterior predictive distribution p(y* | x*, D) = ∫ p(y* | x*, θ) p(θ | D) dθ as the Bayesian prediction, which averages over parameter uncertainty and so gives wider predictive intervals than plugging in a point estimate θ̂
- **Spectral clustering**: a similarity graph (k-nearest-neighbour or Gaussian affinities); the graph Laplacian L = D − W; the first k eigenvectors as an embedding, followed by k-means; why it separates non-convex clusters; normalized cut Ncut(A, B) = cut(A, B) ÷ vol(A) + cut(A, B) ÷ vol(B), relaxed to the generalized eigenproblem (D − W)y = λDy whose second eigenvector gives the bipartition
- **Hierarchical clustering**: agglomerative merging under single, complete, average and Ward linkage; dendrograms and cutting at a height; O(n²) memory and the cost at large n; divisive clustering as the top-down reverse
- **Gaussian processes**: a distribution over functions fixed by a mean function and a kernel; posterior mean and variance from the training covariance matrix; the marginal likelihood for fitting kernel hyperparameters; O(n³) exact inference and sparse approximations
- **Undirected graphical models**: Markov random fields and factor graphs; pairwise potentials and the Gibbs form p(x) ∝ exp(−E(x)); conditional independence as graph separation; the Hammersley–Clifford theorem; pseudolikelihood as a tractable training surrogate
- **Partition function**: Z = Σₓ exp(−E(x)) as the normalizer, and why summing over exponentially many states is intractable; the log-partition gradient identity ∂ log Z ÷ ∂θ = −E[∂E ÷ ∂θ], which turns maximum likelihood into moment matching; annealed importance sampling
- **Ising model**: spins ±1 on a lattice with energy −Σ Jᵢⱼ sᵢsⱼ − Σ hᵢsᵢ; ferromagnetic versus frustrated couplings; the critical point kT ÷ J ≈ 2.269 of the two-dimensional square lattice; Gibbs sampling and mean-field approximations
- **Probabilistic programming**: models written as ordinary programs with random choices and conditioned data; inference engines (Markov chain Monte Carlo, Hamiltonian Monte Carlo, variational inference) run on any such program; posterior predictive checks; Stan, PyMC and Pyro as examples
- **Kalman filters**: the linear-Gaussian state-space model; predict and update steps with gain K = PHᵀ(HPHᵀ + R)⁻¹; the innovation; optimality among linear estimators under Gaussian noise; extended and unscented variants for nonlinear models; tracking as the canonical use
- **Variational inference**: approximate a posterior p(z | x) by a tractable family q(z) chosen to maximize the evidence lower bound ELBO = E_q[log p(x, z)] − E_q[log q(z)] = log p(x) − KL(q ‖ p), so maximizing the ELBO minimizes the KL divergence to the posterior; the mean-field factorization q(z) = Π q_j(z_j) with coordinate-ascent updates q_j ∝ exp(E_{−j}[log p(x, z)]); the reparameterization trick (z = μ + σε) for low-variance stochastic gradients of the ELBO in black-box variational inference; and the trade-off against MCMC, which is asymptotically exact but slow, since variational inference is biased (mean-field under the reverse KL tends to understate posterior variance) but scales to large data
- **Reproducing kernel Hilbert spaces**: a positive semidefinite kernel k defines a Hilbert space H_k of functions in which evaluation is a bounded linear functional, through the reproducing property f(x) = ⟨f, k(·, x)⟩; the Moore–Aronszajn theorem that every positive semidefinite kernel determines exactly one such space; the representer theorem that the minimizer of Σᵢ L(yᵢ, f(xᵢ)) + λ‖f‖²_H over H has the form f = Σᵢ αᵢ k(·, xᵢ), reducing the infinite-dimensional search to n coefficients and the n × n Gram matrix Kᵢⱼ = k(xᵢ, xⱼ); Mercer's theorem giving the eigenexpansion k(x, x′) = Σₘ λₘ φₘ(x) φₘ(x′) for a continuous kernel on a compact domain; and kernel ridge regression and the smoothing-spline solution as worked special cases

- **Document clustering**: documents as TF-IDF vectors compared by cosine similarity, cos(a, b) = a · b ÷ (‖a‖ ‖b‖); spherical k-means on length-normalized vectors, where for unit vectors the squared Euclidean distance is 2 − 2 cos, so k-means clusters by cosine; agglomerative clustering with average linkage on cosine distance 1 − cos; labelling each cluster by its highest-weight terms so a person can read it; and evaluation by purity and normalized mutual information against labelled topics where labels exist
- **Missing-data handling**: the three mechanisms, missing completely at random (MCAR, missingness unrelated to any variable), missing at random (MAR, missingness depending only on observed variables) and missing not at random (MNAR, depending on the missing value itself); complete-case deletion, which is unbiased only under MCAR; mean or median imputation, which shrinks variance and distorts correlations; model-based imputation by regression or k-nearest neighbours; multiple imputation, which draws several completed datasets from the predictive distribution and pools the estimates so that the extra uncertainty from imputation is kept; and missingness indicators, a way to let a model use the fact that a value was absent, which can help when missingness carries signal but does not remove MNAR bias from the other estimates

### Deep learning

- Biological and artificial neurons; the perceptron and its limit to linearly separable data (XOR); multilayer perceptrons; width versus depth
- Activation functions: step, sigmoid, tanh, ReLU, softmax; the forward pass; step gives 0 or 1 with zero gradient almost everywhere, so gradient descent cannot train through it; sigmoid σ(x) = 1 ÷ (1 + e⁻ˣ) maps to (0, 1) and saturates at both ends, where its gradient vanishes; tanh maps to (−1, 1) and is zero-centred; ReLU max(0, x) is cheap and has gradient 1 for positive inputs, but a unit stuck at negative inputs is dead; softmax turns K scores into K probabilities that sum to 1 and is used at the output for K classes; the forward pass computes each layer as a = f(Wx + b) and feeds a to the next layer
- Loss functions: mean squared error for regression, cross-entropy for classification
- Tensors and computational graphs; tensor contractions in einsum notation, where a repeated index is summed and the shapes must agree (X of shape b×n×d times W of shape d×k gives b×n×k); a tensor as a multilinear array whose rank is its number of indices, the mode-n unfolding that flattens one index into rows, broadcasting that aligns shapes from the trailing dimension, and the CP and Tucker decompositions as low-rank forms
- Backpropagation as reverse-mode automatic differentiation over the computation graph; its cost as a small multiple of the forward pass; gradient checking by finite differences
- Vanishing and exploding gradients; residual connections; batch and layer normalization; batch normalization standardizing each channel over the mini-batch, then a learned scale γ and shift β, with running statistics used at evaluation; local minima and saddle points, where saddle points rather than poor local minima are the usual obstacle in high dimension
- Optimizers: batch, mini-batch and stochastic gradient descent, momentum, RMSProp, Adam; learning-rate schedules; Nesterov momentum evaluating the gradient at the look-ahead point θ − βv, with v ← βv + η∇L(θ − βv); AdaGrad accumulating squared gradients per coordinate, so frequently updated parameters take shrinking steps
- **Learning-rate schedules**: large steps early and small steps late; four knobs (warmup length, peak rate, decay shape, floor); linear warmup from near zero; constant, linear decay, cosine decay, step decay and reduce-on-plateau, cosine with restarts, and warmup–stable–decay for runs of unknown length; lengthening warmup and lowering the peak before changing the shape
- **Training instability**: loss spikes, gradient explosion and divergence as one feedback loop (an oversized update raises the loss, which enlarges the next gradient); sharp minima, outlier batches and depth as causes; gradient-norm clipping that rescales to a cap and keeps the direction, with the pre-clip norm logged; float16 overflow near 65,504 turning into Inf and NaN, and bfloat16 as the remedy; alerts on the gradient norm; checkpoints every N steps
- **Batch size**: effective batch = micro-batch × gradient-accumulation steps × devices; filling device memory, then accumulating; small batches (noisy gradients, more updates, often better generalization) versus large ones (stable gradients, higher throughput, fewer updates); the linear scaling rule tying learning rate to effective batch; too few optimizer steps showing as underfitting; computational performance on hardware: data parallelism replicating the model on each device and all-reducing gradients every step, model and pipeline parallelism when the weights do not fit one device, and throughput bounded by memory bandwidth as often as by arithmetic; ring all-reduce, where each device sends and receives 2(N − 1) ÷ N of the gradient bytes per step, so per-device communication barely grows with the device count N; tensor (intra-layer) parallelism splitting each large matrix product across devices; and sharded data parallelism (ZeRO, fully sharded data parallelism) that stores one shard of the parameters, gradients and optimizer moments per device and gathers the weights a layer needs just before it runs
- **Reading loss curves**: both losses high and flat early (underfitting); training loss falling while validation loss turns up (overfitting); both flat at the starting value (nothing is updating); persistent jitter (batch too small or rate too high); both falling with a small stable gap (healthy), checkpointed at the validation minimum
- Initialization that preserves activation variance: Glorot (Xavier) initialization Var(w) = 2 ÷ (n_in + n_out); He initialization Var(w) = 2 ÷ n_in for ReLU units
- Regularization in deep networks: weight decay, dropout, data augmentation, early stopping with patience; ensembles of independently trained networks averaged for accuracy and uncertainty, with the variance of the averaged predictor falling as σ² ÷ M when the member errors are uncorrelated
- Architecture as inductive bias: convolutions sharing one kernel across positions, so parameter count is independent of image size; CNNs for images; convolution with padding p, stride s and dilation d giving output length ⌊(n + 2p − d(k − 1) − 1) ÷ s⌋ + 1; equivariance, so shifting the input shifts the feature map, with pooling adding local invariance; residual blocks F(x) + x with identity shortcuts; VGG's stacked 3 × 3 kernels, GoogLeNet's 1 × 1 bottleneck inception modules and DenseNet's concatenated feature reuse; the lineage from LeNet-5 (convolution, subsampling, fully connected layers on digits) to AlexNet (ReLU, dropout, data augmentation and GPU training winning ImageNet 2012), then VGG, Inception and ResNet; translation invariance from pooling versus translation equivariance from convolution
- Sequence models before attention: RNNs and LSTMs; encoder–decoder (sequence-to-sequence) models and their fixed-length context bottleneck; weak long-range dependencies; no parallelism across time steps; Bahdanau (additive) attention over encoder states; variable-length batches padded to a common length with a boolean mask, so pad positions add nothing to the loss or the pooled state, and packed sequences that skip them in the recurrence
- The perceptron learning rule and its convergence proof (the mistake bound R²/γ²); McCulloch–Pitts units; sigmoid neurons; universal approximation and the trade-off between thin deep and fat shallow networks; the depth result that a deep ReLU network can realize exponentially many linear pieces in its depth, which a shallow network with the same number of units cannot, shown by the sawtooth construction
- Backpropagation through time: unrolling, truncated BPTT, and the gates of LSTM and GRU cells as controlled additive memory
- Visualizing convolutional networks: maximally activating patches, saliency maps and guided backpropagation; attention over image regions and hierarchical attention; Integrated Gradients averaging gradients along a straight path from a baseline input to the actual input, scaled by the input difference, with the completeness check that the attributions sum to the output difference
- **Bidirectional and deep RNNs**: a backward pass whose states are concatenated with the forward pass, so each position sees past and future context (offline tagging, not streaming generation); stacked recurrent layers whose outputs feed the next layer; gradient flow across depth and time
- **GELU and Swish activations**: GELU(x) = x·Φ(x), each input weighted by its Gaussian cumulative probability, with the tanh approximation; Swish x·σ(βx), equal to SiLU at β = 1; smooth non-monotone dips below zero against ReLU's kink at zero; their use in transformer feedforward blocks
- **Noise injection and parameter tying**: Gaussian noise on inputs, weights or activations as a regularizer, with input noise matching an L2 (Tikhonov) penalty to second order for small variance; tied parameters shared across positions or layers, as in convolutions and tied input and output embeddings; the drop in parameter count and its effect on generalization
- **Lottery ticket hypothesis**: a randomly initialized dense network contains a sparse subnetwork that, retrained alone from its original initialization, matches the full network's accuracy; iterative magnitude pruning to find winning tickets; rewinding weights to an early iteration; the contested status of the claim at scale
- **Continual learning**: learning a stream of tasks without forgetting earlier ones; the stability–plasticity trade-off; replay of stored examples; regularization that penalizes moving important weights (elastic weight consolidation's Fisher-weighted penalty); architectural isolation; average accuracy and forgetting as the measures
- **Quantization arithmetic**: affine quantization q = round(x ÷ s) + z with scale s = (x_max − x_min) ÷ (2ᵇ − 1) and zero point z, dequantized as x̂ = s(q − z), so each element's rounding error is at most s ÷ 2; symmetric (z = 0) versus asymmetric schemes; per-tensor versus per-channel scales; int8 matrix products accumulated in int32 and rescaled once per output; calibration of ranges by min–max or by percentiles over sample activations; and quantization-aware training to recover the accuracy that post-training quantization loses
- **Restricted Boltzmann machines and deep belief networks**: energy E(v, h) = −aᵀv − bᵀh − vᵀWh over a bipartite visible–hidden graph with no within-layer edges, so the conditionals factorize, with p(hⱼ = 1 | v) = σ(bⱼ + Σᵢ wᵢⱼ vᵢ) and the symmetric form for v given h; contrastive divergence (CD-k) approximating the log-likelihood gradient E_data[vhᵀ] − E_model[vhᵀ] with k Gibbs steps started from the data; and a deep belief network stacking RBMs trained greedily layer by layer, each hidden layer serving as the visible layer of the next, then fine-tuned with backpropagation, an early route to training deep networks that ReLU units and large labelled datasets later made unnecessary for most tasks

- **Practical methodology**: fix the metric and a non-learning baseline before choosing an architecture; start with a small model that trains end to end, then read the loss curves to choose the next lever, with more capacity for underfitting and more data, regularization or early stopping for overfitting; tune with random search over log-scaled ranges rather than a full grid, since only a few hyperparameters usually matter; and change one thing per run, recording each run's configuration, seed and metric so a result can be reproduced and a change attributed
- **Overfitting a tiny batch as a training diagnostic**: take eight to thirty-two training examples, switch off augmentation, dropout and weight decay, and train until the loss approaches zero; a model that cannot drive the loss to zero on its own small batch has an implementation fault (a sign or reduction error in the loss, a detached gradient, a label-shape mismatch, a learning rate far too high or low) rather than a generalization problem, so the check separates code faults from data and capacity limits; the expected result is 100% training accuracy and a loss near zero within a few hundred steps for a model of adequate capacity; if the batch overfits and the full run does not generalize, the suspects move to the data split, leakage and regularization; cost is one forward and one backward pass per step over a handful of examples, seconds on a CPU

### Natural language processing foundations

- NLP as the interface between people and software; tasks (classification, named entities, translation, summarization, question answering)
- The classical pipeline: cleaning and Unicode normalization, tokenization, stop-word removal, stemming and lemmatization, feature extraction, a classifier; stop-word removal as a fixed-list lookup applied before counting, with the caveat that negations such as "not" and short function words carry sentiment and syntax, so the list must be tuned to the task

- Limits of classical NLP, and the progression from rules to statistical models, deep learning, LLMs and agents
- **Why text is hard**: ambiguity and variation; Zipf's and Heaps' laws as empirical regularities of text

- **Lexical semantics**: WordNet and synsets; word-sense disambiguation (Lesk, supervised, knowledge-based); detecting novel senses; hypernym traversal up the is-a hierarchy to a common ancestor, and path similarity 1 ÷ (1 + shortest path length) between two synsets
- **Topic models**: LSA and pLSA; the LDA generative story, Dirichlet priors and collapsed Gibbs sampling; variants; latent semantic indexing as the rank-k truncated SVD of the term–document matrix, X ≈ U_k Σ_k V_kᵀ, the best rank-k approximation in the Frobenius norm by the Eckart–Young theorem, with a query folded in as Σ_k⁻¹ U_kᵀ q and documents compared by cosine in the k-dimensional space

- **Knowledge graphs**: triples and schemas; completion by embeddings (translation models such as TransE, rotation models such as RotatE, multiplicative models such as DistMult and ComplEx); evaluation by mean reciprocal rank and Hits@k
- **Summarization, classification and sentiment**: extractive (LexRank, centroid, optimization-based) versus abstractive summarization and ROUGE; text classification; sentiment with affective lexicons and aspect-based sentiment
- **N-gram language models**: the chain rule P(w₁ … w_n) = Π P(wᵢ | w₁ … wᵢ₋₁) with the Markov assumption cutting the history to the last n − 1 words, so the bigram estimate P(wᵢ | wᵢ₋₁) = count(wᵢ₋₁ wᵢ) ÷ count(wᵢ₋₁) is the maximum-likelihood estimate; zero counts for unseen sequences and smoothing by add-one (Laplace), absolute discounting and Kneser–Ney continuation counts; perplexity 2^(−(1 ÷ N) Σᵢ log₂ P(wᵢ | history)) over N test tokens as the evaluation measure, lower being better

- **Machine translation**: statistical machine translation with the noisy-channel decomposition ê = argmax P(e) P(f | e), a language model P(e) and a translation model P(f | e); IBM Model 1 word alignment fitted by expectation–maximization over alignments; phrase-based decoding that assembles translated phrases under the model score; BLEU as the geometric mean of clipped n-gram precisions multiplied by a brevity penalty exp(1 − r ÷ c) when the candidate of length c is shorter than the reference length r; and neural encoder–decoder translation with attention and subword vocabularies replacing this pipeline

- **Dictionaries and tolerant retrieval**: a dictionary as a hash table for exact term lookup in O(1) expected time, or as a sorted structure (B-tree, sorted array or trie) when prefix ranges are needed; wildcard queries with one * answered by a permuterm index holding every rotation of each term with a $ sentinel, so the query becomes a prefix range lookup on a rotation, or by a k-gram index that intersects the grams of the query fragments and then filters candidates by exact match; spelling candidates pruned by k-gram overlap before edit distance is computed; and the space cost of the permuterm index, one entry per rotation, about term length + 1 entries per term

### Classical computer vision and geometry

- **Image processing**: sampling and quantization; histograms and equalization; spatial filtering; image transforms (Fourier, DCT); 2-D convolution as a weighted sum over the kernel window, (f ∗ g)[x, y] = Σₛ Σₜ f[s, t] g[x − s, y − t], with the Gaussian and box kernels as smoothing examples and separable filtering, where a kernel that factors into a row and a column pass costs O(k) per pixel instead of O(k²); Gaussian and Laplacian pyramids, built by blurring and downsampling by two at each level, with the Laplacian pyramid storing the differences between levels so the original image is reconstructed exactly
- **Colour**: colour spaces (RGB, HSV, Lab); perception, white balance and colour constancy; colour image processing; gamma encoding as the nonlinear transfer between stored values and linear light (sRGB is close to a power of 2.2), so that filtering and averaging are done on linear intensities; the image formation model, in which a Lambertian surface's irradiance is I = ρ(n · l) and the sensor response is linear before gamma; and the conversions from RGB to HSV and to CIE Lab, with Lab's Euclidean distance ΔE as a perceptual colour difference
- **2-D projective geometry**: homogeneous coordinates; points and lines at infinity; the hierarchy of similarity, affine and projective transformations; homography estimation by the direct linear transform; the homography H with eight degrees of freedom, fixed by four point pairs and solved by SVD as the null vector of the stacked constraints x′ × (Hx) = 0, with the coordinates normalized first (centred and scaled) so that the solve is well conditioned
- **Camera geometry**: the pinhole model; intrinsics K and extrinsics [R | t]; the projection matrix P = K[R | t]; lens distortion; calibration (Zhang's method); the radial distortion model x_d = x(1 + k₁r² + k₂r⁴) on normalized image coordinates, and calibration from several views of a planar target, with one homography per view giving two linear constraints on the intrinsics K
- **Stereo and multi-view geometry**: epipolar constraint; fundamental and essential matrices and the eight-point algorithm; rectification, disparity and triangulation; structure from motion and bundle adjustment; the fundamental matrix F with x′ᵀFx = 0 and, for calibrated cameras, the essential matrix E = [t]ₓR, whose two equal nonzero singular values are a check on the estimate, decomposed into four (R, t) candidates of which the one placing the points in front of both cameras is kept; triangulation as the linear solve for a 3-D point from its two projections, with disparity d = x − x′ and depth Z = fB ÷ d for a rectified pair; bundle adjustment as nonlinear least squares over camera poses and points minimizing the summed squared reprojection error; multi-view stereo that densifies depth from many calibrated views; and visual SLAM as incremental localization and mapping, refined by pose-graph optimization
- **Features**: corners (Harris); scale space and blob detection (LoG, DoG); SIFT-style descriptors; matching with the ratio test; the Harris response R = det M − k(trace M)² on the structure tensor M of summed gradient outer products, with corners at local maxima above a threshold; the difference-of-Gaussians detector approximating the scale-normalized Laplacian for scale-space extrema; SIFT's 128-element descriptor of gradient-orientation histograms over a 4 × 4 grid of cells with eight bins each, normalized and clipped; and HOG as cell histograms of gradient orientation with block normalization, used for detection
- **Model fitting**: least squares; RANSAC; the Hough transform; RANSAC's loop of sampling a minimal set, fitting, counting inliers within a threshold and keeping the largest consensus set, with the iteration count N = ln(1 − p) ÷ ln(1 − wˢ) for success probability p, inlier fraction w and sample size s; and Hough line voting in (ρ, θ) with ρ = x cos θ + y sin θ, where peaks in the accumulator are detected lines
- **Range images**: depth sensing (structured light, time of flight, LiDAR, stereo); point clouds; normals, ICP registration and range-image processing; shape from shading, texture and silhouettes, where silhouettes from several views carve out the visual hull as the intersection of their cones; and surface reconstruction from oriented point clouds by Poisson reconstruction, which solves for a scalar indicator function whose gradient matches the normals and extracts its level set
- **Recognition**: clustering and classification for vision (bag of visual words); dimensionality reduction and sparse representation (sparse coding, dictionary learning)
- **Deep vision**: object detection (R-CNN family, YOLO); semantic and instance segmentation (FCN, U-Net, Mask R-CNN); vision applications
- **Edge detection and thresholding segmentation**: Sobel and Prewitt gradients; Canny's pipeline of Gaussian smoothing, gradient magnitude and orientation, non-maximum suppression, and hysteresis with a high and a low threshold; global thresholding by Otsu's method, which picks the level that maximizes the between-class variance ω₀ω₁(μ₀ − μ₁)²; adaptive thresholds for uneven illumination; and region growing and watershed as the region-based alternatives
- **Mean shift segmentation**: each pixel or feature vector climbs the gradient of a kernel density estimate until it reaches a mode, with the kernel bandwidth setting the scale; points that converge to the same mode form one segment, so no cluster count is set in advance; the cost of repeating the kernel-weighted mean update for every point, which is why it is slow on large images without subsampling or approximate neighbour search

- **Image alignment and stitching**: feature matches between overlapping views, a homography for each pair fitted by RANSAC over the matches (the minimal sample is four point pairs), the pairs chained into one set of transforms relative to a reference view, each image warped onto a common plane or cylinder by inverse mapping with bilinear interpolation, and the overlap blended across a seam so exposure differences do not show; and the direct alternative that minimizes the intensity difference over a parametric motion model

### Applied problem families

- **Problem framing**: prediction, ranking, retrieval, generation and optimization problems; the target action, labels, input entities, metric and failure modes; cold start; feedback loops; delayed labels; a go or no-go test before any model (ML is warranted only when the rule is hard to write, the data and labels exist, and the cost of a model's errors is below the cost of a rule's), and a non-ML baseline (a heuristic, a popularity rule or the current process) that every model must beat on the agreed metric before training starts
- **The case-study method**: problem and business measure → data and labels (implicit, explicit, delayed, weak) → leakage against what is known at decision time → derived metric or estimator → offline evaluation → serving (batch or online) and latency budget → monitoring, rollback and retraining → cost → a named company's design read against the same steps
- **Leakage and point-in-time correctness**: as-of joins; leakage, training–serving skew and online–offline feature drift as three distinct failures; one feature definition for both paths with a parity test
- **Recommendation and feeds**
  - Candidate generation (two-tower retrieval, approximate nearest neighbours, judged by recall@k) separated from ranking and re-ranking
  - Collaborative filtering (item–item), matrix factorization; cold start for a new user or item, where no interactions exist to fit factors, answered by content features, popularity priors and a slice of exploratory traffic
  - Diversity, coverage and multiple objectives; popularity feedback loops; catalogue coverage; maximal marginal relevance re-ranking, which picks each next item to maximize λ·relevance − (1 − λ)·(maximum similarity to the items already chosen); exposure fairness, comparing each group's share of position-weighted exposure in the ranked list with its share of relevance
  - Labels from impressions, plays, dwell and hides; leakage from future popularity and same-session targets
- **Search, learning to rank and ads**
  - Inverted indexes, BM25, semantic and hybrid retrieval with rank fusion; an inverted index maps each term to the list of documents containing it, so a query reads only its own terms' lists; BM25 scores a document as Σ IDF(t) · f(t, d)(k₁ + 1) ÷ (f(t, d) + k₁(1 − b + b·|d| ÷ avgdl)), where k₁ saturates repeated terms and b scales length normalization; semantic retrieval ranks by the cosine between query and document embeddings, finding paraphrases that share no words but missing exact codes; hybrid retrieval merges the two ranked lists by reciprocal rank fusion, Σ 1 ÷ (k + rank) with k = 60, which uses only ranks, so the two score scales never need calibrating against each other
  - Learning to rank: pointwise, pairwise and listwise; sampled pairs; pointwise regresses or classifies each query–document pair's label alone; pairwise trains a scorer on pairs in which one document is preferred, with a logistic loss on the score difference, as in RankNet; listwise optimizes a loss over the whole ranked list, such as a softmax over the list or a surrogate weighted by each pair's effect on nDCG, as in LambdaRank; with n candidates per query there are about n²/2 pairs, so pairs are sampled within each query under a fixed budget
  - Position bias: the position-based click model; inverse-propensity scoring (Horvitz–Thompson) unbiased under positivity; propensity clipping trading bias for variance; propensities from randomized slices; off-policy evaluation of a new ranker from old logs
  - Ads ranked by bid × predicted quality; calibrated click and conversion rates; an impression's expected revenue is bid × predicted click-through rate, so the score is only as good as the rate's calibration: an over-predicted rate overbids and overspends, and an under-predicted rate loses auctions it should win; calibration is checked by comparing predicted and observed click counts in bins across the traffic, and re-checked on each slice of advertisers and placements
  - Wide & deep models: memorized feature crosses plus generalizing embeddings; the wide part is a linear model over sparse crossed features, such as a user–app pair seen together in training, so it memorizes specific co-occurrences; the deep part is a network over dense embeddings of categorical features, so it generalizes to combinations never seen; the two logits are summed and trained jointly, and the costs are manual choice of crosses on the wide side and over-generalization of rare combinations on the deep side
- **Explore and exploit**
  - The K-armed bandit; regret R_T = Tμ* − E[Σ rₜ]; K arms each pay a reward drawn from an unknown distribution with mean μₖ, and each round the learner pulls one arm and observes only that arm's reward, so it must balance exploiting the arm that looks best against sampling the others to learn about them; μ* is the best mean, and regret is the expected shortfall against always pulling that arm over T rounds
  - Greedy lock-in; ε-greedy's linear regret; UCB1 (x̄ᵢ + √(2 ln t ÷ nᵢ)) and Thompson sampling with logarithmic regret; the Lai–Robbins lower bound; the minimax lower bound, under which every algorithm suffers regret of order √(KT) on some K-armed instance over T rounds, matched up to logarithmic factors by UCB-style and Exp3-style methods; explore-then-commit, which pulls each arm m times and then commits to the arm with the highest sample mean for the remaining rounds, where a short exploration phase leaves a constant chance of committing to a suboptimal arm and so linear regret, while an explore length of order (ln T) ÷ Δ² for a gap Δ gives logarithmic regret
  - Contextual and cascade bandits; offline replay evaluation; policy constraints; replay evaluation keeping a logged event only when the logged action equals the new policy's action, which gives an unbiased estimate of the new policy's average reward when the logging policy chose uniformly at random; the case studies of news recommendation and ad allocation read with the same replay evaluator; the linear contextual bandit (LinUCB), which estimates θ̂ by ridge regression on the chosen contexts and plays the arm with the largest xᵀθ̂ + α√(xᵀA⁻¹x), where A is the ridge-regularized design matrix
  - Adversarial bandits: rewards chosen by an adversary with no fixed distribution; regret against the best fixed arm in hindsight; exponential-weight algorithms (Exp3) with importance-weighted reward estimates; regret of order √(KT log K); the update wᵢ ← wᵢ exp(η x̂ᵢ) with x̂ᵢ = xᵢ 1[Iₜ = i] ÷ pᵢ, where pᵢ mixes the normalized weights with uniform exploration, so each reward estimate is the observed reward divided by the probability of playing that arm, which keeps the estimate unbiased
  - Pure exploration: identifying the best arm at a fixed confidence or within a fixed sampling budget; successive elimination and sample-complexity bounds; how it differs from regret minimization, where every pull counts
  - A/B test versus bandit versus feature flag; an A/B test fixes the traffic split for a set horizon (say 50/50) and gives an unbiased comparison with a confidence interval, whereas a bandit moves traffic toward the arm that looks better during the test, earning more reward while making the final estimate harder to read; a feature flag is a switch for rollout or kill and is not a learning method, so the choice depends on whether the goal is a clean answer, a higher reward during learning or a safe release
  - Off-policy evaluation: the importance-sampling estimate V̂ = (1 ÷ n) Σᵢ wᵢ rᵢ with wᵢ = π(aᵢ | xᵢ) ÷ μ(aᵢ | xᵢ) for logging policy μ and new policy π, unbiased when μ gives positive probability wherever π acts; the variance grows with the weight ratio, so self-normalized and clipped weights trade bias for variance; and the doubly robust estimator, which adds a reward model r̂ and corrects its error with the weighted residual, so it is unbiased if either the model or the logged propensities are right
- **Forecasting, ETA, demand and availability**
  - Trend, seasonality and holiday decomposition; moving averages, exponential smoothing, autoregressive intuition; backtesting
  - Covariates as known at forecast time; censored observations; a covariate is usable only if its value is known when the forecast is made, so a price planned in advance qualifies while same-day sales do not; a censored observation is one where true demand exceeded the recorded value, as during a stock-out, so the record is a lower bound, and treating it as the true value biases the forecast downward; a censored likelihood uses the bound directly
  - The pinball (quantile) loss L_τ and why its minimizer is the τ-quantile; prediction intervals judged by coverage and width together; quantile forecasting by fitting one model per τ (for example 0.1, 0.5 and 0.9) to get the interval directly, and repairing quantile crossing by sorting the predicted quantiles
  - Availability probabilities, calibration and the cost of a false "in stock"; the model outputs P(item available at time t), and a false "in stock" leads to a failed order or cancellation, which costs far more than the missed sale that a false "out of stock" causes, so the decision threshold comes from the ratio of those two costs; the expected-cost rule is valid only when the probabilities are calibrated
  - Dynamic regression: regression with ARIMA errors, yₜ = βᵀxₜ + ηₜ with ηₜ a stationary ARIMA process fitted jointly with β, so the covariate effect and the autocorrelation are estimated together; differencing to stationarity by d, and the seasonal ARIMA form (p, d, q)(P, D, Q)ₘ
  - Judgmental forecasting: expert adjustments to a statistical baseline, each written down with its reason and scored against the baseline on the same holdout, since an adjustment that does not beat the baseline on accuracy is noise
  - Hierarchical forecast reconciliation: forecasts for a hierarchy (total, region, store, product) must add up; bottom-up summing, top-down disaggregation by historical shares, and minimum-trace reconciliation ỹ = S(SᵀW⁻¹S)⁻¹SᵀW⁻¹ŷ, where S is the summing matrix and W the covariance of the base forecast errors, which gives the minimum-variance unbiased linear reconciliation
- **Fraud, abuse, risk and trust and safety**
  - Cost-weighted thresholds: flag when p > C_FP ÷ (C_FP + C_FN); expected cost C_FP·FP + C_FN·FN as the measure
  - Review-queue capacity setting the threshold; recall at a fixed false-positive rate
  - Weak, delayed (chargeback) and semi-supervised labels; a weak label comes from a rule or proxy with a known error rate and is combined with others rather than trusted alone; a delayed label arrives only after its window closes, so a recent transaction with no chargeback yet is unlabelled rather than legitimate and must be treated as censored, not negative; semi-supervised learning uses a small labelled set with many unlabelled examples through self-training or label propagation, and its gain depends on the unlabelled data matching the labelled distribution
  - Fraud rings as connected components of shared-device and shared-card graphs; accounts, devices and cards are nodes with an edge wherever one is used with another, and union-find over the edges gives the connected components as candidate rings; component size, edge density and the fraud rate of known members become features, and a shared public device can link unrelated customers, so edges are weighted by how rare the shared item is and very large components are capped and reviewed
  - Synchronous scoring beside an asynchronous review queue; fail-open versus fail-closed decisions
  - Adversarial drift; adversarial test sets built by red-team generation of hard inputs (perturbed, paraphrased or boundary cases), scored apart from the random holdout, with the pass rate reported per attack family
- **Language and support**
  - Ticket routing and classification; macro-F1 and misroute cost; handle time and deflection
  - Sequence labelling scored by entity-level F1
  - Grammatical error correction as structured prediction; the output is a set of edits over the input (insert, delete or replace a span), predicted per token by a tagger or generated as a whole corrected sentence by an encoder–decoder; the metric is span-level precision, recall and F0.5, which weights precision above recall because a wrong correction harms the text more than a missed one, and edits are scored against annotated references rather than one corrected sentence
  - Suggested replies sent by a human; masking personal data before a model sees text
- **Vision, documents and speech in production**: buying a perception API versus building a model; batch versus GPU-endpoint serving; near-duplicate leakage across splits; mean average precision; image search with batch embedding and an online nearest-neighbour index
- **Marketing, churn, lifetime value and notifications**
  - Potential outcomes; randomization versus confounding; average and segment (uplift) effects; targeting the persuadable; uplift metrics, the Qini curve, which plots the cumulative incremental gain (treated response minus control response) as users are targeted in decreasing predicted-uplift order, against random targeting, and the area between the two (the Qini coefficient)
  - Holdouts and incremental lift instead of raw click rate; a holdout withholds the feature or message from a random slice of users, and incremental lift is the treated group's outcome minus the holdout's, reported with its standard error, so a click that would have happened anyway is not credited to the campaign; raw click rate counts every click, including those the holdout would also have produced
  - Survival analysis and churn hazard; censoring; the survival function S(t) = P(T > t) gives the share of users still active at time t, and the hazard h(t) is the chance of churning at t given the user is still active; the Kaplan–Meier estimator multiplies the conditional survival at each event time, and users still active when observation stops are censored, staying in the risk set until then and not counted as churned; a Cox proportional-hazards model adds covariates to the hazard
  - Customer lifetime value: CLV = m r ÷ (1 + d − r) for constant retention r, margin m and discount rate d
  - Lookalike audiences; send time under frequency caps; notification fatigue; a lookalike audience trains a model on known converters, scores a wider population and takes the top-scoring users up to the target audience size; send time picks, for each user, the hour with the highest predicted open probability, subject to a cap on messages per day; notification fatigue is the rise in opt-outs and uninstalls as frequency grows, so the objective carries a cost for each extra message as well as the value of each open
- **Causal inference**: causal graphs and structural causal models (structural equations, interventions, counterfactuals); front-door adjustment for unobserved confounders; instrumental variables, difference in differences, regression discontinuity and synthetic control; propensity weighting; overlap (positivity) and its failure; sensitivity analysis for unmeasured confounding; observational bias; the E-value, the minimum strength of association that an unmeasured confounder must have with both treatment and outcome to explain away an observed risk ratio, and overlap diagnostics such as propensity histograms as the check for positivity before any weighting; instrumental variables, with the conditions of relevance (the instrument moves the treatment), exclusion (it affects the outcome only through the treatment) and independence (it shares no cause with the outcome), the Wald ratio Cov(Z, Y) ÷ Cov(Z, X) for a binary instrument, and two-stage least squares as the general estimator; weak instruments, where the first-stage relevance is small, make the two-stage estimate biased toward the confounded OLS value and its intervals too narrow, so the first-stage F statistic is checked first, with a common rule of thumb of F above 10 for a usable instrument
- **Marketplace pricing** as an applied family; a two-sided market where one price moves both buyer demand and seller supply, so a demand model estimates purchase probability as a function of price and the objective is margin or revenue under supply constraints; a price experiment has interference between the two sides, so a switchback design alternates prices over time blocks rather than splitting buyers at random, and the variance of the estimate is computed over the blocks, not over individual buyers
- **Time-varying treatments and g-methods**: treatment–confounder feedback, where past treatment changes later confounders that in turn steer later treatment, which conventional regression adjustment cannot handle; inverse-probability weighting with stabilized weights and marginal structural models; the parametric g-formula, standardizing the outcome over the observed covariate history; g-estimation of structural nested models, which removes the feedback through an estimating equation; and the failure modes of positivity violations and extreme weights
- **Effect modification and selection and measurement bias**: effect modification as a difference in the effect across strata of a covariate, kept distinct from confounding; selection bias from conditioning on a collider or on study participation; measurement bias from error in exposure or outcome measurement, with the direction of each bias worked out from a causal graph before any adjustment
- **Causal survival analysis**: time-to-event causal estimands, such as the survival curve under a treatment strategy, with censoring handled by inverse-probability-of-censoring weights, and the risk difference or hazard ratio read from the standardized survival curve
- **Target trial emulation**: specify the hypothetical randomized trial (eligibility, treatment strategies, assignment, start of follow-up, outcome and causal contrast) and map the observational data onto it, aligning time zero with eligibility to avoid immortal-time bias, then estimate with the weighting or g-methods above
- **Causal mediation analysis**: natural direct and indirect effects that split a total effect into the part through a mediator and the part not through it, identified under sequential ignorability, including no unmeasured mediator–outcome confounding; the product-of-coefficients form for linear models; and sensitivity analysis for violations of that assumption

- **Offline model evaluation**: a holdout split the way production splits (by time for forecasts and ranking, by entity for users and devices) so that no future or same-entity information leaks in; metrics chosen from the decision and its costs, computed per slice with the worst slice reported; uncertainty on each metric by a bootstrap over examples, or a paired test on the same examples when comparing two models; an offline-to-online check that compares offline gains with the online result of past launches; and counterfactual replay for a ranker or policy, where a logged decision is kept only when it matches the new policy's action

### MLOps

- The ML lifecycle: data → features → training → evaluation → deployment → monitoring → retraining
- **Technical debt in ML systems**: entanglement; hidden feedback loops; undeclared consumers; data dependencies; glue code and pipeline jungles; the model as a small part of the system
- **Data and feature platforms**: event contracts; batch versus streaming features; freshness and backfills; point-in-time correctness; schema evolution; feature stores with online and offline parity; fresh features streamed from user-action and inventory events; per-entity features cached with a TTL; data contracts between producers and consumers as a versioned schema with field types, nullability, units and allowed ranges, an owner and a freshness target, validated by the producer at publish time and by consumers on read, where removing or retyping a field is a breaking change that needs a version bump and a deprecation window; a feature store's offline store, columnar tables read by point-in-time joins to build training sets, and its online store, a key–value store keyed by entity ID with a TTL on each value, filled by a materialization job from the offline store or from a stream, with a parity test that one feature definition gives the same value on both paths
- **Training pipelines**: dataset snapshots and versions; reproducible training (pinned data, code, environment, seeds); experiment tracking; hyperparameter search; model registries with lineage, aliases and approval status; model cards; code, data and model as three independent change axes, so a model record pins all three and a change to any one yields a new version; hyperparameter search by random sampling over log-scaled ranges, Bayesian optimization with a Gaussian-process surrogate and expected improvement, and successive halving that stops weak trials early; a rollback pointer as an alias moved back to the previous approved version, so serving reads one name and a rollback changes no artefact; and approval as a recorded state transition, with the approver and the evaluation run that justified it
- **Production readiness**: the ML Test Score rubric across data, model, infrastructure and monitoring; the Google Cloud Professional Machine Learning Engineer exam guide as a named benchmark for this part's design, build, operationalize and govern groups, with each of its sections mapped to a group above
- **CI/CD/CT**: continuous training; continuous evaluation triggered from CI; the triggers for a retraining run (a schedule, a new data volume, or a drift or metric threshold); the pipeline run (validate the data, train, evaluate the candidate against the current champion on the same holdout, register it with lineage, and deploy only if the rollback check passes); and the difference between CI (code and pipeline tests on every commit), CD (promotion of a validated artefact) and CT (retraining when the data changes, with tests on the data and the model as well as the code)
- **Serving architectures**: online, batch, near-real-time, embedded, sidecar and asynchronous inference; caches, fallbacks and timeout budgets; batch scores delivered by schedulers and task queues; a prediction service as a model server behind a load balancer, with health checks, autoscaling on concurrency and a p99 latency budget split across feature fetch, model call and post-processing; edge deployment of compressed models on phones and microcontrollers, with post-training int8 quantization and size and latency measured on the target device; resilient serving as a set of measured guards: a timeout on every call to a dependency, retries with capped exponential backoff and jitter only for idempotent requests, a circuit breaker that opens after an error-rate threshold and serves the fallback (a heuristic or the last good scores) until probes succeed, load shedding when queue depth passes a limit, and drills that exercise the fallback path before an incident does
- **Experimentation and rollout**: A/B tests as two-sample hypothesis tests; holdouts; assignment bucketing and exposure logs; bootstrap intervals; segment analysis; metric guardrails; shadow, canary and ramp deployments of models as a dial separate from the application canary; rollback gates; a rollback gate as a pre-registered rule that compares the candidate's guardrail metrics with control and reverts automatically when a one-sided test rejects, evaluated by a command-line evaluator that exits nonzero on failure so the pipeline stops
- **Monitoring and drift**
  - Covariate shift (P(x) changes), prior or label shift (P(y) changes) and concept drift (P(y | x) changes)
  - Two-sample detection: the Kolmogorov–Smirnov statistic D = supₓ |F₁(x) − F₂(x)|; the population stability index PSI = Σ (aᵢ − eᵢ) ln(aᵢ ÷ eᵢ) and its conventional 0.1 and 0.25 thresholds
  - Training–serving skew as a pipeline defect that retraining does not fix; the training features and the serving features are computed by different code paths, so the model meets an input at serving that it never saw in training, and retraining on the same offline pipeline reproduces the mismatch; detection logs the serving-time feature values and compares them with the offline values for the same entity and timestamp, and the fix is one shared feature definition with the code corrected, not the model retrained
  - Calibration drift; data-quality and freshness checks; a written retraining policy; incident runbooks; a retraining policy that names its trigger (a schedule, a drift threshold or a metric drop), the validation gate a candidate must pass and the rollback path; model-evaluation dashboards with per-slice metrics over time, confidence bands and alert thresholds that link to the runbook; a shortlist-quality monitor, the bad-shortlist rate, being the share of sampled routing requests whose shortlist holds no acceptable candidate, scored on logged requests against a labelled acceptability judgement and alerted against a threshold set before launch; freshness as a metric is the age of the newest row or feature value at serving time, measured as now minus the event time of the latest record a feature reads, alerted when the age passes a threshold set from the feature's business need, and reported as an SLO, the share of time the age stays under the bound
- **Governance and responsible ML**: privacy and PII minimization; consent, retention and deletion; bias and fairness; explanations; human-in-the-loop review and human override; audit logs; non-destructive, undoable AI actions; abuse resistance and red-team tests; data-loss prevention on logs; group fairness assessment comparing selection rate, true-positive rate and false-positive rate across groups (demographic parity, equalized odds), reported per slice before launch and after each retrain; Fairness Lens, the Vertex AI tool for running this per-slice comparison on a deployed model, with its console path and command grammar learned from the product's current documentation; SHAP attributions as Shapley values φᵢ of each feature, which add with the base value E[f(X)] to the prediction f(x) (local accuracy), so one prediction's feature contributions are read directly; audit logs as append-only records of each model-driven action with its input, model version, output and actor; non-destructive, undoable AI actions, where the model drafts or stages a change that a person applies, or applies it with an undo window, instead of an irreversible write; consent and retention recorded per dataset, with the consent basis for each use, a retention window that ends in deletion, and deletion propagated to the snapshots, features and training sets derived from the data; and model cards and risk documentation, with each release's intended and out-of-scope uses, training and evaluation data, per-slice metrics and known limits, plus a risk register naming each harm with its likelihood, severity, mitigation and owner
- **Scale and cost**: hot keys and fan-out; approximate retrieval; request batching; concurrency limits; accelerators as remote services; cost per prediction and per successful decision; cost at ten times the traffic; latency percentiles (p50, p95, p99) measured under load, and throughput as requests per second at a stated latency objective, because batching raises throughput and adds queueing delay
- **ML platforms**: shared features, training, registry, serving and monitoring across teams; platform-enforced point-in-time joins; measured by time to train, failed deploys, skew incidents and cost per training hour; shared datasets as versioned, owned assets with a schema, access control and recorded lineage, so a team reuses a published snapshot instead of copying it
- **TensorFlow Extended (TFX)**: a production pipeline of components (ExampleGen, StatisticsGen, SchemaGen, ExampleValidator, Transform, Trainer, Evaluator, Pusher); the metadata store recording artifact lineage; orchestration by Apache Beam, Apache Airflow or Kubeflow Pipelines
- **TensorFlow Data Validation (TFDV)**: per-feature statistics; schema inference from training data; validation of new batches against that schema to flag missing values, unseen categories and out-of-range values; training–serving skew checks
- **Kubeflow Pipelines**: pipelines as directed acyclic graphs of containerized components, each run as a pod on Kubernetes with declared inputs and outputs; parameters, step caching and run history; Vertex AI Pipelines executing Kubeflow pipeline definitions
- **MLflow**: runs with parameters, metrics and artifacts; a tracking server backed by a database and an artifact store; autologging; the model registry with versions, aliases and lineage back to the run
- **Weights & Biases experiment monitoring**: runs and live metric dashboards; sweeps for hyperparameter search; versioned artifacts with lineage; reports and team access; hosted versus self-hosted trade-offs against MLflow
- **Evidently drift monitoring**: reference and current windows compared column by column with drift tests (Kolmogorov–Smirnov for numeric features, chi-square for categorical) and PSI; target and prediction drift reports; threshold alerts wired to a runbook
- **Custom containers for serving**: a model image carrying its runtime and serving code, exposing a health route and a predict route on the configured port; `gcloud ai models upload` with `--container-image-uri`, `--container-health-route` and `--container-predict-route`; `gcloud ai endpoints deploy-model` to put it behind an endpoint
- **Multi-model endpoints**: several models deployed to one endpoint with a traffic split across the deployed models; `gcloud ai endpoints deploy-model` with `--traffic-split` and `gcloud ai endpoints undeploy-model`; compute shared against isolation from noisy neighbours; rollback of one model by shifting its share of the split
- **The human side of ML**: team roles across data scientists, engineers and product owners; ownership of a production model with on-call and runbooks; setting stakeholder expectations for error costs and uncertainty; user-facing design for model mistakes (fallbacks, feedback capture); labelling workforce quality and annotator agreement; responsible-AI review as a team process; a labelling workflow with written guidelines, a gold set for audits, double-labelled samples that measure agreement by Cohen's kappa, and adjudication of disagreements
- **Data versioning tools**: DVC, which keeps a small pointer file per dataset in Git (with a content hash) while the data sits in remote storage, using `dvc add`, `dvc push` and `dvc pull`, with multi-step pipelines declared in a stage file and re-run by `dvc repro` only where an input hash has changed; lakeFS, which gives an object store Git-like branches, commits and merges, so a training run pins a commit, an experiment branches the data without copying it, and a pre-merge check runs data tests before a change lands; the lineage each records from a commit to a model run, and the trade-off between copying snapshots and recording pointers
- **Training data sampling and labelling strategy**: stratified and class-balanced sampling for skewed labels; active learning by uncertainty sampling (least-confidence and margin scores) and query-by-committee; a labelling budget set by expected gain per label; weak supervision by labelling functions whose votes are combined into probabilistic labels; and an error estimate for the labels from a gold set before a model is trained on them
- **Continual learning in production**: the retraining trigger and a replay buffer of past examples kept in each training mix; forgetting measured on a fixed evaluation suite of older slices before promotion, so that gains on new data cannot hide regressions on old traffic; shadow evaluation of each candidate against the current champion; and the stability–plasticity trade-off set as an explicit policy choice rather than left to the retraining schedule
- **ML design patterns catalogue**: recurring solutions to recurring ML-system problems, each stated with its problem, the structure of the solution and its trade-offs: data-representation patterns (hashed feature, embeddings, feature cross, transform), problem-framing patterns (reframing as ranking or classification, cascade, rebalancing, ensemble), model-training patterns (useful overfitting, checkpoints, distribution strategy, hyperparameter tuning), resilient-serving patterns (stateless serving function, batch serving, continued model evaluation, two-phase predictions), reproducibility patterns (repeatable sampling), and responsible-AI patterns (heuristic benchmark, explainable predictions), read as one system and not as a checklist

- **Dataset engineering**: building a training set as a reproducible transform of its sources: deduplication by exact content hash, then near-duplicate detection by MinHash signatures, where the fraction of matching signature positions estimates the Jaccard similarity of two shingle sets; cleaning rules applied in a fixed order, with the count removed at each step logged; label audits on a random sample against a gold set; and a datasheet per dataset giving its motivation, composition, collection process, intended uses, known gaps and maintenance owner

- **Troubleshooting ML pipelines and deployments**: a fixed order of checks from the outside in: confirm the symptom against a metric and a slice; then the serving path (latency split across feature fetch, model call and post-processing, errors and timeouts from each dependency); then the inputs (schema, freshness and null rates against the expected distribution); then the deployed artefact (its registry version and pinned data and code); then the training run; a diff of the last good run against the failing one across data, code, environment and configuration, bisected across dataset versions or commits when many change at once; and a written record of the trigger, the fix and the guard added so the same failure is caught earlier next time
