### Speech science and analysis

- **Speech production and human hearing**: the source–filter model of the vocal tract (glottal source, oral and nasal cavities, lips); voiced and unvoiced excitation; acoustic-phonetic characterization of vowels, fricatives, stops and nasals by formant frequencies, spectral shape and timing; the ear as a front end (cochlear filter bank, loudness, the Bark and mel scales); auditory masking and critical-band spreading, the basis of perceptual coders, and the threshold of hearing in quiet as the reference for loudness)
- **Phonetics and phonology**: articulatory description by place, manner and voicing; the International Phonetic Alphabet; phonemes as contrastive units versus allophones; syllables and stress; phonetic transcription of words, and the spelling-to-sound gap that transcription makes visible
- Syllable structure and word formation: a syllable has a nucleus, the sonority peak (usually a vowel), with an optional onset before it and coda after it; sonority rises from onset to nucleus and falls after it, and the maximal-onset principle assigns each intervocalic consonant cluster to the following onset where the language allows it; word formation is morphology: prefixes and suffixes attach to a stem by rules that either change its category (derivation) or mark its grammatical features (inflection), and compounding joins two stems; stress is assigned to a syllable within the word, and a shift of stress can change the category (noun record, verb record)
- **Pole-zero speech models and linear prediction**: nasal and fricative sounds as pole-zero systems and vowels as all-pole; linear predictive coding (LPC) as an all-pole vocal-tract model fitted per speech frame by minimizing prediction error, by the autocorrelation or covariance method; formant frequencies read from the poles; the residual as the excitation estimate; stability of fitted poles
- **Homomorphic speech analysis**: the source–filter product becomes a sum after a logarithm, so the real cepstrum of a voiced frame puts the vocal-tract envelope at low quefrency and the periodic excitation at high quefrency; liftering separates them; cepstral smoothing gives the spectral envelope used by recognizers and synthesizers
- **Filter-bank analysis and synthesis**: a bank of band-pass channels spaced on a perceptual scale, measuring short-term energy per channel; channel-vocoder analysis and resynthesis by overlap-add; the trade-off between channel count, time resolution and reconstruction error; filter-bank energies as the input to MFCC and PLP features
- **Sinusoidal analysis and synthesis**: speech as a sum of slowly varying sinusoids; peak picking in each short-time spectrum; partial tracking across frames with birth, death and continuation; phase interpolation and additive resynthesis; the sinusoidal model as the basis for voice transformation
- **Frequency-domain pitch estimation**: fundamental frequency (F0) as the spacing of harmonics; harmonic-sum and harmonic-product spectra; the cepstral pitch peak; the octave error; voicing decisions and smoothing of the pitch track across frames
- **Nonlinear measurement and modelling of speech**: where the linear source–filter model fails (turbulence, creaky and breathy voice, rapid pitch change); the Teager energy operator and amplitude–frequency (AM–FM) decomposition of a harmonic; nonlinear prediction and dynamical measures for voice quality
- Teager energy operator and AM–FM separation: the discrete Teager operator is Ψ[x(n)] = x(n)² − x(n−1)x(n+1), which for a sinusoid A cos(ωn + φ) gives A² sin²ω, so it tracks amplitude and frequency together; the energy-separation algorithm applies it to x and to the difference y(n) = x(n) − x(n−1), giving the instantaneous frequency ω(n) = arccos(1 − Ψ[y(n)] ÷ (2Ψ[x(n)])) and the instantaneous amplitude a(n) = √(Ψ[x(n)] ÷ sin²ω(n)); the separation is exact for a single sinusoid and approximate for a slowly varying AM–FM harmonic, and because the operator amplifies noise it is applied to band-passed speech
- **Speech coding: PCM, µ-law and CELP**: uniform pulse-code modulation and its bit rate (sampling rate times bits per sample, so 8 kHz by 8 bits gives 64 kbit/s); µ-law companding for log-like quantization of telephone speech; transform and sub-band coders; code-excited linear prediction (CELP) with analysis-by-synthesis codebook search and perceptual weighting; low-bit-rate vocoders and the rate–quality trade-off
- Transform and sub-band coders: the signal is split into bands (sub-band coding) or into transform coefficients (for example by the MDCT), and each coefficient is quantized with a bit allocation that follows the perceptual need of its band; bands below the masking threshold get no bits, and the rest share the bit budget, with each band's quantization noise rising by about 6 dB for every bit removed; the bit rate is the bits allocated per frame times the frame rate
- Low-bit-rate LPC vocoders: per frame of about 20 to 30 ms the coder sends the pitch, a voiced or unvoiced flag, the gain and the LPC coefficients, and the decoder excites the all-pole filter with a pulse train (voiced) or noise (unvoiced); the rate falls to a few kbit/s (LPC-10 runs at 2.4 kbit/s) because only parameters are sent, and the cost is the buzzy, synthetic quality of the excitation
- **Perceptual linear prediction (PLP) features**: the Bark-scale critical-band spectrum, equal-loudness weighting, the cube-root intensity–loudness law, then an all-pole fit; compared with MFCC features on noisy speech; choosing the feature front end for a recognizer
- **Speech enhancement and environmental robustness**: additive noise and reverberation as the degradations; spectral subtraction and Wiener-style gains from a noise estimate tracked in silent frames; feature-level robustness (cepstral mean normalization, parallel model combination, vector Taylor series); measuring gains by segmental SNR and intelligibility; acoustic echo cancellation by an adaptive FIR filter that models the loudspeaker-to-microphone echo path, updated by normalized LMS from the far-end signal and frozen during double talk; microphone arrays and beamforming, with delay-and-sum weights w = a(θ) ÷ M for a uniform linear array steered to θ, and the minimum-variance distortionless response w = R⁻¹a ÷ (aᴴR⁻¹a) that keeps unit gain toward the target while minimizing output power; multi-condition training on noise-added speech, and word error rate plotted against SNR to show where recognition breaks
- Cepstral mean normalization: for each utterance, subtract the mean of each cepstral coefficient over the frames, ĉₜ[k] = cₜ[k] − (1/T)·Σₛ cₛ[k]; a fixed channel's linear filter adds a constant to the log spectrum and so a constant to every cepstral frame, which the subtraction removes; a running mean over a sliding window gives the online form
- Parallel model combination: the clean-speech Gaussians are combined with a noise model in the log-spectral domain, so the noisy mean is μ_y = log(exp(μ_x) + exp(μ_n)) per dimension, with the variances combined by an approximation such as a Monte Carlo or unscented transform; the noise model is estimated from silent frames and updated as the noise changes
- Vector Taylor series: the nonlinear mapping from clean to noisy log spectrum, y = x + log(1 + exp(n − x)), is linearized by a first-order Taylor expansion around the current clean-speech mean; the mean and covariance of the noisy Gaussians follow from the linearized mapping, with the noise mean and covariance estimated from silence; it is cheaper than PMC per Gaussian, but its accuracy falls when the noise is far from the expansion point

### Speech recognition: from HMMs to Kaldi

- **Pattern comparison by dynamic time warping**: aligning a test sequence to a stored template on a non-linear time axis; the local cost matrix and the accumulated-cost recurrence; step patterns and slope constraints; endpoint constraints; the isolated-word template recognizer and its limits (no statistics, one template per word)
- **Connected-word and continuous speech recognition**: word sequences recognized by joining word models under a grammar; the level-building algorithm and one-pass beam search over word transitions; word-boundary decisions; N-best hypotheses as the step to large-vocabulary decoding; N-best extraction from a word lattice by a backward search that uses forward scores as the exact heuristic, rescoring of the list by a stronger language model, and the oracle word error rate of the list as the bound on what rescoring can recover
- **Acoustic modelling and speaker adaptation**: phone units as left-to-right hidden Markov models with Gaussian-mixture emissions; forward, Viterbi and Baum–Welch training over frames; context-dependent triphone units; speaker adaptation by maximum a posteriori (MAP) re-estimation and by maximum likelihood linear regression (MLLR) with regression classes; confidence scores; MLLR as an affine transform of the Gaussian means, μ̂ = Aμ + b, with the transform estimated by maximizing the EM auxiliary function over the adaptation data, and a regression-class tree that shares one transform among many Gaussians when adaptation data are scarce
- Semi-continuous HMM: every state shares one codebook of M Gaussians, and each state keeps only its own mixture weights over that codebook, so the emission is b_j(o) = Σₘ c_jm N(o; μₘ, Σₘ); this sits between the discrete HMM, which quantizes each frame to one codebook index, and the fully continuous HMM with per-state mixtures, and it needs fewer parameters per state, which suits small training sets
- Confidence scores: a word's confidence is its posterior probability in the word lattice, the sum of path probabilities through the word divided by the sum over all paths, computed by forward–backward over the lattice; or a classifier over features such as the acoustic score, word duration, the language-model score and agreement among N-best hypotheses; a rejection threshold, tuned on held-out data, accepts a word above it and rejects it below, trading false accepts against false rejects
- **DNN–HMM hybrid systems**: a deep network replaces the Gaussian emissions and outputs posteriors over context-dependent HMM states; scaled likelihoods by dividing posteriors by state priors; training from a GMM alignment; decoding on the same HMM graph; training speed-ups by pipelining, low-rank layers and distillation; the network itself as a stack of affine layers and nonlinearities over a spliced window of acoustic frames, with a softmax over context-dependent states trained by cross-entropy and backpropagation; pretraining by stacked restricted Boltzmann machines (deep belief networks), less necessary once good initialization and ReLU units arrived; ADMM splitting the training objective across workers with consensus updates on the shared parameters; asynchronous SGD with gradient staleness bounded by the worker count
- Training and decoding speed-ups: pipelined backpropagation gives each device a block of layers and feeds micro-batches through in sequence so the stages work at once, at the cost of gradients computed on slightly stale weights; asynchronous SGD lets workers push updates to a parameter server without a barrier, with staleness bounded by the worker count; low-rank factorization replaces an m × n weight matrix by a product of rank r, cutting the multiplies per frame from mn to r(m + n); sparse networks prune small weights and retrain, and sparse matrix products skip the zeros; distillation trains a small student on the large teacher's posteriors, so decoding cost is the student's forward pass
- **CTC and sequence-discriminative training**: connectionist temporal classification with a blank symbol and many-to-one alignments; the forward–backward recursion over the label lattice that sums every alignment of a transcript; greedy and beam decoding; sequence criteria (maximum mutual information, minimum Bayes risk) that train on whole utterances
- Sequence-discriminative training of DNN–HMM: maximum mutual information maximizes the log posterior of the correct transcript, log p(O | W_ref) P(W_ref) − log Σ_W p(O | W) P(W), with the denominator summed over competing hypotheses in a lattice generated by a decoding pass; minimum Bayes risk maximizes expected accuracy, Σ_W P(W | O)·A(W, W_ref), where A counts matched words, and the state-level form (sMBR) scores matched HMM states; the gradient with respect to each frame's output is the difference between the numerator and denominator state posteriors, obtained by forward–backward over the two lattices; training starts from a cross-entropy-trained network
- **Conformer and self-supervised speech representations**: the Conformer block (half-step feed-forward, multi-head self-attention, convolution block) for ASR; self-supervised pretraining on unlabelled audio by masked prediction of quantized latent units with a contrastive objective (wav2vec 2.0 style); CTC fine-tuning on a small labelled set; word error rate after fine-tuning
- **Speech feature learning and transfer**: learned front ends replacing hand-built features; bottleneck and stacked features; multitask and multilingual training; transfer of hidden layers across languages and tasks; audio-visual features; measured by word error rate on target data with little labelled speech
- Bottleneck features: a narrow hidden layer (for example 40 units) inside the trained network; its activations are taken as features for a GMM–HMM, usually after splicing neighbouring frames into a window (stacked features) and decorrelating them with a linear transform such as LDA and MLLT
- Shared hidden layers in multitask and multilingual training: a shared trunk of hidden layers feeds one output head per task or language, and the training loss is the weighted sum L = Σₜ λₜ Lₜ, so every task's gradient updates the shared weights while each head keeps its own; transfer across languages or tasks pretrains the trunk on a source set, then replaces the output layer and fine-tunes; audio-visual systems fuse an audio encoder and a video encoder by concatenating or attending over their features
- **Pronunciation lexicons**: word-to-phone-sequence dictionaries with several pronunciations and optional silence; grapheme-to-phoneme fallback for out-of-vocabulary words; building a lexicon from a seed dictionary; how lexicon errors surface as word errors
- **State clustering and phonetic decision trees**: too many context-dependent triphone states to estimate; tying states by clustering their statistics; a phonetic decision tree that splits a phone's contexts by yes-or-no questions about neighbouring phone classes, choosing the split with the largest likelihood gain; the stopping threshold; predicting unseen triphones from the tree
- **Symbol tables for words and phones**: integer IDs for words, phones, HMM states and transition IDs in a weighted finite-state transducer; input and output symbol sides; mapping IDs back to text in decoder output; the table as the contract between graph-building stages
- **HCLG decoding graph**: the search space as the composition H ∘ C ∘ L ∘ G, where H maps HMM states to transition IDs, C maps context-dependent phones to context-independent phones, L maps phones to words, and G is the language-model acceptor; determinization and minimization keep the graph small; Viterbi shortest path through the composed graph; beam pruning that drops hypotheses whose cost exceeds the best cost plus a beam width, trading word error rate against decoding speed; stack decoding, an A*-style best-first search over partial word sequences with a heuristic estimate of the remaining cost, as the alternative to Viterbi over the full graph; epsilon removal, which follows each epsilon-labelled path and folds its summed weight into one direct arc, so the graph has no epsilon arcs left to traverse
- Building H, C, L and G: H is built from the HMM topology, one state per HMM state with a transition-ID label on each arc, so its input side is an HMM state and its output a transition ID; C is built from the context window, one state per (n − 1)-phone context with an arc for each phone that extends it, mapping each context-dependent phone to the context-independent phone at the centre; L loops over the lexicon with a path per pronunciation, outputting the word on its first phone and carrying the pronunciation probability as weight; G is an n-gram acceptor with one state per history, an arc per word weighted by −log P(w | history), and backoff arcs as epsilon transitions to the lower-order state
- Weighted determinization and minimization: determinization builds an automaton in which each input string has at most one path, each output state standing for a set of (state, residual weight) pairs, so its size can grow exponentially in the worst case; weight pushing moves weight toward the initial state first, so that the pairs can be merged; minimization then partitions the states into equivalence classes by refinement (Hopcroft-style, about O(n log n)) and merges each class into one state, which leaves the weighted language unchanged
- **Disambiguation symbols and self-loop addition**: reserved symbols (#0, #1, #2) that make the lexicon deterministic where one pronunciation is a prefix of another; their removal after composition; self-loops added to the context transducer so the composition is defined; what breaks when they are omitted
- **Kaldi recipes, scripting and deployment**: the recipe layout (data preparation, feature extraction, monophone and triphone training, LDA with MLLT, speaker-adaptive training, chain and neural models); staged scripts with checkpoints; lattices and rescoring; packaging a decoding graph for serving, with the latency and memory budget it implies; streaming decoding with first-word and end-of-utterance latency, and word error rate sliced by noise condition, accent and language to show where the recognizer fails
- **Recognizer pipeline end to end**: framing and feature extraction (MFCC or PLP), the acoustic model (GMM–HMM, DNN–HMM or CTC), the pronunciation lexicon, the language model, the decoder over the HCLG graph, and word error rate as WER = (S + D + I) ÷ N, where S, D and I count substitutions, deletions and insertions in a Levenshtein alignment of hypothesis to reference and N is the reference word count; character error rate computed the same way over characters, for languages without word boundaries and for finer scoring of near-misses
- **System fusion (ROVER)**: combining hypotheses from several recognizers into one output (Recognizer Output Voting Error Reduction): each system's word sequence aligned to a backbone by iterative dynamic-programming alignment, forming a confusion network whose slots hold the competing words with a NULL option for deletions; the output takes the highest-voted word in each slot, with votes weighted by each system's confidence or word-level accuracy; fusion helps when the systems make uncorrelated errors and hurts when one system dominates; scored by the word error rate of the fused output against each input system alone
- DNN–GMM system fusion at the score level: the state scores are a weighted sum of the two models' log scores, λ·log p_GMM(o | s) + (1 − λ)·(log p_DNN(s | o) − log p(s)), with λ tuned on held-out data; or the lattices of the two systems are merged, word posteriors averaged across systems, and the best word in each slot taken from the averaged posteriors; fusion helps when the systems make uncorrelated errors, and the weight shows how far each model's errors are trusted
- **Attention-based encoder–decoder speech recognition**: an encoder (stacked bidirectional recurrent or Conformer layers, with frame-rate reduction by stacking adjacent frames) producing hidden states hᵤ over the acoustic frames; an attention decoder that emits one character or subword per step from the previous outputs and a context vector cₜ = Σᵤ αₜᵤ hᵤ, with weights αₜᵤ from a softmax over content-based scores; training by cross-entropy with teacher forcing; joint CTC–attention training with loss λ·L_CTC + (1 − λ)·L_att, the CTC term enforcing monotonic alignment; beam search with length normalization or a coverage penalty; repeated or skipped content on long utterances as the failure mode of attention alignment; Whisper-style models as large encoder–decoder instances, scored by word error rate
- **Lexical-tree search and multipass decoding**: the pronunciation lexicon compiled into a prefix tree in which words sharing initial phones share HMM states, so the number of active states per frame falls; word identity known only at the leaves, so the language-model score is applied at word ends with lookahead, using the best unigram probability within each subtree; multipass decoding in which a fast first pass with a bigram graph produces lattices or N-best lists and a second pass rescores them with a trigram or neural model; the trade-off between pass speed, lattice memory and word error rate
- **OpenFst weighted finite-state transducer library**: a weighted automaton as states and arcs labelled input:output with a weight in a semiring (tropical for minimum-cost paths, log for summed probabilities); the text description of arc and final-state lines compiled to a binary machine; the operations composition (output labels of one transducer matched to input labels of the next, with weights combined by the semiring product), determinization, minimization, epsilon removal, arc sorting, union, concatenation, closure and shortest path; the command-line tools fstcompile, fstprint, fstcompose, fstdeterminize, fstminimize, fstrmepsilon, fstarcsort and fstshortestpath; composition simulated by hand as a product of states with matched labels and summed weights, then checked against the library on the same graph
- **Streaming recognition latency**: a streaming recognizer emits partial hypotheses while audio is still arriving, so latency is measured at three points: first-token latency from the start of speech to the first emitted word, end-of-utterance latency from the end of speech to the final stable transcript, and the real-time factor RTF = processing time ÷ audio duration, where RTF below 1 means the decoder keeps pace with live audio; the controls on these numbers are the chunk or frame size that sets the floor on first-token latency, the look-ahead (right context) of the encoder, which buys accuracy at the cost of added latency, and endpointing by a silence or classifier threshold that decides the utterance has ended, where a short threshold cuts speakers off and a long one adds latency; partial-result stability, since a word that changes after display is a correction the user sees; measured by replaying a recorded stream at real time against a clock, with first-token and end-of-utterance percentiles and word error rate reported together, because lowering one usually raises another

### Speech synthesis, speakers and spoken-language systems

- **Text-to-speech synthesis**: the TTS pipeline from text front end to waveform; formant synthesis, concatenative unit selection and PSOLA pitch-synchronous overlap-add; statistical parametric synthesis from acoustic features to a vocoder; neural acoustic models with neural vocoders; evaluation by mean opinion score and intelligibility
- **Text normalization and letter-to-sound**: expanding numbers, dates, currency, abbreviations and symbols into words before pronunciation; context-dependent expansion and homograph disambiguation; letter-to-sound rules and their failure on names; pronouncing-dictionary lookup with a learned grapheme-to-phoneme model as fallback
- **Prosody**: symbolic prosody (prosodic phrases, accents, break labels); duration modelling from phone identity and context; F0 contour generation with declination and phrase-final lengthening; prosody as a carrier of questions and focus, and why flat prosody sounds robotic
- **Speaker recognition and i-vectors**: speaker verification as a hypothesis test with a score threshold, measured by equal error rate and detection cost; Gaussian-mixture universal background models with MAP adaptation; the i-vector as a low-dimensional total-variability factor of the GMM supervector; probabilistic linear discriminant analysis (PLDA) scoring with length normalization; closed-set speaker identification as the variant
- **Spoken language understanding and dialogue**: from recognized words to meaning, with intent classification and slot filling on noisy ASR output; frame-based dialogue with slot–value frames; dialogue state tracking, dialogue policy and response generation; error handling by confirmation and repair; end-to-end evaluation by task success rate; dialogue acts as labels of each utterance's communicative function (question, statement, confirmation, backchannel), predicted from the words and the previous act, and the conversation structure as sequences of acts linked by adjacency pairs
- Spoken dialogue system architecture: the recognizer produces one-best words or a lattice, the language-understanding module turns them into intent and slots with confidence, the dialogue manager updates the dialogue state and picks the next action from its policy, the generation module renders that action as words, and the synthesizer speaks them; each module passes its output and confidence downstream, so an error in one module shows up as a wrong action in the next, and the loop runs once per turn, with barge-in handled by recognition running during synthesis
- Voice user-interface design: prompts short and one question at a time, with options stated by example; barge-in lets the caller interrupt a prompt, so recognition runs during playback with echo cancellation; confirmation is explicit when the ASR confidence is low and implicit when it is high; after no-input or no-match the system re-prompts with narrower options, and after a fixed number of failures it hands over to a person

### Computer vision: images, motion and multi-view geometry

- **Image restoration and deconvolution**: the degradation model (blur kernel plus noise) as an inverse problem; noise models (additive Gaussian, Poisson photon noise, salt-and-pepper impulse noise) and their estimation; inverse and Wiener filtering; constrained least-squares restoration; blind deconvolution and iterative Richardson–Lucy restoration; why inversion amplifies noise and is ill posed; constrained least-squares restoration with the filter F̂ = [H* ÷ (|H|² + γ|P|²)]·G, where P is a smoothness operator (the Laplacian) and γ is set so that ‖g − Hf‖² = ‖n‖² holds, which bounds the amplification that the inverse filter gives where |H| is small
- Reconstruction from projections: a projection of f at angle θ is its line integral along lines at that angle, R(ρ, θ) = ∫∫ f(x, y) δ(x cos θ + y sin θ − ρ) dx dy (the Radon transform); the Fourier slice theorem says the one-dimensional Fourier transform of each projection is a slice through the two-dimensional transform of f at angle θ; plain back-projection smears each projection across the image and blurs the result by a factor of 1 ÷ r, so filtered back-projection first filters each projection with the ramp |ω| and then back-projects it, f(x, y) = ∫₀^π (p_θ ∗ h)(x cos θ + y sin θ) dθ, with h the inverse transform of |ω|
- **Wavelets and multiresolution for images**: separable two-dimensional wavelet decomposition into approximation and detail subbands at successive scales; the multiresolution pyramid and perfect reconstruction; thresholding coefficients for denoising; why wavelets localize edges better than the Fourier basis
- **Image compression and watermarking**: transform coding with the discrete cosine transform on 8 by 8 blocks, quantization tables and entropy coding (the JPEG pipeline); rate–distortion measured by PSNR and structural similarity; wavelet compression; watermarks as embedded signals (spread-spectrum, least-significant bits, frequency-domain embedding) judged by imperceptibility and robustness to compression and cropping
- Content provenance: a signed manifest that records an asset's origin and each edit applied to it, such as C2PA manifests, which bind the asset's hash to the signer's certificate and to an ordered list of actions; a verifier checks the signature and recomputes the hash, so an altered asset fails, while a missing manifest proves nothing either way; a watermark stays in the pixels, whereas provenance is metadata that a re-encode can strip, so the two are used together
- **Motion estimation and optical flow**: block matching and translational alignment; the brightness-constancy constraint and the aperture problem; Lucas–Kanade least squares over a window with a coarse-to-fine image pyramid; Horn–Schunck smoothness regularization; parametric (affine) and layered motion models; endpoint error as the evaluation measure
- **Computational photography: HDR and super-resolution**: bracketed exposures merged into radiance by weighting unsaturated pixels, then tone mapping for display; recovering the camera response curve; multi-frame super-resolution from sub-pixel shifts under a degradation model; learned single-image super-resolution; limits (ghosting from motion, hallucinated detail)
- **Image-based rendering and neural rendering**: view interpolation from images with known geometry; plenoptic sampling and light fields; a scene as a function from position and direction to colour and density, rendered by volume integration along rays (neural radiance fields); sampling and training costs; novel-view quality against geometric accuracy
- **Light, shading and reflectance**: image formation as illumination times surface reflectance; Lambertian shading and the cosine law; specular terms and the bidirectional reflectance distribution function (BRDF); photometric stereo recovering surface normals from images under known lights; shape from shading and its ambiguity
- **Texture as filter-bank representations**: texture as statistics of filter responses at several scales and orientations (Gabor and oriented derivative filters); energy and local-mean features pooled over a region; texture classification and segmentation by these features; texture synthesis by matching filter statistics
- **Tracking by detection and Kalman filters**: tracking as association of detections across frames; the Kalman filter's predict and update equations on a constant-velocity state; gating by the innovation covariance; assignment of tracks to detections by the Hungarian method; identity switches and occlusion as the failure modes
- **Smooth surfaces and aspect graphs**: differential geometry of smooth surfaces (curvature, parabolic and occluding contours); the apparent contour seen from a viewpoint; aspect graphs grouping viewpoints by the topological structure of the visible outline; limits of aspect graphs for recognition over large view sets; surface reconstruction from oriented points by solving Poisson's equation ∇²χ = ∇·V and extracting the isosurface by marching cubes; shape from texture and shape from focus as further shape-from-X cues; the curvature of a plane curve κ = (x′y″ − y′x″) ÷ (x′² + y′²)^(3/2) for a parametrized curve, with the osculating circle of radius 1 ÷ κ, the one-dimensional case that surface curvatures generalize
- **Single-view geometry: vanishing points and cross-ratio**: vanishing points of parallel lines and the vanishing line of a plane; measuring lengths and angles on a plane from one image; the projective invariance of the cross-ratio on a line; the scale ambiguity that a single view cannot remove
- **Affine epipolar geometry**: the affine camera (parallel projection) as a special case of perspective; the affine fundamental matrix and its parallel epipolar lines; how the affine case simplifies correspondence search and reconstruction; when the affine approximation holds (small depth range, long focal length)
- **Trifocal tensor and three-view geometry**: point and line constraints across three views; the trifocal tensor of three cameras, with 27 entries and 18 degrees of freedom; point–line–line incidence and point transfer between views; computing the tensor from correspondences
- Linear computation of the trifocal tensor: each point–line–line correspondence gives linear equations in the 27 tensor entries; stacking the equations for enough correspondences gives a design matrix whose null vector, found by SVD, is the tensor up to scale; the image coordinates are normalized (Hartley's isotropic normalization) before the solve, and the tensor is then refined by minimizing the reprojection error, since the linear solve ignores the internal constraints that a valid tensor satisfies
- **N-view tensors and multiple-view methods**: multilinear constraints among N views (bilinear, trilinear and quadrilinear forms); N-view tensor computation; bundle adjustment as joint nonlinear least squares over cameras and points; structure from motion as incremental two-view initialization, camera registration by perspective-n-point, triangulation and bundle adjustment; SLAM as the same joint estimation run online, with a motion model predicting the pose, loop-closure detection, and a pose graph optimized after each closure; the factorization method of Tomasi and Kanade for affine cameras, which stacks the centred image measurements into a 2F × P matrix and reads the motion and structure from its rank-3 singular value decomposition up to an affine ambiguity
- Degrees of freedom of N-view constraints: N cameras have 11N parameters less the 15 of the projective gauge, so 11N − 15 degrees of freedom: 7 for two views (the fundamental matrix, with the bilinear constraint x′ᵀFx = 0), 18 for three (the trifocal tensor, with trilinear constraints) and 29 for four (the quadrifocal tensor, with 81 entries and quadrilinear constraints)
- **Autocalibration (self-calibration)**: recovering the intrinsic matrix from several views without a calibration object; the absolute conic and the dual absolute conic; the Kruppa equations; constraints from zero skew, square pixels and fixed intrinsics; the minimal number of views needed
- **Duality of points and lines**: projective duality in the plane (points and lines exchange under incidence) and in space (points and planes); any statement about points and lines has a dual; using duality to swap roles in constructions and in line-based estimators
- **Cheirality**: the sign of reconstructed depth; a projective reconstruction that is correct up to a projective ambiguity yet places points behind a camera; the cheirality test that selects one of the four camera decompositions of the essential matrix; triangulation with positive-depth checks
- **Degenerate configurations**: point and camera arrangements where estimation is underdetermined: collinear or coplanar points for homography and fundamental-matrix estimation; coincident camera centres in trifocal computation; critical configurations for autocalibration; detecting degeneracy by rank deficiency of the design matrix
- **Image sampling, aliasing and quantization**: sampling on a grid with spacing Δ and the Nyquist condition (sampling rate above twice the highest spatial frequency); spatial aliasing as moiré when a fine texture is sampled coarsely, and anti-alias blurring before decimation; uniform quantization to L = 2^k intensity levels, with the error uniform on [−Δ ÷ 2, Δ ÷ 2] and variance Δ² ÷ 12 for a quantization step Δ
- Visual perception: the retina has about 75 to 150 million rods (low light, no colour) and about 6 to 7 million cones (colour, bright light); brightness adaptation lets the eye work over a range of about 10^10 in light intensity, but at any one adaptation level it resolves only about two dozen grey levels; Weber's law says the just-noticeable change ΔI is a fixed fraction of the background intensity I, ΔI ÷ I ≈ 0.02, so perceived brightness is roughly logarithmic in intensity; Mach bands, the overshoot seen beside a sharp edge, show that perceived brightness is not simply the intensity
- **Point transforms, gamma and histograms**: pixel-wise mappings s = T(r), including the negative, log and power-law mappings s = c·rᵞ, gamma correction for display and contrast stretching; the histogram h(r_k) = n_k ÷ n; histogram equalization by the transform s_k = (L − 1) Σⱼ≤ₖ h(rⱼ), which flattens the cumulative distribution of intensities
- **Frequency-domain filtering**: the convolution theorem, so filtering is a pointwise product H(u, v)·F(u, v) after zero-padding to avoid wrap-around; ideal, Butterworth and Gaussian low-pass and high-pass filters; the ringing of the ideal filter's sinc-shaped impulse response; sharpening as the high-pass complement of smoothing
- **Classical segmentation**: global thresholding by Otsu's method, which picks the threshold t that maximizes the between-class variance ω₀ω₁(μ₀ − μ₁)²; region growing from seeds; split-and-merge over quadtrees; watershed on the gradient magnitude; graph cuts on a pixel graph minimizing a data term plus a smoothness term
- **Projective camera and homographies**: 3-D projective transformations as 4 × 4 homogeneous matrices with 15 degrees of freedom; the 3 × 4 camera matrix P with 11 degrees of freedom, computed from six or more 2-D–3-D point correspondences by the direct linear transform; plane-induced homographies H = K′(R + t nᵀ ÷ d)K⁻¹ for a scene plane nᵀX = d; image alignment and stitching by a homography fitted to matched features with RANSAC, then warping and blending the overlap; blending by feathering weights or by multi-band (Laplacian-pyramid) blending to hide seams, gain compensation across frames, and cylindrical or spherical warps for wide panoramas
- **Image experiments and reproducibility**: fixed image splits and dataset versions recorded with each result; one metric definition for every method (PSNR = 10 log₁₀(MAX² ÷ MSE) with the peak MAX stated; the IoU threshold for a detection match); seeds, augmentation policy and preprocessing pinned, so that a stranger can reproduce a reported gain from the stated pipeline; training-data snapshot manifests that list each file by path and content hash, with the schema, the split seed and the source query, so a snapshot can be rebuilt exactly
- **Gestalt grouping and perceptual organization**: the Gestalt cues of proximity, similarity, good continuation, closure, common fate and symmetry as evidence that image elements belong to one object; segmentation by clustering of pixels or features (intensity, colour, texture and position) as points in a feature space, grouped by k-means or mean shift, with the kernel bandwidth as the one free choice; the affinity matrix W of a similarity graph and the normalized cut Ncut(A, B) = cut(A, B) ÷ vol(A) + cut(A, B) ÷ vol(B), whose relaxation is solved by the second smallest generalized eigenvector of (D − W)y = λDy; the Hough transform as voting in parameter space, so that edge points agreeing on a line or circle are grouped
- **Algorithm evaluation and error analysis for multi-view geometry**: the geometric (reprojection) error as the maximum-likelihood cost when image points carry independent Gaussian noise, against the algebraic residual of a linear method, which weights points unevenly; the Sampson distance as the first-order approximation of geometric error for a fundamental-matrix fit; the gold-standard pipeline of Hartley's isotropic normalization (centroid to the origin, mean distance √2), a linear estimate, then nonlinear refinement of the geometric cost; error statistics from simulated data, reporting the residual against the injected noise level, the bias of the estimate and its covariance from the Jacobian at the optimum; sensitivity to the spread of the points and to the scale of the image coordinates
- **Camera models and calibration with a planar target**: the pinhole model with weak-perspective and orthographic approximations as its limits; intrinsics K (focal lengths, skew and principal point) and radial lens distortion as the nonlinear terms; Zhang's method on a planar target of known geometry, where each view gives a homography H = K[r₁ r₂ t] (up to scale) whose columns satisfy r₁ᵀr₂ = 0 and r₁ᵀr₁ = r₂ᵀr₂, two linear constraints on the image of the absolute conic ω = K⁻ᵀK⁻¹ per view, so five intrinsics need three or more views and four (zero skew) need two; K recovered from ω by Cholesky factorization; extrinsics from K⁻¹H; nonlinear refinement of intrinsics, distortion and poses by minimizing the reprojection error over all corner observations
- **Self-supervised visual representation learning**: pretext tasks that need no labels, such as predicting the rotation applied to an image, solving patch jigsaws and inpainting masked regions; contrastive instance discrimination with two augmented views of one image as positives and the NT-Xent loss −log(exp(sim(zᵢ, zⱼ) ÷ τ) ÷ Σₖ≠ᵢ exp(sim(zᵢ, zₖ) ÷ τ)) over a batch, with temperature τ; momentum encoders and a queue of negatives (MoCo), and negative-free self-distillation with a stop-gradient target network (BYOL, DINO); masked image modelling that reconstructs the pixels of a high fraction of masked patches, with the encoder seeing only the visible patches (masked autoencoders, with a 75 percent mask ratio); evaluation by linear probing of frozen features against fine-tuning on labelled data
- **Spatial filters and derivative edge detectors**: the discrete two-dimensional convolution sum over a kernel, and its separable form when the kernel is an outer product, so that an n × n filter costs 2n multiplications per pixel instead of n²; box and Gaussian smoothing with σ setting the scale; the first-derivative masks of Prewitt and Sobel with gradient magnitude and orientation; the Laplacian as the sum of second derivatives and the Laplacian of Gaussian ∇²G = ((x² + y² − 2σ²) ÷ σ⁴)·G, whose zero crossings mark edges and whose scale-normalized response selects blob scales; unsharp masking g = f + k(f − f_smooth) as sharpening built from smoothing
- **Nonlinear least squares and iterative refinement**: minimizing S(x) = Σᵢ rᵢ(x)² over parameters x with residual Jacobian J; the Gauss–Newton step from JᵀJ δ = −Jᵀr; Levenberg–Marquardt damping, (JᵀJ + λI)δ = −Jᵀr, with λ raised when a step fails to lower the cost and lowered when it succeeds, interpolating between Gauss–Newton and gradient descent; convergence to a local minimum, so initialization decides the answer; robust losses such as Huber that bound the influence of outlier residuals; in bundle adjustment the Jacobian is sparse, with a 2 × 6 block per camera and a 2 × 3 block per point observation, and the Schur complement eliminates the point blocks so that only the camera system is solved densely
- **Stereo depth and dense correspondence**: rectification of a calibrated pair by a homography per image so that epipolar lines become horizontal scanlines; disparity d = x_L − x_R and depth Z = fB ÷ d for focal length f and baseline B; window-based block matching by sum of absolute differences or normalized cross-correlation over a range of disparities, failing on textureless and occluded regions; the disparity cost volume and semi-global matching, which minimizes the sum of per-pixel costs and of penalties P₁ for a disparity change of one and P₂ > P₁ for larger jumps, along several scanline paths; the left–right consistency check to flag occlusions; depth uncertainty ΔZ ≈ Z² Δd ÷ (fB), so error grows with the square of distance
- **Feature detection and matching**: the Harris corner response R = det M − k(trace M)² over the structure tensor M summed in a window, large at corners, near zero on flat regions and negative on edges; scale-space extrema of the difference of Gaussians as an approximation to the scale-normalized Laplacian (SIFT); the SIFT descriptor as a 4 × 4 grid of cells with 8 gradient-orientation bins each, a 128-dimensional vector normalized against illumination change; binary descriptors (BRIEF, ORB) compared by Hamming distance; nearest-neighbour matching with Lowe's ratio test, accepting a match only when the best distance is under about 0.8 of the second best; repeatability versus distinctiveness as the trade-off in choosing a detector
- **Pixel relationships and connectivity**: a pixel's 4-neighbours are the pixels directly above, below, left and right of it, and its 8-neighbours add the four diagonals; m-adjacency keeps every 4-neighbour link and admits a diagonal link only when the two pixels have no common 4-neighbour in the set, which removes the double paths that 8-adjacency allows; connected components labelling gives one label to each region of mutually connected foreground pixels, by a two-pass scan with a union–find equivalence table or by a breadth-first flood fill; the distance measures between pixels are city-block D4 = |x − s| + |y − t|, chessboard D8 = max(|x − s|, |y − t|) and Euclidean √((x − s)² + (y − t)²), and the adjacency chosen fixes which pixels count as connected
- **Morphological operations**: erosion takes the minimum of the image over the structuring element placed at each pixel, which shrinks binary foreground, and dilation takes the maximum, which grows it; opening (erosion then dilation) removes specks and thin protrusions smaller than the element while keeping larger shapes near their size; closing (dilation then erosion) fills holes and gaps narrower than the element; the structuring element is the probe's shape and size, so it decides which features survive; binary cleanup chains these, opening to strip salt noise from a thresholded image and closing to fill pinholes in a foreground object, with the element large enough to cover the noise it removes and no larger than the features it must keep

### Information retrieval engines

- **Index construction**: the term–document incidence matrix as the starting point and why it does not scale; blocked sort-based indexing (BSBI) that sorts term–docID pairs per block and merges sorted runs; single-pass in-memory indexing (SPIMI) that builds per-block dictionaries; merging postings lists; distributed indexing by MapReduce and the cost of merges
- **Index compression**: postings stored as gaps between document IDs; variable-byte, Elias gamma and delta codes, and block codes such as PForDelta; dictionary compression by front coding and a single string with pointers; the space–time trade-off measured as bits per posting against decoding speed
- **Top-k retrieval and computing scores**: term-at-a-time and document-at-a-time accumulators over postings; cosine scoring with length normalization; top-k selection with a bounded heap instead of a full sort; early termination by WAND and MaxScore using per-term score upper bounds; champion lists as static pruning
- **XML retrieval**: documents as trees of elements; element-level indexing with structural context (paths); returning the smallest element that answers a query; structural conditions on paths and containment; weighting elements by tag and depth; evaluation at element granularity
- **Language models for retrieval (query likelihood)**: ranking documents by P(query | document) under a unigram language model per document; smoothing by Jelinek–Mercer interpolation or a Dirichlet prior so that a missing query term does not zero the score; the sum of log-probabilities as the ranking score; its relation to BM25 and TF-IDF
- **Probabilistic and feedback retrieval**: the probability ranking principle, which ranks documents by P(relevant | document) and is optimal for expected precision when relevance judgments are independent; the binary independence model, whose term weight is the log-odds log[p(1 − u) ÷ (u(1 − p))] for a term present in relevant (p) versus non-relevant (u) documents, with BM25 as its term-frequency-saturating extension; Rocchio relevance feedback, q′ = αq + β·mean(relevant vectors) − γ·mean(non-relevant vectors); pseudo-relevance feedback that treats the top-k documents as relevant; query expansion from a thesaurus or from term co-occurrence statistics that adds related terms to the query, with query drift (added terms that move the query off its topic) as the failure mode
- **Boolean retrieval and postings intersection**: AND, OR and NOT queries over postings lists; intersecting two sorted docID lists by a linear merge in O(x + y) comparisons; skip pointers spaced about √L apart in a list of length L, so the merge can jump over runs that cannot match; processing terms in ascending document frequency so the smallest intermediate list comes first
- **Tolerant retrieval and dictionaries**: wildcard queries by a permuterm index (every rotation of each term with a $ end marker, so a query such as mo*n becomes the prefix lookup n$mo* on the rotated index) and by a k-gram index that intersects the k-gram postings of the pattern and then filters the candidates against the full pattern; Soundex as a phonetic key that keeps the first letter, codes the following consonants as digits by sound class, collapses adjacent repeats and pads or cuts the code to four characters, so surnames that sound alike share a key
- **Link analysis**: HITS as query-specific link analysis, with a root set of pages matched by text and expanded by their in-links and out-links; hub scores h ← A·a and authority scores a ← Aᵀ·h, renormalized after each round, so the scores converge to the principal eigenvectors of AAᵀ and AᵀA; compared with PageRank's query-independent scores over the whole graph
- **PageRank and web crawling**: the random surfer as a Markov chain on the link graph, with the column-stochastic transition matrix M from out-link normalization and rank vector r solving r = ((1 − d) ÷ N)·1 + d·M·r for damping d (about 0.85), uniform teleportation and dangling pages spread over all pages; power iteration whose error shrinks by a factor d per step; personalized PageRank with a non-uniform teleport vector; the crawl frontier as a priority queue of URLs with robots.txt exclusion, per-host politeness delays and content deduplication by shingles or simhash; anchor text added to the index entry of the page it links to
- **Latent semantic indexing and matrix decompositions**: the term–document matrix X = UΣVᵀ by singular value decomposition, truncated to the k largest singular values, which by the Eckart–Young theorem is the best rank-k approximation in the Frobenius norm; documents represented by the rows of V_kΣ_k and a query folded in as q_k = Σ_k⁻¹U_kᵀq; cosine ranking in the reduced space, so that a document can match a query through synonyms; polysemy and the loss of exact term matching; choosing k by held-out retrieval effectiveness; the dense SVD cost of O(mn·min(m, n)) and the sparse and randomized methods used at scale
- **Multimodal and embedding retrieval evaluation**: image, document and audio items embedded in one vector space, with the query embedded by the same or a paired encoder; recall@k, mean reciprocal rank and nDCG over a labelled query set with graded relevance; recall at a fixed candidate budget as the measure of an approximate index; splitting queries and items so that near-duplicates of an evaluation query stay out of the index it is scored against; a per-slice report by query type, language and item category; the latency and memory of the embedding index weighed against the gain in recall

### Classical NLP and toolkits

- **Discourse analysis and coherence**: discourse as structure across sentences; rhetorical structure theory (RST) trees with nucleus and satellite spans and relations; the Penn Discourse TreeBank's explicit and implicit connective relations; the entity grid model of local coherence by grammatical-role transitions; the limits of automatic discourse parsing
- **Collocations**: word pairs that co-occur more often than chance; hypothesis tests (t-test, chi-square) and pointwise mutual information for ranking candidates; window-based co-occurrence counts; filtering by frequency and part-of-speech pattern; the NLTK collocation-finder workflow
- Pointwise mutual information: PMI(x, y) = log [P(x, y) ÷ (P(x) P(y))], estimated from counts as log(N·c(x, y) ÷ (c(x)·c(y))); it is positive when the pair co-occurs more often than independence predicts; PMI inflates pairs of rare words, since a pair seen once between two rare words scores high, so it is ranked alongside a frequency filter or a significance test
- **Text categorization**: document classification from bag-of-words features with multinomial and Bernoulli naive Bayes event models; Laplace smoothing; feature selection by chi-square or mutual information; precision, recall and F1 per class with micro and macro averages; Rocchio centroid classification, which assigns a document to the class whose TF-IDF centroid is nearest by cosine similarity, compared with kNN on the same vectors; stop-word removal from a fixed list before counting, and its cost for negations and short function words; linear support vector machines on the same weighted vectors, minimizing ½‖w‖² + C·Σᵢ max(0, 1 − yᵢ(wᵀxᵢ + b)) with one-versus-rest for several classes, and the margin as the reason they generalize well on sparse text
- **Semantic role labelling**: predicate–argument structure with PropBank-style roles (agent, patient, instrument); the pipeline of predicate identification, argument identification from parse-tree paths, and role classification; span-based labelling in neural models; precision, recall and F1 on argument spans
- **Sentence segmentation**: sentence-boundary decisions at periods, question marks and exclamation marks; abbreviation and initial handling; the unsupervised Punkt approach that learns abbreviations from unlabelled text; rule-based and statistical sentencizers; boundary errors that carry into tagging and parsing; word_tokenize as the Treebank-style word tokenizer, which runs sent_tokenize (the Punkt model) first and then splits clitics and punctuation (don't becomes do and n't)
- **Vocabulary cutoffs and UNK replacement**: frequency cutoffs that keep the top N types or types with count at least k; mapping rare or unseen words to an unknown token (UNK) or to word-class placeholders; out-of-vocabulary rate on held-out text as the measure; the trade-off between vocabulary size and the probability mass reserved for unknowns
- **Span offsets and token alignment**: character offsets as the canonical position of every token and annotation; mapping a span through a tokenizer that changes whitespace or splits words; aligning tokens from NLTK, spaCy and subword tokenizers so that labels move without drift; offset errors in entity extraction; the Hugging Face tokenizers library's encoding object, whose offsets field gives the character span of each subword and whose word_ids() maps each subword to its word index, so word-level labels are propagated to subwords and checked against those offsets
- **Kneser–Ney smoothing**: interpolated absolute discounting with a continuation distribution that counts distinct left contexts instead of raw frequency; the recursion across n-gram orders; why it beats add-one smoothing on sparse text; perplexity of the smoothed model on held-out text
- **NLTK corpus interfaces**: corpus readers exposing raw text, words, sentences and tagged tokens (raw, words, sents, tagged_words, tagged_sents); file identifiers and categories; the sentence as the unit of sequence models; corpus licences and reproducible file selection
- **Frequency distributions and conditional counts**: FreqDist as a count and probability table with most-common lists and hapaxes; ConditionalFreqDist as counts per condition (for example, per previous word or per tag); n-gram and tag-transition estimates built from them; the sparsity they expose; the defaultdict as a count map that returns zero for an unseen key, and the Index as a mapping from each key to the positions where it occurs; the concordance view, a keyword-in-context listing of each occurrence of a word with its left and right context windows, built from token positions
- **Treebanks and annotated corpora**: Penn Treebank bracketed parse trees and the Brown corpus as tagged text; CoNLL token-per-line and dependency formats; how annotation conventions (tokenization, bracket labels, empty elements) constrain evaluation; train, development and test splits of treebanks
- **Tagsets**: the 12-tag Universal POS set, the 45-tag Penn Treebank set (punctuation included) and the Brown tagset; mappings between them; why tagset choice changes accuracy and downstream use; tagged-token tuples and their unpacking
- **Baseline taggers and backoff chains**: default, regex, lookup and unigram taggers; bigram and trigram taggers that back off to the unigram and then the default tagger when a context is unseen; the sparse-data problem at higher orders; accuracy baselines measured on held-out tagged text
- **Brill transformation-based tagging**: starting from a baseline tagger, learning an ordered list of rules (change tag X to Y when the context matches) greedily by the drop in errors; rule templates; the interpretable rule list versus the probabilistic HMM tagger; training cost
- **spaCy industrial pipelines and processing components**: industrial text processing as a typed annotation engine (Doc, Token, Span) fed by an ordered pipeline (tokenizer, then tagger, parser, named-entity recognizer and custom components); components that are trained or rule-based and share one document; pipeline configuration and serialization
- **spaCy object model**: Language and Vocab with a string store mapping hashes to strings; Lexeme as shared lexical entries; Doc holding the tokens, with Token and Span as views over its array; character offsets, whitespace, lexical attributes and context-dependent attributes; why spans are cheap and stable
- **spaCy tokenizer design and exceptions**: non-destructive tokenization (doc.text equals the input); prefix, suffix and infix rules, token_match and special cases; language-specific exceptions for contractions and abbreviations; retokenization and pre-tokenized input; the tokenizer explanation that shows which rule produced each token
- **Noun chunks**: base noun phrases read from the dependency parse (noun heads with their modifiers); the language rules that define a chunk; use as entity candidates and keyphrases; dependence of chunk quality on the parser
- **Rule-based matching**: token patterns with attributes and quantifiers in the Matcher; exact-phrase matching in the PhraseMatcher for speed; the EntityRuler adding named-entity spans from patterns or dictionaries; combining rules before or after a statistical component; overlapping-span resolution
- **spaCy efficiency**: batching with nlp.pipe rather than one call per text; disabling or excluding unneeded components; batch size and memory; process-based parallelism and its start-method limits on macOS and Windows; GPU and transformer-component caveats
- **Choosing NLTK, spaCy or Hugging Face by goal**: NLTK for corpus inspection, frequency analysis, lexical resources and transparent baselines; spaCy for fast production annotation with rules plus models and robust document objects; Hugging Face for pretrained neural models, subword tokenizers and fine-tuning; the shared contracts (tokenization policy, offsets, labels, splits, metrics) that must match across all three; the labels contract (one tag scheme such as BIO or BILUO, with an explicit mapping between tagsets), the split contract (document-level splits so near-duplicates do not leak between train and test) and the metrics contract (one span-matching rule and one averaging rule for every system compared)
- **Coreference resolution**: mention detection, then grouping mentions that refer to the same entity into clusters, by pairwise coreference scores or a mention-pair classifier over features such as distance, head match and agreement in number and gender; the failures on pronouns and nested names; distinct from entity linking, which maps a mention to a knowledge-base entry; evaluation by MUC, B-cubed and CEAF cluster-level scores
- **Sentiment lexicons**: polarity lexicons with positive and negative scores per word, aggregated over a text by sum or mean; negation handled by scope marking from a negation word to the next punctuation, which flips polarity; intensifiers and downtoners as multipliers; domain shift, where a word's polarity changes between domains; lexicon coverage and the bias of lexicon-only systems; evaluation by accuracy and F1 against annotated reviews, compared with a supervised classifier on the same bag of words
- **Information extraction and named-entity recognition**: mention spans as BIO or BIOES tags scored by a sequence model, a conditional random field decoded by Viterbi or a neural tagger; relation extraction by lexical patterns (Hearst patterns for hyponyms), bootstrapping from seed pairs, and distant supervision that labels every sentence mentioning a known knowledge-base pair, with the label noise this assumption adds; template filling into event slots; open information extraction as subject–relation–object triples without a fixed schema; span scoring by exact-match precision, recall and F1, and relation scoring by correct pairs
- Multilingual named-entity recognition: one BIO tagger is trained over a shared multilingual encoder with one tagset, each language's annotation scheme mapped onto it; zero-shot transfer trains on a high-resource language and tests on another, and results are reported per language, since the gap between languages often exceeds the gap between models; scoring is exact-match span F1 in each language under the same span rule
- **Lexical acquisition from corpora**: learning lexical facts such as verb subcategorization frames, selectional preferences and collocation lists by counting frame cues in parsed text; a hypothesis test (binomial likelihood ratio or chi-square) that rejects a frame when its cue counts are explainable by the tagger's error rate alone; prepositional-phrase attachment decided by a log-likelihood ratio of verb-preposition against noun-preposition co-occurrence counts from unlabelled text (the Hindle–Rooth estimate); the precision–recall trade-off of the decision threshold; sparse counts for rare verbs and the smoothing that they require
- **Chomsky hierarchy and combinatory categorial grammar**: four classes of formal grammar ordered by production restrictions: regular (type 3, finite automata, and the class of an n-gram model of fixed order, whose state is the last n − 1 words), context-free (type 2, pushdown automata, the basis of constituency parsing), context-sensitive (type 1, linear-bounded automata) and unrestricted (type 0, Turing-equivalent); pumping lemmas as the test that a language is not regular or not context-free; cross-serial dependencies in some languages as the argument, contested, that natural language is not context-free, motivating the mildly context-sensitive class; combinatory categorial grammar with atomic categories such as NP and S, functor categories such as S\NP, forward and backward application, composition and type-raising, which derive coordination and long-range dependencies from one lexicon
- **Cache, topic and maximum-entropy language models**: the cache model that interpolates the n-gram estimate with the recent frequency of the word, P(w | h) = λ·P_cache(w) + (1 − λ)·P_n-gram(w | h), exploiting the burstiness of content words; the topic mixture P(w | h) = Σₜ P(w | t)·P(t | h), with topic posteriors updated as the document is read; the maximum-entropy language model P(w | h) = exp(Σᵢ λᵢ fᵢ(h, w)) ÷ Z(h) with feature functions and weights fitted by maximizing the likelihood of the training text, which is the maximum-entropy distribution matching the feature expectations; training by generalized iterative scaling or gradient ascent, with the normalizer Z(h) summed over the vocabulary as the cost driver
- **Word sense disambiguation**: resolving which sense of a polysemous word a context selects; the Lesk algorithm scoring each sense by the overlap of its dictionary gloss with the context words; supervised classifiers over a window of ±k words, collocations at fixed offsets and bag-of-words context, trained with naive Bayes or support vector machines on sense-tagged text; the most-frequent-sense baseline that a system must beat; knowledge-based similarity over WordNet paths; accuracy per word against that baseline; the scarcity of sense-tagged data as the bottleneck
