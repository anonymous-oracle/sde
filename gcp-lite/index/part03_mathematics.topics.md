### Proof and discrete mathematics

- **Proof techniques**
  - Direct proof, contraposition, contradiction and cases; biconditional proofs as two implications, proving P ⇒ Q and Q ⇒ P; existence proofs by explicit construction versus a contradiction showing that no counterexample exists; existence and uniqueness as two separate claims, ∃x P(x) and then ∃!x, where uniqueness shows that any two witnesses are equal, with the Banach fixed-point theorem as a worked example of both; a proof by contradiction assumes the negation of the claim and derives a statement that contradicts a known truth, such as assuming √2 = p/q in lowest terms, which gives p² = 2q² so p and q are both even, contradicting lowest terms; a direct proof assumes P and chains valid steps to Q, such as n even ⇒ n² even, since n = 2k gives n² = 2(2k²)
  - Ordinary and strong induction; structural induction over lists and trees; ordinary induction proves P(1) and then P(n) ⇒ P(n + 1); strong induction may assume P(k) for every k below n, which is equivalent but often more convenient; structural induction follows the shape of the data, proving the property for the base constructors (the empty list, a leaf) and then for each constructor applied to substructures that already have it, so the induction runs over the depth of a tree rather than over a number
  - The well-ordering principle: every nonempty set of natural numbers has a least element, which is equivalent to ordinary induction; it gives the descent argument, since a strictly decreasing sequence of natural numbers must stop, so a measure that decreases on every step proves termination
  - Invariants as the proof tool for algorithms: a property that holds before a loop, is preserved by each iteration and implies the desired result at exit; a proof has three steps, initialization (the invariant holds before the first iteration), maintenance (each iteration keeps it true) and termination (on exit the invariant together with the failed loop condition gives the postcondition), as when insertion sort's sorted prefix is shown to stay sorted
- **Logic and sets**
  - Propositions and connectives; implication, converse, inverse and contrapositive; logical equivalence and tautologies
  - Predicates and the quantifiers ∀ and ∃; negating quantified statements; a predicate P(x) becomes a statement once x is fixed; ∀x P(x) is true when P holds for every x in the domain, and ∃x P(x) when it holds for at least one; negation swaps the quantifiers and negates the predicate, so ¬∀x P(x) is equivalent to ∃x ¬P(x) and ¬∃x P(x) to ∀x ¬P(x); nested quantifiers matter in order, since ∀x∃y P(x, y) is weaker than ∃y∀x P(x, y)
  - Rules of inference: modus ponens, modus tollens, syllogism; modus ponens infers Q from P and P ⇒ Q; modus tollens infers ¬P from P ⇒ Q and ¬Q; hypothetical syllogism infers P ⇒ R from P ⇒ Q and Q ⇒ R; an argument is valid when its conclusion holds on every assignment that makes the premises true, and affirming the consequent (inferring P from P ⇒ Q and Q) is a fallacy because it is invalid
  - The universal set U: the set of all elements under consideration, of which every set in the discussion is a subset; complements are taken relative to it, Aᶜ = U ∖ A, so De Morgan's laws and 1_(Aᶜ) = 1 − 1_A hold only relative to a stated U, and the complement changes when the universe changes
  - Set-builder notation, subsets, power sets, Cartesian products; roster notation listing the elements of a finite set; the operations of union, intersection, complement and set difference, De Morgan's laws (A ∪ B)ᶜ = Aᶜ ∩ Bᶜ and (A ∩ B)ᶜ = Aᶜ ∪ Bᶜ, and the proof of a set identity by showing mutual inclusion; the empty set ∅ as the unique set with no elements, a subset of every set; a finite set as one in bijection with {1, …, n} for some n ≥ 0, and an infinite set as one in bijection with no such {1, …, n}, where without the axiom of choice an infinite set need not contain a countably infinite subset; the set-builder form {x : P(x)} collects the elements of a domain that satisfy the predicate P, as {x ∈ ℤ : x² < 10} = {−3, …, 3}; A ⊆ B means every element of A is in B, and a set with n elements has 2ⁿ subsets, one for each choice of whether to include each element, which is the size of its power set; a membership indicator 1_A(x) is 1 when x ∈ A and 0 otherwise, under which the connectives become arithmetic: 1_(A∩B) = 1_A·1_B (AND), 1_(A∪B) = 1_A + 1_B − 1_A·1_B (OR), 1_(Aᶜ) = 1 − 1_A (NOT), and A ⇒ B has indicator 1 − 1_A + 1_A·1_B, which is 0 only when A holds and B fails
- **Axiomatic set theory and mathematical logic**: the Zermelo–Fraenkel axioms with choice (extensionality, separation, pairing, union, power set, infinity, replacement, foundation and choice); Russell's paradox as the reason naive comprehension is restricted; Cantor's theorem |P(S)| > |S| for every set S; Zorn's lemma as equivalent to the axiom of choice for the existence of maximal elements; the completeness theorem of first-order logic (every semantic consequence is provable) against Gödel's incompleteness theorems, that a consistent, recursively axiomatized theory containing arithmetic is incomplete and cannot prove its own consistency; ordinals as transitive sets well-ordered by membership, with the first infinite ordinal ω, transfinite induction and transfinite recursion, and ordinal addition and multiplication, which are not commutative (1 + ω = ω ≠ ω + 1); cardinals as initial ordinals, with ℵ₀ the cardinality of the naturals and 2^ℵ₀ that of the reals, and the Schröder–Bernstein theorem that injections in both directions give a bijection
- **Relations, functions and counting**
  - Equivalence relations and partitions; partial orders; posets (P, ≤) as sets with a reflexive, antisymmetric and transitive order, drawn as Hasse diagrams; chains and antichains; Dilworth's theorem, that the largest antichain has as many elements as the fewest chains needed to cover the poset; joins, meets and linear extensions (topological sorts of a poset); an equivalence relation is reflexive, symmetric and transitive, and its classes [a] = {x : x ~ a} partition the set, since each element lies in exactly one class and two classes are either equal or disjoint, so an equivalence relation and a partition determine each other
  - Injections, surjections and bijections, and what they say about cardinality; composition of functions and the inverse f⁻¹, which exists exactly when f is a bijection and satisfies f⁻¹(f(x)) = x; countable sets such as the integers and the rationals, and Cantor's diagonal argument that the reals are uncountable; a sequence as a function from the natural numbers, with partial sums and the telescoping identity; the graph of f as the set of pairs (x, f(x)), and the graph of f⁻¹ as the reflection of that graph in the line y = x; f is one-one (injective) when f(x) = f(y) implies x = y, onto (surjective) when every element of the codomain is f(x) for some x, and bijective when both hold; the codomain is the set that contains all the outputs, distinct from the range, the set of outputs actually attained, so f is onto exactly when range = codomain; a function is given by a rule, a table of input–output pairs, a graph or code, the rule determines the table by evaluation, a table determines the function only on its listed inputs, and a curve is the graph of a function exactly when every vertical line meets it at most once; an even function satisfies f(−x) = f(x) and has a graph symmetric about the y-axis, an odd function satisfies f(−x) = −f(x) and has a graph symmetric about the origin; the floor ⌊x⌋ is the greatest integer at most x, so x − 1 < ⌊x⌋ ≤ x, the fractional part {x} = x − ⌊x⌋ lies in [0, 1), and the signum sgn(x) is −1, 0 or 1 by the sign of x
  - The pigeonhole principle; the sum and product rules; if n + 1 objects are placed in n boxes, some box holds two, and in general N objects in n boxes force a box with at least ⌈N/n⌉; the sum rule counts disjoint cases by adding and the product rule counts sequences of independent choices by multiplying; pigeonhole proves existence without constructing the witness, the tool behind hash collisions and quorum-intersection arguments
  - Permutations, combinations, the binomial theorem, inclusion–exclusion; permutations with repetition n^k and combinations with repetition C(n + k − 1, k) counted by stars and bars; the general term C(n, r)·aⁿ⁻ʳbʳ of (a + b)ⁿ and the middle term(s), one when n is even and two when n is odd; the binomial theorem proved by induction on n through Pascal's rule C(n, k) = C(n − 1, k − 1) + C(n − 1, k), with a = b = 1 giving the sum of coefficients 2ⁿ; inclusion–exclusion |A₁ ∪ … ∪ Aₙ| = Σᵢ|Aᵢ| − Σᵢⱼ|Aᵢ ∩ Aⱼ| + … + (−1)ⁿ⁺¹|A₁ ∩ … ∩ Aₙ|, proved by counting each element's memberships, with the derangement count Dₙ = n! Σₖ (−1)ᵏ/k! ≈ n!/e as its standard application; permutations as bijections of {1, …, n} with their cycle decomposition, and the sign sgn(σ) = (−1)^(n − c(σ)), where c(σ) counts the cycles of σ, multiplicative under composition, with exactly n!/2 even permutations for n ≥ 2
  - Linear recurrences solved by the characteristic equation; first-order difference systems x_{k+1} = Ax_k with solution x_k = A^k x_0, the matrix form of a linear recurrence; generating functions G(z) = Σₙ aₙzⁿ turning a recurrence into an algebraic equation for G, with the product of two series as their convolution, and the probability generating function G_X(s) = E[s^X], whose derivative at 1 gives the mean, G′(1) = E[X]; the forced (particular) solution for an input x[n], as in y[n] + a₁y[n−1] + ⋯ = x[n], is found by a trial form that matches the type of the input, such as a polynomial or exponential, and the general solution is the homogeneous plus the particular solution; the complete response is the zero-input response, from the initial conditions, plus the zero-state response, which is the convolution of x with the impulse response h[n]
  - Arithmetic and geometric progressions: aₙ = a + (n − 1)d with Sₙ = n(2a + (n − 1)d)/2 and arithmetic mean (a + b)/2; aₙ = arⁿ⁻¹ with Sₙ = a(1 − rⁿ)/(1 − r) for r ≠ 1, the sum to infinity S∞ = a/(1 − r) for |r| < 1, and the geometric mean √(ab)
- **Number theory for cryptography**
  - Divisibility; gcd and the Euclidean algorithm with its O(log n) step bound; a divides b when b = ka for some integer k; gcd(a, b) is found by replacing (a, b) with (b, a mod b) until the remainder is zero, since every common divisor of a and b also divides the remainder; the step count is O(log n) because two steps at least halve the smaller number, and the worst case is consecutive Fibonacci numbers
  - Bézout's identity and the extended Euclidean algorithm: for integers a and b there are integers x and y with ax + by = gcd(a, b), found by running the Euclidean steps backwards and carrying the coefficients along; when gcd(a, b) = 1, the coefficient x reduces to the inverse of a modulo b, which is how modular inverses are computed in practice
  - Modular arithmetic and modular inverses: a ≡ b (mod n) when n divides a − b, and arithmetic reduces mod n after each step to keep numbers bounded; a has an inverse modulo n exactly when gcd(a, n) = 1, and the extended Euclidean algorithm computes it in O(log n) steps; RSA's decryption exponent is such an inverse, modulo φ(n); the linear congruence ax ≡ b (mod n) has a solution exactly when d = gcd(a, n) divides b, and then exactly d solutions modulo n, found by dividing through by d to get (a/d)x ≡ b/d (mod n/d) and multiplying by the inverse of a/d
  - Primes and the fundamental theorem of arithmetic: every integer above 1 is a product of primes in exactly one way up to order, and Euclid's argument shows there are infinitely many primes; trial division by the primes up to √n decides primality, the sieve of Eratosthenes lists the primes up to N in O(N log log N), and primes of cryptographic size are found by random search with a probabilistic test such as Miller–Rabin, because trial division is far too slow at that size
  - Integer factorization: trial division by all primes up to √n costs O(√n) divisions and proves the factorization complete; Pollard's rho finds a nontrivial factor p of n in expected O(√p) steps by iterating x ↦ x² + c mod n and detecting a cycle modulo p with gcd(|x − y|, n), so for the smallest prime factor it runs in O(n^(1/4)) in the worst case; the quadratic sieve and the number field sieve are the fastest known general methods, with the general number field sieve running in L_n[1/3, (64/9)^(1/3)] subexponential time, the cost that sets the size of RSA moduli
  - Fermat's little theorem and Euler's theorem; the Chinese remainder theorem; for a prime p and a not divisible by p, a^(p−1) ≡ 1 (mod p); Euler's theorem extends this to any n with gcd(a, n) = 1 as a^φ(n) ≡ 1 (mod n), where φ(n) counts the residues coprime to n; the Chinese remainder theorem says that for pairwise coprime moduli n₁, …, n_k the system x ≡ aᵢ (mod nᵢ) has exactly one solution modulo their product, which lets RSA decryption run separately modulo p and modulo q
  - Fast modular exponentiation by repeated squaring: to compute aᵏ mod n, square the running value at each bit of k and multiply in a when the bit is 1, so about log₂ k squarings and at most as many multiplications replace k − 1 multiplications; each intermediate value is reduced mod n, so no number grows beyond n²; this is the step that makes RSA and Diffie–Hellman practical
  - The correctness proof of RSA; the discrete-logarithm problem; with n = pq and ed ≡ 1 (mod φ(n)), decryption gives (m^e)^d ≡ m (mod n) for every m, by applying Euler's theorem modulo p and modulo q separately and recombining with the Chinese remainder theorem; the security rests on the difficulty of factoring n, which the proof does not establish; the discrete-logarithm problem asks for x given gˣ mod p in a cyclic group, is believed hard in suitable groups, and underlies Diffie–Hellman and ElGamal; baby-step giant-step solves gˣ = h in O(√N) time and space for a group of order N, writing x = im + j with m = ⌈√N⌉ and matching the baby steps g^j against the giant steps h·g^(−im); Pohlig–Hellman reduces the problem to each prime-power factor of N and combines the answers by the Chinese remainder theorem, so its cost is set by the largest prime factor q of N, O(√q), and it is ineffective when N has a large prime factor; index calculus, the most efficient known method in prime-field groups, runs in subexponential time L_p[1/2] = exp((c + o(1))√(ln p · ln ln p))
- **Graph theory**
  - Vertices, edges and degrees; the handshake lemma; the degree of a vertex counts the edges meeting it, and each edge meets two vertices, so the degrees sum to twice the number of edges; in particular the number of odd-degree vertices is even, the fact that the handshake lemma states and that Euler tours rely on
  - Paths, cycles and connectivity: a path visits no vertex twice, a cycle returns to its start, and two vertices are connected when some path joins them; breadth-first or depth-first search labels the connected components in O(V + E) time, and a graph is connected exactly when it has one component
  - Trees: n − 1 edges and a unique path between any two vertices; a tree is a connected acyclic graph, and for n vertices any two of connected, acyclic and n − 1 edges imply the third; removing any edge disconnects a tree, and the unique path between two vertices is what makes parent pointers well defined
  - Bipartite graphs and 2-colouring: a graph is bipartite exactly when it has no odd cycle; a breadth-first search that colours each level alternately either produces a 2-colouring or finds an edge joining two vertices of the same colour, in O(V + E) time; König's theorem then equates the maximum matching with the minimum vertex cover in bipartite graphs
  - Directed acyclic graphs and topological order
  - Euler tours: a connected graph has a closed walk that uses every edge exactly once (an Euler circuit) exactly when every vertex has even degree, and an open Euler trail exists exactly when exactly two vertices have odd degree; Hierholzer's algorithm builds one in O(E) time by splicing closed sub-walks together
  - Planarity and colouring: Euler's formula V − E + F = 2 for a connected plane graph, which gives E ≤ 3V − 6 for simple planar graphs with at least three vertices, so K₅ and K₃,₃ are not planar; Kuratowski's theorem that a graph is planar exactly when it contains no subdivision of K₅ or K₃,₃; the chromatic number χ(G) with the greedy bound χ(G) ≤ Δ + 1 in any vertex order; Brooks' theorem that χ(G) ≤ Δ for a connected graph unless G is complete or an odd cycle; and the four colour theorem for planar graphs
- **Circular permutations**: (n − 1)! arrangements of n distinct objects around a circle, since rotations are identified; fixing one object to break the rotational symmetry; reflections identify further arrangements only for necklaces and bracelets
- **Multinomial theorem and expansion**: (x₁ + … + xₖ)ⁿ as a sum over compositions of n with coefficients n!/(n₁! … nₖ!); the coefficient as the number of ways to split n labelled items into k labelled groups; the count of terms C(n + k − 1, k − 1); extracting a single coefficient
- **Catalan numbers**: Cₙ = C(2n, n)/(n + 1) counting correct bracketings, binary trees with n internal nodes and lattice paths that never cross the diagonal; the recurrence Cₙ₊₁ = Σᵢ CᵢCₙ₋ᵢ; growth as 4ⁿ/(n^{3/2}√π)
- **Burnside's lemma and orbit counting**: for a finite group G acting on a set X, the orbits as the equivalence classes of the action; the orbit–stabilizer theorem |G·x| · |G_x| = |G|; Burnside's lemma, that the number of orbits equals (1/|G|) Σ over g in G of |Fix(g)|, where Fix(g) is the set of points that g leaves unchanged; counting necklaces of n beads in k colours by summing over the rotations, which gives (1/n) Σ over d dividing n of φ(d)·k^(n/d) with Euler's φ; the cycle-index form of Pólya's enumeration theorem

### Probability

- **Foundations**
  - Sample spaces, events and the axioms; the Kolmogorov axioms: nonnegativity, P(Ω) = 1, and countable additivity for pairwise disjoint events; finite additivity, P(A ∪ B) = P(A) + P(B) for disjoint A and B, is weaker, and a finitely additive assignment need not be countably additive, which is the source of nonconglomerability on infinite partitions; a probability can be read as the long-run frequency or the propensity of a repeatable experiment, as the logical support that evidence gives a hypothesis, or as a subjective degree of belief about a one-off outcome, and the axioms and the rules derived from them hold under each reading; a Venn diagram draws the sample space as a region whose subregions' areas stand for the probabilities of events
  - Joint and marginal probability; marginalizing a joint table; joint densities f(x, y) for continuous variables, with marginals f_X(x) = ∫ f(x, y) dy, conditional densities f(y | x) = f(x, y)/f_X(x), and independence equivalent to f(x, y) = f_X(x)·f_Y(y)
  - Conditional probability, independence and Bayes' theorem derived from the definition; the prior P(H) is the probability of a hypothesis before the data, and the posterior P(H | D) ∝ P(D | H)P(H) reweighs it; a simple hypothesis fixes the parameter, θ = θ₀, while a compound hypothesis allows a set of values, θ ∈ Θ₁, so its likelihood is the average or maximum over that set; for independent events the product rule gives P(A ∩ B) = P(A)P(B), so two independent fair coin tosses give P(HH) = 1/4 and two independent fair dice sum to 7 with probability 6/36 = 1/6
  - Random variables, expectation, linearity of expectation (no independence needed), variance and covariance; E[X] = Σ x·p(x) for a discrete variable and ∫ x f(x) dx for a continuous one, with E[g(X)] given by the same sum or integral with g(x) in place of x; Var(X) = E[(X − μ)²] = E[X²] − μ²; Cov(X, Y) = E[XY] − E[X]E[Y], which is zero when X and Y are independent
  - Indicator variables: the indicator 1{A} of an event A is 1 when A occurs and 0 otherwise, so its expectation is P(A); a count of events is a sum of indicators, and linearity of expectation gives its mean without any independence assumption, as in the expected number of fixed points of a random permutation, which is 1
- **Distributions**
  - Probability mass, density and cumulative distribution functions: a discrete variable has a mass function p(x) = P(X = x) that sums to 1, and a continuous one has a density f with P(a ≤ X ≤ b) equal to the integral of f over [a, b]; the cumulative distribution function F(x) = P(X ≤ x) is nondecreasing, runs from 0 to 1, and its derivative is the density wherever the density exists
  - Bernoulli, binomial, geometric, Poisson, uniform, exponential and normal, with their means and variances; the beta distribution on [0, 1] with density x^(α−1)(1 − x)^(β−1)/B(α, β) and mean α/(α + β), and its conjugacy with the binomial: a Beta(α, β) prior with k successes in n trials gives the posterior Beta(α + k, β + n − k); the normal law derived twice, by Gauss, who showed that if the arithmetic mean is the maximum-likelihood location of independent errors for every sample, then the log-density is quadratic, and by Herschel and Maxwell, who showed that if the two coordinates of a point in the plane are independent and the joint density depends only on the distance from the origin, then each coordinate is normal
  - Error cancellation and the normal law: the errors of many small independent causes partly cancel, so the sum of n independent errors each with variance σ² has spread σ√n while their mean has variance σ²/n and standard error σ/√n; the central limit theorem makes the standardized sum normal in the limit, and the sum of two independent normals is normal with the variances adding
  - The memoryless property: the geometric law (discrete) and the exponential law (continuous) are the only ones with P(X > s + t | X > s) = P(X > t), so the time already waited says nothing about the remaining time; a Poisson arrival process inherits this, because its interarrival times are exponential, which is why it models independent requests well
  - Sampling from distributions; Monte Carlo estimation; survey sampling designs: stratified sampling with proportional allocation, whose variance is no larger than simple random sampling's, and cluster sampling, whose variance grows with the correlation inside each cluster; estimands as the population quantity a design targets (a mean, a total or a difference in means), distinct from the estimator, the rule applied to the sample, and the estimate, its value; design-based inference, in which the randomness comes from the sampling design, with the Horvitz–Thompson total Σ yᵢ/πᵢ unbiased when every unit has a known inclusion probability πᵢ > 0
  - Sampling without replacement and the hypergeometric distribution: drawing n items from a population of N with K successes gives P(X = k) = C(K, k)·C(N − K, n − k)/C(N, n), with mean nK/N and variance n(K/N)(1 − K/N)(N − n)/(N − 1), the finite-population correction (N − n)/(N − 1) that makes its variance smaller than the binomial's; the binomial as the limit when N is large
  - Location-scale families: a family of densities f_(μ,σ)(x) = (1/σ) f((x − μ)/σ) generated by one standard density f, with μ the location and σ > 0 the scale; the normal and the Laplace as location-scale families, the exponential as a scale family with its location fixed at 0; the standardized variable Z = (X − μ)/σ with the reference density, which makes estimators of μ and σ equivariant under the change of units
  - The multivariate normal N(μ, Σ) with density (2π)^(−d/2)|Σ|^(−1/2) exp(−½(x − μ)ᵀΣ⁻¹(x − μ)) for a positive definite covariance Σ; closed under affine maps, so that AX + b is normal with mean Aμ + b and covariance AΣAᵀ; its marginals are normal; its conditional mean E[X₁ | X₂] = μ₁ + Σ₁₂Σ₂₂⁻¹(x₂ − μ₂) is linear in the observed coordinates; uncorrelated coordinates are independent
- **The birthday bound**
  - P(some collision) ≈ 1 − e^(−n(n−1)/2N); collisions become likely near n ≈ √N
  - Why a random 64-bit ID collides after about 2³² draws
- **Tail bounds and order statistics**
  - Markov, Chebyshev and Chernoff bounds; the union bound; Markov gives P(X ≥ a) ≤ E[X]/a for nonnegative X; Chebyshev gives P(|X − μ| ≥ kσ) ≤ 1/k² by applying Markov to (X − μ)²; Chernoff bounds apply Markov to e^(tX) and optimize over t > 0, giving exponential tails for sums of independent variables; the union bound says P(A₁ ∪ … ∪ Aₙ) ≤ Σ P(Aᵢ) with no independence needed; for a centred random variable X, sub-Gaussianity with parameter σ means E[e^(tX)] ≤ e^(σ²t²/2) for all t, and Hoeffding's inequality for independent variables in a common range [a, b] gives P(|x̄ − μ| ≥ s) ≤ 2 exp(−2ns²/(b − a)²), so the deviation of a sample mean is O(√(log(1/δ)/n)) with probability at least 1 − δ; the binomial tail P(X ≥ k) = Σ from j = k to n of C(n, j)pʲ(1 − p)ⁿ⁻ʲ is exact, and for a sum of independent Bernoulli variables with mean μ the Chernoff bound gives P(X ≥ (1 + δ)μ) ≤ (e^δ/(1 + δ)^(1+δ))^μ
  - The law of large numbers and the central limit theorem; convergence in probability (the weak law) versus almost sure convergence (the strong law), which is the stronger mode; convergence in distribution, Fₙ(x) → F(x) at every continuity point of F, the sense in which the central limit theorem holds; the chain of implications almost sure convergence ⇒ convergence in probability ⇒ convergence in distribution, whose converses fail in general
  - The maximum of n samples: P(max ≤ t) = F(t)ⁿ; the k-th order statistic X₍ₖ₎ with P(X₍ₖ₎ ≤ t) = Σ from j = k to n of C(n, j)·F(t)ʲ·(1 − F(t))ⁿ⁻ʲ
  - Fan-out tails: a request waiting on 100 sub-requests, each slow with probability 1%, is slow with probability 1 − 0.99¹⁰⁰ ≈ 63%
  - Hedged requests: a second copy after the p95 delay makes the tail P(X > t)² for about 5% extra load; tied requests cancel the loser
  - Why averages hide tails, and why a p99 cannot be averaged across hosts: the mean moves only slightly when a small fraction of requests becomes very slow, while the 99th percentile can rise several-fold, so an average can look healthy while the tail is failing
- **Balls into bins**
  - n requests at random onto n servers: maximum load Θ(log n / log log n)
  - The power of two choices: the less loaded of d ≥ 2 random servers drops the maximum load to ln ln n / ln d + O(1)
  - Why "least connections" over stale load reports herds, and why two random choices tolerate staleness
- **Stochastic processes**
  - Markov chains and stationary distributions; the transition matrix P, the stationary equation πP = π, and the n-step probabilities given by P^n, whose rows converge to the stationary law for an irreducible aperiodic chain
  - Random walks: the simple symmetric walk on ℤ, Sₙ = X₁ + ⋯ + Xₙ with each Xᵢ = ±1 with probability ½, so E[Sₙ] = 0 and Var(Sₙ) = n; P(S₂ₙ = 0) = C(2n, n)/4ⁿ ≈ 1/√(πn); the walk is recurrent (it returns to 0 with probability 1) in one and two dimensions and transient in three or more (Pólya's theorem); gambler's ruin with fair steps started at k between 0 and N gives P(reach N before 0) = k/N and expected duration k(N − k)
  - Poisson processes: the counting process N(t) with independent increments and N(t + s) − N(t) ~ Poisson(λs), so that P(N(t) = k) = e^(−λt)(λt)ᵏ/k!; interarrival times exponential with mean 1/λ and memoryless; superposition adds rates, and thinning keeps each event independently with probability p
  - Random processes as indexed families of random variables X(t) or Xₙ with their sample paths; strict and wide-sense stationarity, the wide-sense case requiring a constant mean and an autocorrelation R(s, t) = E[X(s)X(t)] that depends only on t − s; ergodicity, under which a time average along one sample path converges to the ensemble mean; the Wiener–Khinchin theorem, under which the power spectral density is the Fourier transform of the autocorrelation
- **Plausible reasoning and Cox's desiderata**: degrees of plausibility ordered qualitatively; Cox's desiderata of consistency and rationality forcing the sum and product rules up to a monotone reparametrization; probability as the unique consistent extension of deductive logic to uncertain propositions; deductive reasoning gives certain conclusions from premises, whereas plausible reasoning only raises or lowers the plausibility of a conclusion, and common language is too vague and context-dependent to carry deduction reliably, so formal logic fixes the meaning of each connective; the analogy with physical theories is that a theory of plausible inference, like a physical theory, is built from a few axioms and checked against the cases it must reproduce
- **Law of total probability**: P(B) = Σᵢ P(B | Aᵢ)P(Aᵢ) over a partition of the sample space; the continuous form with integration against a density; its role as the denominator of Bayes' theorem when conditioning on competing causes; the conditional expectation E[Y | X] is the function of X obtained by averaging Y over its conditional law given X, with the law of total expectation E[Y] = E[E[Y | X]] as the expectation form of the partition rule, and E[g(X)Y | X] = g(X)E[Y | X] for any function g, so what is known given X can be taken out of the average
- **Transformations of random variables**: the change-of-variable formula f_Y(y) = f_X(g⁻¹(y))·|d g⁻¹(y)/dy| for monotone maps; the CDF method for general maps; summing over branches for non-monotone maps; the Jacobian determinant for vector transformations
- **Log-normal and multinomial distributions**: a log-normal variable exp(X) with X normal, median e^μ and mean e^(μ + σ²/2), arising from multiplicative effects; the multinomial as n trials over k categories with probability n!/(∏ nᵢ!)·∏ pᵢ^nᵢ; negative covariance between category counts
- **Maximum-entropy distributions**: maximizing entropy under moment constraints with Lagrange multipliers, giving exponential-family densities; the uniform law with no constraint, the geometric law for a fixed mean on the non-negative integers, the normal for a fixed mean and variance, the exponential for a fixed mean on the positive reals; the maximum-entropy principle as the rule for choosing a prior or model consistent with known constraints and nothing more; the multiplicity of a macrostate is the number of microscopic arrangements W = N!/(n₁!⋯n_k!) that realize occupation counts nᵢ, and by Stirling's formula (1/N) log W approaches the entropy −Σ pᵢ log pᵢ with pᵢ = nᵢ/N, so maximum entropy is maximum multiplicity
- **Jeffreys and ignorance priors**: non-informative priors for parameters about which nothing is known; the Jeffreys prior proportional to the square root of the Fisher information, invariant under reparametrization; the flat prior for a location parameter and 1/σ for a scale parameter; a flat prior in one parametrization becoming informative in another; for a Poisson rate λ the Jeffreys prior is proportional to λ^(−1/2), since one Poisson observation carries Fisher information 1/λ, whereas the scale-invariant prior 1/λ comes from requiring the answer to be unchanged by rescaling the time unit, the worked case in which the two rules differ; a truncated prior, flat on [a, b] and zero outside, expresses a known bounded range, and the urn problem inverts a draw distribution into a posterior over the urn's contents by Bayes' theorem, which with a uniform prior on the composition gives Laplace's rule
- **Paradoxes of probability (Borel–Kolmogorov)**: conditioning on an event of probability zero depends on the limiting sequence of events chosen, so the same conditioning event on a sphere yields different conditional densities in different coordinates; nonconglomerability and the marginalization paradox as related failures of naive conditioning; Bertrand's paradox: a chord of a circle chosen at random has probability 1/3 of exceeding the side of the inscribed equilateral triangle if its random endpoints are chosen, 1/2 if its distance from the centre is chosen at random and 1/4 if its midpoint is uniform in the disc, the three answers differing only in the invariant measure used to pick the chord, so the question must state the random mechanism
- **Rule of succession and exchangeable sequences**: a sequence is exchangeable when its joint law is invariant under permutation of its terms; de Finetti's representation as a mixture of independent and identically distributed sequences; Laplace's rule (k + 1)/(n + 2) after k successes in n trials under a uniform prior; Carnap's λ-continuum generalizes the rule to (kᵢ + λ/K)/(n + λ) for K categories, with kᵢ the count of category i, so λ = 2 with two categories recovers Laplace's rule, λ → 0 gives the observed frequency kᵢ/n, and λ → ∞ gives the uniform 1/K

### Statistics and experimental design

- **Estimation**
  - Estimators, bias and variance; maximum-likelihood estimation; Fisher information; consistency (θ̂ₙ → θ in probability as n grows) and asymptotic normality of the maximum-likelihood estimator; the mean squared error as bias² + variance, and the trade of added bias for lower variance; Fisher information I(θ) = E[(∂ log f(X; θ)/∂θ)²] = −E[∂² log f(X; θ)/∂θ²] under regularity conditions, additive over independent observations so that n of them carry n·I(θ), with the Bernoulli case I(p) = 1/(p(1 − p)); the maximum-likelihood estimator is equivariant under reparametrization, so the MLE of g(θ) is g(θ̂) for any function g, with the induced likelihood defining it when g is not one-to-one
  - The sample mean, its standard error σ/√n, and confidence intervals; the median and mode as the other measures of central tendency, the median being the 0.5 quantile and the trimmed mean its outlier-resistant compromise; the (1 − α) confidence interval for a normal mean is x̄ ± z·σ/√n when σ is known and x̄ ± t_(n−1)·s/√n when σ is estimated by s, and it is the set of values μ₀ that a two-sided level-α test does not reject (the duality of intervals and tests); a confidence set C(X) has coverage P(θ ∈ C(X)) ≥ 1 − α, which means that the procedure, repeated over many samples, captures the true value with frequency 1 − α, not that one realized interval has that probability
  - Bayesian inference: prior and posterior updates; the naive Bayes classifier, whose posterior P(y | x) ∝ P(y)·∏ᵢ P(xᵢ | y) assumes the features are independent given the class; CART (classification and regression trees) grows a tree by a greedy search over binary splits, at each node choosing the feature and threshold that most reduce an impurity measure such as the Gini index or entropy, and then prunes by cost-complexity pruning, minimizing training error plus α times the number of leaves over nested subtrees, with α chosen by cross-validation; Bayesian model averaging weights each model Mₘ by its posterior probability, P(y | x, D) = Σₘ P(y | x, Mₘ)P(Mₘ | D), and stacking instead fits the combination weights on held-out predictions by minimizing their cross-validated loss
  - The bootstrap and the jackknife; the parametric bootstrap, which resamples from a fitted distribution instead of the data, and the jackknife's leave-one-out estimate of bias; percentile intervals as the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles of the bootstrap distribution of the estimate, and the bias-corrected and accelerated (BCa) interval, which shifts those percentile cut-points by a bias constant and an acceleration constant computed from the jackknife
- **Hypothesis testing**
  - Hypothesis tests, p-values and their common misreadings: a p-value is the probability, if the null hypothesis were true, of a test statistic at least as extreme as the one observed; it is not the probability that the null is true, and a non-significant result is not evidence that no effect exists; the significance level α is fixed before the data are seen and bounds the false-positive rate of one test
  - Power and sample size; the sample size of an A/B test, n = 2σ²(z₁₋α/₂ + z₁₋β)²/δ² per arm for a difference δ in means at two-sided level α and power 1 − β, with each unit assigned to one variant by hashing its identifier into buckets so that its variant stays fixed across sessions; canary releases, which send a change to a small traffic slice, watch guardrail metrics against the control and roll back on a breach; and shadow traffic, which runs the new path on copies of requests without serving its output to users
  - Practical significance and effect size: a standardized effect such as Cohen's d = (μ₁ − μ₂)/σ, read against the conventional benchmarks of 0.2 (small), 0.5 (medium) and 0.8 (large), and reported beside the p-value; a confidence interval for the difference in means or proportions as the range of effect sizes compatible with the data; the minimum detectable effect, the smallest effect that a test of given power and sample size can reliably find; a statistically significant difference too small to matter in the product's units, and the reverse
  - Multiple testing and the false discovery rate: testing m true nulls at level α each gives a family-wise error rate of 1 − (1 − α)^m when the tests are independent, so Bonferroni tests each at α/m; the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure sorts the p-values and rejects every hypothesis up to the largest i with p₍ᵢ₎ ≤ (i/m)q, which controls the expected share of false discoveries among the rejections at level q
  - Sequential testing and the peeking problem; CUPED variance reduction; checking a fixed-horizon test repeatedly and stopping at the first p below 0.05 pushes the false-positive rate well above 5%, so either fix the number of looks in advance with group-sequential boundaries or use always-valid p-values; CUPED subtracts from each outcome the part predicted by a pre-experiment covariate, Y − θ(X − X̄) with θ = cov(X, Y)/var(X), which shrinks the variance without biasing the difference between groups
  - Chi-squared tests: the goodness-of-fit statistic Σᵢ (Oᵢ − Eᵢ)²/Eᵢ over k cells, approximately χ² with k − 1 − m degrees of freedom when m parameters are estimated from the data; the test of independence in an r × c contingency table, with expected counts Eᵢⱼ = (row total)(column total)/n and (r − 1)(c − 1) degrees of freedom; the rule that expected counts should be at least about 5
- **Quantiles**
  - Estimating quantiles from histograms: with bucketed counts, find the bucket where the cumulative count crosses the target rank and interpolate linearly inside it; the error is bounded by the width of that bucket, so the bucket boundaries set the precision, and a tail with few counts in wide buckets is estimated poorly
  - Why per-host p99 values do not average to the fleet p99: a quantile is not a linear function of the samples, so the mean of the per-host p99 values says nothing reliable about the fleet's p99, which is set by the slowest 1% of all requests and may come from one host; to get the fleet quantile, merge the raw samples or mergeable histograms (fixed-bucket or sketch structures such as t-digest) and then take the quantile
- **Correlation and causation**
  - Confounders: a confounder causes both the treatment and the outcome, so the naive difference between treated and untreated mixes the treatment's effect with the confounder's; randomization breaks that link, while in observational data the confounder must be measured and adjusted for, and Simpson's paradox is the reversal a confounder can produce
  - Causal DAGs and adjustment; propensity scores and inverse-propensity weighting; the backdoor criterion, under which a set Z of covariates adjusts for confounding when it blocks every backdoor path from treatment to outcome and contains no descendant of the treatment
  - Difference-in-differences; uplift modelling; difference-in-differences compares the change over time in a treated group with the change in a control group, so the shared trend cancels under the parallel-trends assumption that both groups would have moved alike without treatment; uplift modelling estimates for each unit the difference in outcome probability with and without treatment, and targets the units whose outcome the treatment changes rather than those who would act anyway
- **Association between variables**: the correlation matrix; Pearson correlation for linear association versus Spearman rank correlation for monotone association and outlier resistance; Kendall tau from concordant and discordant pairs; correlation is not causation; Pearson's r = Σ(xᵢ − x̄)(yᵢ − ȳ)/√(Σ(xᵢ − x̄)² · Σ(yᵢ − ȳ)²) = cov(X, Y)/(s_x s_y), which lies in [−1, 1]; the simple linear regression of y on x has slope β̂₁ = r·s_y/s_x and intercept β̂₀ = ȳ − β̂₁x̄, so the fitted slope is the correlation rescaled by the two standard deviations, and R² = r² for simple regression
- **Functional and structural relationships**: in the functional model the regressors are fixed and only the response carries error, so ordinary least squares minimizes vertical residuals; in the structural model each regressor is itself a random variable observed with error, and with noisy regressors the least-squares slope is attenuated toward zero by the factor σₓ²/(σₓ² + σᵤ²) (errors in variables); total least squares minimizes the sum of squared perpendicular distances to the fitted line, which is the direction of smallest singular value of the centred data matrix
- **Sufficiency and the factorization theorem**: a statistic T(X) is sufficient for θ when the conditional law of the data given T does not depend on θ; the factorization criterion f(x; θ) = g(T(x); θ)h(x); the sample mean of a normal sample as sufficient for its mean; a sufficient statistic loses no information about θ; the Rao–Blackwell theorem says that conditioning an unbiased estimator on a sufficient statistic gives an estimator with mean squared error no larger, E[(E[θ̂ | T] − θ)²] ≤ E[(θ̂ − θ)²], so improvements are found among functions of a sufficient statistic, and with a complete sufficient statistic the result is the unique best unbiased estimator (Lehmann–Scheffé); the likelihood principle says that all the evidence about θ in an experiment lies in its likelihood function, so experiments with proportional likelihoods carry the same evidence
- **Basu's theorem and complete sufficient statistics**: completeness, meaning E[g(T)] = 0 for all θ forces g(T) = 0 almost surely; a complete sufficient statistic is independent of every ancillary statistic; the sample mean and sample variance of a normal sample are independent; an ancillary statistic is one whose distribution does not depend on θ, such as the sample range of a normal sample with known variance, which is ancillary for the mean, so that Basu's theorem makes it independent of the complete sufficient sample mean
- **Exponential families and multiparameter models**: densities in the form exp(η(θ)·T(x) − A(θ))h(x); natural parameters, and the log-partition function A whose derivatives give the mean and variance of T; the normal, Bernoulli and Poisson as members; multiparameter maximum likelihood with the Fisher information matrix
- **Method of moments**: equating sample moments to population moments and solving for the parameters; consistency from the law of large numbers; the gamma distribution as a worked example; efficiency compared with maximum likelihood
- **Delta method**: the asymptotic normal law of g(θ̂) from a central limit theorem for θ̂ through a first-order Taylor expansion, with variance g′(θ)²σ²/n; variance-stabilizing transformations; the second-order case when g′(θ) = 0
- **Cramér–Rao bound**: Var(θ̂) ≥ 1/(n I(θ)) for unbiased estimators, from the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality applied to the score; efficient estimators attain it and the maximum-likelihood estimator attains it asymptotically; the Bernoulli mean as a worked case; the asymptotic relative efficiency of estimator A relative to B is the limit of the ratio of the sample sizes that give the same variance, which for asymptotic variances σ_A²/n and σ_B²/n is σ_B²/σ_A², and the Pitman efficiency of tests is the analogous ratio of the sample sizes needed for the same power at the same level under local alternatives; an estimator is asymptotically efficient when its asymptotic variance equals 1/I(θ)
- **Student's t and F distributions**: t as a standard normal divided by the square root of a chi-square over its degrees of freedom, with heavier tails at small n; F as a ratio of independent scaled chi-squares, governing variance ratios and F-tests; the chi-square distribution as a sum of k squared independent standard normals, with mean k and variance 2k, and (n − 1)s²/σ² distributed χ²(n−1) for a normal sample, the fact from which t and F are built
- **One-sample and two-sample t-tests**: t = (x̄ − μ₀)/(s/√n) with n − 1 degrees of freedom; Welch's test for unequal variances; paired differences; the assumptions of independence and an approximately normal sample mean
- **z-test**: z = (x̄ − μ₀)/(σ/√n) when σ is known or n is large; the two-proportion z-test for conversion rates, with a pooled variance under the null
- **Type I and Type II errors**: rejecting a true null with probability α, the significance level; failing to reject a false one with probability β; power 1 − β; the trade-off at a fixed sample size and the Neyman–Pearson lemma for the most powerful test at a given α
- **Analysis of variance (ANOVA)**: splitting the total sum of squares into between-group and within-group parts; the F ratio of mean squares under the null of equal means; one-way and two-way designs; post hoc comparisons with multiple-comparison correction
- **Mann–Whitney U test**: a rank test for two independent samples, where U counts the pairs in which one sample exceeds the other; the normal approximation for large samples with a tie correction; insensitive to outliers because only ranks enter
- **Wilcoxon signed-rank test**: paired differences ranked by absolute size, with the sum of ranks of positive differences as the statistic; its null distribution under symmetry about zero; corrections for ties and zero differences
- **Skewness and kurtosis**: standardized third and fourth central moments, skewness E[(X − μ)³]/σ³ and excess kurtosis E[(X − μ)⁴]/σ⁴ − 3; what they say about asymmetry and tail weight; sample estimates of them being unstable at small n
- **Nonparametric inference**: estimating a distribution without a parametric family; the empirical CDF Fₙ(x) = (1/n)Σ 1{Xᵢ ≤ x} and the Dvoretzky–Kiefer–Wolfowitz bound on its uniform error; kernel density estimation and the bias–variance trade-off in its bandwidth; permutation tests that shuffle group labels to build the null distribution of a statistic under exchangeability; a statistical functional T(F) maps a distribution to the quantity of interest, such as the mean ∫x dF or a quantile, and the plug-in principle estimates it by T(Fₙ), applying the same functional to the empirical distribution, so the sample mean is T(Fₙ) for the mean functional
- **Exploratory data analysis**: the five-number summary (minimum, Q1, median, Q3, maximum) drawn as a box plot, with the 1.5 × IQR fence (IQR = Q3 − Q1) for flagging outliers; residual plots against fitted values to check linearity and constant variance; quantile–quantile plots against a reference distribution to compare tail shapes; the four measurement scales of a variable: nominal (unordered categories, summarized by the mode and counts), ordinal (ordered categories, summarized by the median and quantiles), interval (equal differences on an arbitrary zero, where means and differences are meaningful) and ratio (a true zero, where ratios and the geometric mean are meaningful), and the scale fixes which summaries and tests are valid
- **Sampling distributions**: a random sample X₁…Xₙ as n independent draws from one distribution, and a statistic as a function of the sample; the sampling distribution of a statistic as its law over repeated samples; for the sample mean, E[x̄] = μ and Var(x̄) = σ²/n, exactly normal when the population is normal and approximately normal for large n by the central limit theorem; the t statistic (x̄ − μ)/(s/√n) as the standardized sample mean when the scale is estimated, and the standard error as the standard deviation of a sampling distribution
- **Kernel methods**: a positive-definite kernel k(x, y) that equals an inner product in some feature space (Mercer's theorem); the kernel trick of computing those inner products without forming the feature space; the representer theorem, under which a regularized empirical-risk minimizer lies in the span of the kernel functions at the training points, so that the problem reduces to the n × n Gram matrix K; kernel ridge regression with coefficients α = (K + λI)⁻¹y, costing O(n³) to solve; the Gaussian kernel exp(−‖x − y‖²/(2σ²)) and its bandwidth as the bias–variance knob shared with kernel density estimation
- **Moment generating functions**: M_X(t) = E[e^(tX)], whose k-th derivative at 0 gives the k-th moment E[X^k]; uniqueness, so that an MGF finite on an interval determines the distribution; M_(X+Y)(t) = M_X(t)·M_Y(t) for independent X and Y, which is how sums of normals and Poissons are identified; cumulants as the coefficients of log M_X(t); the cumulants κₙ are the derivatives of log M_X at 0, so κ₁ = E[X] and κ₂ = Var(X), and since log M is additive over independent sums, the cumulants of an independent sum are the sums of the cumulants

### Queueing and performance modelling

- **Operational laws** (no distributional assumption)
  - The utilization law U = X·S: for a resource, the fraction of time busy equals the completion rate X times the mean service time S each completion needs there, so U = X·S with U ≤ 1; the law needs only averages over an observation window and no distribution, and it implies that throughput cannot exceed 1/S
  - The forced-flow law Xₖ = Vₖ·X: every completed request visits resource k Vₖ times on average, so the completion rate at k is the system throughput X times Vₖ; the law follows from flow balance, since each request enters and leaves the system once and the number of visits to k is fixed by the routing
  - Little's law L = λW, with its sample-path proof: L is the time-average number of requests in the system, λ the arrival rate and W the mean time each spends there, and the law holds for any stable system with no distributional assumption; the sample-path proof integrates the number in system over an observation window in two ways, as the area under the count curve and as the sum of the individual times in system, and divides both by the window length as it grows
  - The bottleneck law: maximum throughput is 1 / max(Vₖ·Sₖ); each resource's demand Dₖ = Vₖ·Sₖ is the total busy time one request places on it, and the resource with the largest demand saturates first, so throughput cannot exceed 1/Dmax however many requests are in flight; adding capacity anywhere except at that bottleneck leaves the ceiling unchanged
  - The interactive response-time law for closed systems: R = N/X − Z; with N users cycling through think time Z and response time R, each cycle takes R + Z, and Little's law applied to the whole loop gives N = X(R + Z), so R = N/X − Z; at light load R approaches the unloaded service demand, and past saturation the bottleneck makes R grow in proportion to N
- **Queueing models**
  - Arrival and service processes: the arrival process gives the times between requests and the service process the time each request needs; Kendall's notation names a model by these choices, so M/M/1 means Poisson arrivals with exponential interarrival times (written M), exponential service and one server, with G for a general distribution and D for deterministic times
  - M/M/1 from its birth–death chain: πₙ = (1 − ρ)ρⁿ, mean number in system ρ/(1 − ρ), mean time 1/(μ − λ)
  - The knee: 2 service times at ρ = 0.5, 10 at 0.9, 100 at 0.99; utilization ceilings of 70–80% in capacity planning
  - M/M/k and the Erlang C formula; why one shared queue for k servers beats k separate queues
  - M/G/1 and the Pollaczek–Khinchine formula, E[W_q] = ρ·E[S]·(1 + C²)/(2(1 − ρ)); why service-time variability costs as much as load
- **Estimation as a method**
  - Fermi estimation: decompose, estimate each factor within a factor of 3, multiply
  - Sensitivity: the widest-ranging factor dominates; estimate it two ways; in a product of uncertain factors, the factor whose plausible range spans the most orders of magnitude sets the spread of the answer, so it deserves the most care; estimate that factor by two independent routes, such as a bottom-up count and a top-down ratio, and trust the answer only when the two agree within the range of the estimate
  - Capacity planning and back-of-the-envelope arithmetic in design work

### Linear algebra

- Vectors, matrices, scalar multiplication, the transpose, dot and cross products, norms; the order m × n of a matrix and its types (square, diagonal, triangular, symmetric, identity); (AB)ᵀ = BᵀAᵀ and the adjoint identity ⟨Ax, y⟩ = ⟨x, Aᵀy⟩ for real matrices; permutation matrices P that reorder the rows of the identity, with PᵀP = I; the zero (null) matrix has every entry 0 and is the additive identity; the product AB is defined only when the number of columns of A equals the number of rows of B, so an m × n matrix times an n × p matrix gives an m × p matrix with (AB)ᵢⱼ = Σₖ aᵢₖbₖⱼ
- Matrix multiplication and its O(n³) naive cost; why it parallelizes; each entry of AB is an independent dot product of a row of A with a column of B, so all n² entries can be computed at once and the work splits cleanly across cores and tiles; blocked algorithms keep the tiles in cache; Strassen's method uses seven half-size products instead of eight to reach about n^2.81, and the best known exponent is lower still in theory, with constant factors too large for practice; matrix multiplication is associative, A(BC) = (AB)C, and distributes over addition, A(B + C) = AB + AC, but it is not commutative in general, AB ≠ BA; block multiplication treats partitioned matrices as if the blocks were scalars, so the product of conforming block matrices is computed by the same formula applied to the blocks
- Vector spaces, span, linear independence, basis and dimension: a vector space is a set closed under addition and scalar multiplication that satisfies the usual axioms; the span of a set is all its linear combinations; vectors are linearly independent when only the trivial combination gives zero; a basis is an independent spanning set, and any two bases of a finite-dimensional space have the same number of vectors, the dimension
- Matrices as linear maps; rank and the rank–nullity theorem; composition of linear maps as the matrix product AB; a linear map fixed by the images of the basis vectors, which are the columns of its matrix; rotation matrices in three dimensions and unit quaternions q = (w, x, y, z) acting by v ↦ qvq̄; the matrix of a linear map relative to a chosen basis, and change of basis, where the same map written in a new basis has matrix P⁻¹AP for the change-of-basis matrix P whose columns are the new basis vectors in the old coordinates; the adjoint T* of a linear map T on an inner-product space is defined by ⟨Tx, y⟩ = ⟨x, T*y⟩ for all x and y, equal to Aᵀ for a real matrix A in an orthonormal basis and Aᴴ for a complex one, and its kernel and range satisfy ker T* = (range T)^⊥ and range T* = (ker T)^⊥, so the four fundamental subspaces come in orthogonal pairs
- Solving Ax = b by Gaussian elimination and LU factorization; Cramer's rule as determinant ratios xᵢ = det(Aᵢ)/det(A), where Aᵢ replaces column i with b, factorial in cost by cofactor expansion; row operations; determinants (ad − bc for 2 × 2) and invertibility; the n × n determinant by cofactor expansion along a row, with the 3 × 3 case a(ei − fh) − b(di − fg) + c(dh − eg) and the sum over permutations; the adjugate with A⁻¹ = adj(A)/det(A); row substitution, replacing one row by itself plus a multiple of another, which leaves the solution set and the determinant unchanged; row echelon form and reduced row echelon form reached by the same row operations, with the pivots counting the rank; forward elimination costing about 2n³/3 flops followed by back substitution, and partial pivoting with the factorization PA = LU for stability; Cholesky factorization of a symmetric positive definite matrix costs about n³/3 flops, half of LU; Householder QR of an m × n matrix with m ≥ n costs about 2mn² − 2n³/3 flops; the SVD costs a constant multiple of n³ whose size depends on whether the singular vectors are wanted; a triangular solve costs n² flops, so once A is factored each further right-hand side costs only n², and solving by factoring and substituting beats forming A⁻¹, which costs more flops and is less accurate; Cholesky factorization writes a symmetric positive definite matrix as A = LLᵀ with L lower triangular and positive diagonal, computed by the recurrence Lⱼⱼ = √(aⱼⱼ − Σₖ<ⱼ Lⱼₖ²) and Lᵢⱼ = (aᵢⱼ − Σₖ<ⱼ LᵢₖLⱼₖ)/Lⱼⱼ for i > j, and a nonpositive value under the square root shows that A is not positive definite, so the factorization also tests positive definiteness; each elimination step is a left multiplication by an elementary matrix, so the steps give EA = U with E = E_k⋯E₁, and the matrix that subtracts ℓ times row j from row i is I − ℓeᵢeⱼᵀ, whose inverse I + ℓeᵢeⱼᵀ adds the multiple back
- Orthogonality, projections, Gram–Schmidt and least squares (the normal equations AᵀAx = Aᵀb); multi-objective least squares, minimizing Σᵢ λᵢ‖Aᵢx − bᵢ‖² for weights λᵢ ≥ 0, which is ordinary least squares on the stacked matrix with rows √λᵢAᵢ, with the trade-off curve traced as the weights vary; signal space, in which finite-energy waveforms are vectors in an orthonormal basis built by Gram–Schmidt on the waveforms, with energy as the squared norm and the distance between two waveforms as the Euclidean distance between their coordinates; Gram–Schmidt orthogonalizes a list a₁, …, a_k by subtracting from each vector its projections onto the earlier orthonormal vectors, uᵢ = aᵢ − Σⱼ<ᵢ (aᵢ·qⱼ)qⱼ, and then normalizing qᵢ = uᵢ/‖uᵢ‖, which gives an orthonormal basis of the same span in O(mk²) operations for vectors in ℝᵐ; the projection of v onto a nonzero u is ((u·v)/(u·u))u, with residual v − proj_u v perpendicular to u, and onto the column space of A with independent columns the projection matrix is P = A(AᵀA)⁻¹Aᵀ
- Eigenvalues and eigenvectors; power iteration; Hotelling deflation A − λ₁v₁v₁ᵀ, which removes a found eigenpair of a symmetric matrix so that power iteration finds the next; spectral methods as the eigen-based family behind PCA and spectral clustering, where the graph Laplacian L = D − W has eigenvectors for its smallest eigenvalues that embed the vertices so that k-means on them separates clusters; power iteration repeats x ← Ax/‖Ax‖, and the eigenvector error shrinks by |λ₂/λ₁| per step, so it converges slowly when the two largest eigenvalues are close in modulus; the QR algorithm is made practical by first reducing A to upper Hessenberg form in O(n³) with Householder reflections, which keeps each QR step at O(n²), and by Wilkinson shifts, which give cubic local convergence for symmetric matrices
- The singular value decomposition, principal component analysis and low-rank approximation; every m × n real matrix factors as A = UΣVᵀ with orthonormal columns in U and V and a nonnegative diagonal Σ whose entries σᵢ are the singular values, the square roots of the eigenvalues of AᵀA; the top singular triplet is found by power iteration on AᵀA, v ← AᵀAv/‖AᵀAv‖ with σ₁ = ‖Av‖ and u₁ = Av/σ₁, and later triplets by deflation A ← A − σ₁u₁v₁ᵀ; PCA is the SVD of the centred data matrix, whose right singular vectors are the principal directions
- Distance and similarity: Euclidean, Manhattan, cosine; the metric axioms (non-negativity, identity of indiscernibles, symmetry and the triangle inequality); the Minkowski distance (Σᵢ|xᵢ − yᵢ|ᵖ)^(1/p) for p ≥ 1, with p = 2 giving Euclidean, p = 1 Manhattan, and the limit p → ∞ giving the Chebyshev distance, the largest coordinate difference; a norm inducing the metric d(x, y) = ‖x − y‖, so the norm axioms (positive definiteness, absolute homogeneity, triangle inequality) imply the metric axioms; cosine similarity x·y ÷ (‖x‖‖y‖) and the angular distance arccos(cos θ), which is a metric, whereas 1 − cos θ fails the triangle inequality; on unit vectors ‖x − y‖² = 2(1 − cos θ), so the Euclidean and cosine rankings agree; the O(d) cost per pair, and how each measure responds to scaling and translation; Euclidean and Manhattan distances scale linearly, d(cx, cy) = |c|·d(x, y), and are unchanged by translation, d(x + t, y + t) = d(x, y), whereas cosine similarity is unchanged by positive scaling of either vector but not by translation, since subtracting a common vector changes the angle between x and y, so cosine measures direction only and is not translation invariant
- Column space and null space; the Moore–Penrose pseudo-inverse for over- and under-determined systems; hyperplanes and the distance of a point to a hyperplane; the four fundamental subspaces (column space, null space, row space and left null space) with dimensions r, n − r, r and m − r for a rank-r matrix of size m × n; two subspaces are orthogonal when every vector of one is perpendicular to every vector of the other, as the row space and null space are, and the residual b − Pb of the projection onto the column space is perpendicular to every column of A, so that Aᵀ(b − Pb) = 0
- **Symmetric and skew-symmetric matrices**: real symmetric matrices have real eigenvalues and orthogonal eigenvectors by the spectral theorem; skew-symmetric matrices have purely imaginary eigenvalues; every square matrix splits uniquely into ½(A + Aᵀ) plus ½(A − Aᵀ); the spectral theorem, A = QΛQᵀ = Σᵢ λᵢqᵢqᵢᵀ with orthonormal eigenvectors qᵢ, so that a symmetric matrix is a sum of rank-one projections weighted by its eigenvalues, and a self-adjoint operator on a finite-dimensional inner product space is diagonalized by an orthonormal eigenbasis in the same way
- **Positive definite matrices and minima**: xᵀAx > 0 for every nonzero x, equivalently positive eigenvalues, positive pivots or a Cholesky factorization; the quadratic ½xᵀAx − bᵀx minimized where Ax = b; a positive definite Hessian as the second-order test for a strict minimum
- **Complex matrices and similarity transformations**: the conjugate transpose, Hermitian and unitary matrices; similar matrices B = P⁻¹AP share eigenvalues and the characteristic polynomial; diagonalizability as similarity to a diagonal matrix; powers A^k = P D^k P⁻¹ computed from the diagonal form D = P⁻¹AP; complex vectors with the Hermitian inner product ⟨x, y⟩ = x*y = Σ x̄ᵢyᵢ and norm ‖x‖ = √(x*x), and the spectral theorem for Hermitian matrices, with real eigenvalues and orthonormal eigenvectors, so that A = UΛU* for a unitary U
- **Characterization of commutators**: a square matrix over a field is a commutator AB − BA exactly when its trace is zero; necessity holds because tr(AB) = tr(BA), and sufficiency is the Albert–Muckenhoupt theorem that every trace-zero matrix is a commutator; the identity I is therefore not a commutator over ℂ, since its trace is n ≠ 0
- **Jordan form and Lyapunov stability**: Jordan blocks as the canonical form of defective matrices, built from generalized eigenvectors; the system x′ = Ax is asymptotically stable exactly when every eigenvalue has negative real part, and the Lyapunov equation AᵀP + PA = −Q certifies it; the matrix exponential e^(At) = Σ (At)^k / k! as the solution of x′ = Ax, computed from the Jordan form
- **QR factorization and the QR algorithm**: A = QR with orthogonal Q and upper-triangular R, computed by Gram–Schmidt, Householder reflections or Givens rotations; least squares by solving Rx = Qᵀb without forming AᵀA; the QR algorithm iterating QR factorizations, with shifts, to reveal eigenvalues; a Householder reflection H = I − 2vvᵀ/(vᵀv) is symmetric and orthogonal and reflects across the hyperplane perpendicular to v, and with v = x + sign(x₁)‖x‖e₁ it maps a column x to a multiple of e₁, zeroing every entry below the first; applying one reflection per column gives Householder QR at about 2mn² − 2n³/3 flops
- **Sparse and structured matrix problems**: compressed storage of nonzeros; fill-in during elimination and reordering to limit it; banded, triangular, Toeplitz and circulant structure giving fast solves; iterative methods preferred for large sparse systems
- **Multilinear forms and complexification**: a k-linear form is linear in each argument separately, with bilinear forms as matrices, B(x, y) = xᵀMy, and alternating multilinear forms such as the determinant, which as a function of the n columns of an n × n matrix is the alternating n-linear form, unique up to a scalar; the complexification V_ℂ = V ⊗ ℂ of a real vector space, over which a real matrix's eigenvalues are taken, with complex conjugation of coordinates making the complex eigenvalues of a real matrix come in conjugate pairs; a tensor of order k is a multilinear object, in coordinates an array indexed by k indices; the Einstein summation convention drops the summation sign for each index repeated in a product, so a matrix product is written cᵢₖ = aᵢⱼbⱼₖ; contraction sums a tensor over a pair of indices, and matrix multiplication and the trace are contractions; covariant and contravariant components transform in opposite ways under a change of basis, which is why the position of an index records its kind; in machine learning a tensor is a multi-dimensional array, and einsum is a library form of index contraction
- **Matrix norms**: the Frobenius norm ‖A‖_F = √(Σᵢⱼ aᵢⱼ²) = √(trace(AᵀA)); induced norms ‖A‖ = max over ‖x‖ = 1 of ‖Ax‖, with ‖A‖₁ the maximum absolute column sum and ‖A‖∞ the maximum absolute row sum; the spectral norm ‖A‖₂ equal to the largest singular value; the nuclear norm as the sum of singular values; the Eckart–Young theorem that the truncated SVD is the best rank-k approximation in both the spectral and the Frobenius norm
- **Matrix functions**: f(A) defined through the spectrum, f(A) = P f(D) P⁻¹ for diagonalizable A, and for a Jordan block J = λI + N with N nilpotent, f(J) = Σₖ f⁽ᵏ⁾(λ)Nᵏ/k!, so that the Jordan form extends any sufficiently smooth scalar function to a matrix; the matrix square root and logarithm and their non-uniqueness when several eigenvalues share a branch; the Cauchy integral f(A) = (1/2πi)∮ f(z)(zI − A)⁻¹ dz over a contour enclosing the spectrum; the matrix exponential as the case f(z) = eᶻ; a spectral function F(X) = Σᵢ f(λᵢ(X)) of a symmetric matrix, with f convex, is convex, and its proximal operator is computed on the spectrum, prox_F(X) = Q diag(prox_f(λ₁), …, prox_f(λₙ)) Qᵀ for X = Q diag(λ) Qᵀ, so with f = |·| the proximal step is eigenvalue soft-thresholding

### Calculus and optimization

- **Single-variable calculus**
  - Limits and continuity; the derivative as a limit; f is continuous at a when the limit of f(x) as x approaches a equals f(a), so small changes in input give small changes in output; the derivative at a is f′(a) = the limit as h → 0 of (f(a + h) − f(a))/h, the slope of the tangent, and differentiability at a implies continuity there but not conversely
  - Product, quotient and chain rules: (fg)′ = f′g + fg′, (f/g)′ = (f′g − fg′)/g² where g ≠ 0, and (f ∘ g)′(x) = f′(g(x))·g′(x); each follows from the limit definition by adding and subtracting a cross term, and the chain rule is the one that backpropagation applies layer by layer
  - The first-order Taylor approximation, and why a small gradient step decreases a smooth function
  - The integral as accumulated area; probability densities; the definite integral of f from a to b is the signed area between f and the axis, and the fundamental theorem of calculus, which states that the derivative of the integral from a to x of f(t) dt equals f(x), makes differentiation and integration inverse operations; a probability density is nonnegative with total integral 1, and an event's probability is the area under the density over that event
  - First-order differential equations; Euler and Runge–Kutta (RK4) methods; multistep methods such as Adams–Bashforth, which reuse past slopes and need one new function evaluation per step, with implicit backward differentiation formulas for stiff systems; the local truncation error and the global error one order lower; the forward-time centred-space scheme for the heat equation u_t = αu_xx, stable when r = αΔt/Δx² ≤ 1/2, and the CFL bound on the time step for advection; the Crank–Nicolson scheme for the heat equation, the average of the explicit and implicit schemes, second-order accurate in time and unconditionally stable, solved by a tridiagonal system at each step; von Neumann stability analysis, which substitutes a Fourier mode e^(ikx) and requires the amplification factor to satisfy |G| ≤ 1 for every wavenumber; elliptic problems such as Poisson's equation ∇²u = f are discretized by the five-point stencil (uᵢ₊₁,ⱼ + uᵢ₋₁,ⱼ + uᵢ,ⱼ₊₁ + uᵢ,ⱼ₋₁ − 4uᵢⱼ)/h² = fᵢⱼ and solved as a sparse linear system, with Neumann conditions ∂u/∂n = g imposed by ghost-point elimination, mixed conditions by combining the Dirichlet and Neumann forms, and irregular boundaries by a finite-volume or cut-cell treatment
- **Multivariable and matrix calculus**
  - Partial derivatives and the gradient: the partial derivative with respect to one variable differentiates while holding the others fixed; the gradient collects them into a vector that points in the direction of steepest increase, with length equal to that rate, so a small step in the direction of −∇f decreases a smooth function locally
  - Gradients of vector functions, Jacobians, the matrix chain rule; the inverse function theorem, under which a C¹ map with invertible Jacobian at a point is locally invertible there, with the derivative of the inverse equal to the inverse of the Jacobian; the implicit function theorem, under which F(x, y) = 0 defines y as a C¹ function of x near a point where ∂F/∂y is invertible, with dy/dx = −(∂F/∂y)⁻¹(∂F/∂x)
  - The Hessian: the matrix of second partial derivatives Hᵢⱼ = ∂²f/∂xᵢ∂xⱼ, symmetric when those derivatives are continuous (Schwarz's theorem); at a critical point a positive definite Hessian gives a strict local minimum, a negative definite one a local maximum, and an indefinite one a saddle point
  - Vector calculus: divergence ∇·F = Σᵢ ∂Fᵢ/∂xᵢ, measuring local source strength; curl ∇ × F in three dimensions, measuring local rotation; ∇ × ∇f = 0 and ∇ · (∇ × F) = 0; the divergence theorem and Stokes' theorem as the integral forms of these local statements
- **Optimization**
  - Convex sets and functions; every local minimum of a convex function is global; the separating hyperplane theorem, under which disjoint convex sets are split by a hyperplane aᵀx ≤ b on one side and aᵀx ≥ b on the other; the dual cone K* = {y : yᵀx ≥ 0 for all x in K}; quasiconvex functions, whose sublevel sets are convex; the conjugate f*(y) = sup over x of (yᵀx − f(x)); convexity and concavity, with a concave function defined as one whose negative is convex; the first-order characterization f(y) ≥ f(x) + ∇f(x)ᵀ(y − x) for differentiable convex f and the second-order test that the Hessian is positive semidefinite; Jensen's inequality f(E X) ≤ E f(X) for convex f; the operations that preserve convexity of sets are intersection, affine images and preimages, scaling, and sums of sets, and those that preserve convexity of functions are nonnegative weighted sums, composition with an affine map and pointwise maximum (the supremum of a family of convex functions is convex); the supporting hyperplane theorem says that at each boundary point x₀ of a convex set C there is a nonzero a with aᵀx ≤ aᵀx₀ for all x in C; a log-concave function has a concave logarithm, as the Gaussian density does, and a log-convex function has a convex logarithm
  - Extended real-valued functions: f : ℝⁿ → (−∞, +∞] with effective domain dom f = {x : f(x) < +∞}; f is proper when it never takes the value −∞ and is not identically +∞ (dom f nonempty), and the indicator function of a set C, equal to 0 on C and +∞ off C, writes a constraint as a function, so a constrained problem becomes the unconstrained minimization of f plus an indicator; conjugates and subgradients are defined for such functions, with the conjugate f*(y) = sup over x of (yᵀx − f(x)) computed over dom f
  - Gradient descent and its convergence rates; stochastic gradient descent; the L-smoothness inequality ‖∇f(x) − ∇f(y)‖ ≤ L‖x − y‖ and the μ-strong convexity, under which gradient descent with step 1/L contracts the gap by a factor (1 − μ/L) per step, f(x_k) − f* ≤ (1 − μ/L)^k (f(x₀) − f*), while convex L-smooth functions give the O(1/k) rate; steepest descent as gradient descent with the step chosen by exact line search, whose iterates zig-zag between orthogonal directions on an ill-conditioned quadratic, each step shrinking the A-norm error by (κ − 1)/(κ + 1) with κ the condition number; the update itself is x_(k+1) = x_k − η∇f(x_k), a step of size η against the gradient, and with η = 1/L it gives the contraction above
  - Momentum and adaptive optimizers: momentum keeps an exponentially weighted average of past gradients, with v ← βv + ∇f(x) and x ← x − ηv, so consistent directions accumulate speed while oscillating components cancel; adaptive methods scale each coordinate's step by a running estimate of its gradient size, as AdaGrad does with the sum of squared gradients, RMSProp with a decaying average and Adam with decaying averages of the gradient and its square, corrected for their start-up bias
  - Regularization (L1, L2); coordinate descent; ridge regression β̂ = (XᵀX + λI)⁻¹Xᵀy, which shrinks each coefficient along the principal directions of X by the factor d²/(d² + λ) for singular value d, and the lasso's L1 penalty, which sets some coefficients exactly to zero and so selects variables; dropout as random masking of units with keep probability p during training, scaled by 1/p so that test-time activations need no change, a cheap average over an ensemble of thinned networks; high-dimensional problems, with p ≫ n, make ordinary least squares singular because XᵀX has rank at most n < p, so ridge regression with λ > 0 restores a unique solution, and under sparsity (few nonzero coefficients) the lasso recovers the support when the design satisfies a condition such as the restricted eigenvalue condition and n grows like s log p for s nonzero coefficients; implicit regularization, in which no penalty appears but the optimizer still selects a solution, so that gradient descent started from zero on an underdetermined least-squares problem converges to the minimum-ℓ2-norm interpolant, and early stopping after t steps acts approximately like ridge regression with λ ≈ 1/(ηt) for step size η
  - Constrained optimization: Lagrange multipliers and KKT conditions; the Lagrangian dual function g(λ) = inf over x of L(x, λ) for λ ≥ 0, weak duality g(λ) ≤ f(x*), and strong duality under Slater's condition; the KKT conditions for minimizing f(x) subject to gᵢ(x) ≤ 0 and hⱼ(x) = 0: stationarity ∇f + Σᵢ λᵢ∇gᵢ + Σⱼ νⱼ∇hⱼ = 0, primal feasibility, dual feasibility λᵢ ≥ 0, and complementary slackness λᵢgᵢ(x) = 0; necessary at a local minimum under a constraint qualification such as Slater's, and sufficient for convex problems
  - Penalty methods and the augmented Lagrangian: for equality constraints h(x) = 0, the quadratic penalty minimizes f(x) + (μ/2)‖h(x)‖² for increasing μ, which drives the violation to zero only as μ → ∞ and makes the subproblems increasingly ill-conditioned; the augmented Lagrangian L_ρ(x, λ) = f(x) + λᵀh(x) + (ρ/2)‖h(x)‖² keeps ρ fixed and updates the multiplier by λ ← λ + ρh(x), so the penalty need not grow without bound; sequential quadratic programming solves at each iterate a quadratic program with the Hessian of the Lagrangian (or a BFGS approximation of it) and the linearized constraints, with a line search or trust region on a merit function, and converges superlinearly near a solution that satisfies the second-order sufficiency conditions
  - Linear programming; the assignment problem and the Hungarian method; quadratic programming, minimizing ½xᵀQx + cᵀx subject to linear constraints with Q positive semidefinite; geometric programming with posynomial objectives, which become convex under the substitution x = e^y; the simplex method, which moves between adjacent vertices of the feasible polyhedron by pivots that exchange one basic variable for another and improve the objective, stopping when every reduced cost is nonnegative, exponential in the worst case (Klee–Minty) yet fast in practice; quadratic programming solved through its KKT system, which is linear in x and the multipliers when only equalities are imposed, with active-set and interior-point methods for inequality constraints; a linear program min cᵀx subject to Ax = b, x ≥ 0 has a feasible region that is a polyhedron, and if its objective is bounded below an optimum is attained at a vertex, a basic feasible solution, which is the fact the simplex method exploits; its dual, max bᵀy subject to Aᵀy ≤ c, satisfies weak duality, cᵀx ≥ bᵀy for every pair of feasible points, and when both problems are feasible their optimal values are equal (strong duality); the Hungarian method solves the min-cost assignment in O(n³) by subtracting row and column minima to create zeros, covering the zeros with the fewest lines, and, when fewer than n lines are needed, subtracting the smallest uncovered entry from the uncovered rows and adding it to the doubly covered columns, repeating until an assignment of zeros exists
  - Zero-sum matrix games: a payoff matrix A for the row player, with the column player receiving −A; a mixed strategy is a probability vector on the rows, and the row player's security level is max over x of min over y of xᵀAy; von Neumann's minimax theorem gives max over x of min over y of xᵀAy = min over y of max over x of xᵀAy, the value of the game, and each player's optimal mixed strategy is found by a linear program, max v subject to Aᵀx ≥ v·1 with x on the probability simplex, whose dual is the column player's program
  - Generalization versus optimization; approximation error; the excess risk of a learned model splits into approximation error (the best model in the class is still far from the target), optimization error (training stops short of the best model in the class) and generalization error (the gap between training and test loss); more optimization shrinks only the middle term, so a lower training loss does not by itself mean better test performance; a real-data experiment fixes its protocol before the run: split the data into training, validation and test sets and touch the test set once, compare against a simple baseline and a naive predictor, repeat over several random seeds or folds and report the spread beside the mean, and report the hyperparameters chosen and the number of runs so that selection does not inflate the comparison
  - Generalized inequalities and proper cones: a proper cone K is closed, convex, pointed and has nonempty interior, and x ⪯_K y means y − x ∈ K; the nonnegative orthant gives the componentwise order and the positive semidefinite cone gives the Loewner order, A ⪯ B when B − A is positive semidefinite; the dual cone K* = {y : ⟨y, x⟩ ≥ 0 for all x in K}, which equals K for the orthant and for the positive semidefinite cone; vector optimization with respect to K, whose solutions are the minimal elements of the order, the Pareto-optimal points
- **Newton's method**: the step x ← x − H⁻¹∇f(x) from the second-order Taylor model; quadratic convergence near a minimum with a positive definite Hessian; O(n³) per step to solve the Hessian system; quasi-Newton updates (BFGS) that build the Hessian from gradient differences; with equality constraints Ax = b, the Newton step from the KKT block system whose matrix has H and Aᵀ in the first block row and A and 0 in the second; the secant condition B_(k+1)s_k = y_k with s_k = x_(k+1) − x_k and y_k = ∇f(x_(k+1)) − ∇f(x_k); the BFGS update B_(k+1) = B_k − (B_k s_k s_kᵀ B_k)/(s_kᵀ B_k s_k) + (y_k y_kᵀ)/(y_kᵀ s_k), which keeps B positive definite when y_kᵀ s_k > 0 and gives superlinear local convergence; limited-memory L-BFGS, storing the last m pairs (s, y) in O(mn) memory
- **Line search and trust regions**: Armijo's sufficient-decrease condition f(x + αd) ≤ f(x) + c₁α∇f(x)ᵀd, found by backtracking; the Wolfe conditions adding the curvature condition ∇f(x + αd)ᵀd ≥ c₂∇f(x)ᵀd, with the strong Wolfe form required by quasi-Newton updates; trust-region methods minimizing the quadratic model m_k(p) = f(x_k) + ∇f(x_k)ᵀp + ½pᵀB_k p over a ball ‖p‖ ≤ Δ_k, comparing the actual with the predicted reduction ρ_k = (f(x_k) − f(x_k + p_k))/(m_k(0) − m_k(p_k)) to accept the step and to grow or shrink Δ_k; the Cauchy point, the minimizer of the model along steepest descent within the ball, which guarantees convergence to a stationary point
- **Conjugate gradient**: solving Ax = b for symmetric positive definite A with A-conjugate search directions in a Krylov subspace; termination in at most n steps in exact arithmetic; convergence governed by the square root of the condition number; preconditioning; the Krylov subspace K_k(A, b) = span{b, Ab, …, A^(k−1)b}, from which the k-th iterate is drawn and in which it minimizes the error in the A-norm; the Krylov methods built on the same subspaces: Lanczos iteration, which builds an orthonormal basis of K_k(A, b) for symmetric A with a tridiagonal projection, MINRES for symmetric indefinite systems, and GMRES, which for nonsymmetric A minimizes the residual over the Krylov subspace at each step using Arnoldi orthogonalization
- **Proximal methods**: minimizing f(x) + g(x) with a nonsmooth g through the proximal operator prox_g(v) = argmin over x of g(x) + ½‖x − v‖²; proximal gradient iterations; soft-thresholding as the proximal operator of the L1 norm
- **Block proximal gradient**: for a smooth f plus a separable nonsmooth term g(x) = Σⱼ gⱼ(x_j) over blocks of coordinates, each block update is x_j ← prox_(tⱼgⱼ)(x_j − tⱼ∇_j f(x)) with its own step tⱼ ≤ 1/Lⱼ, where Lⱼ is the Lipschitz constant of the block gradient; with these steps the objective does not increase, and the block method is the proximal form of block coordinate descent
- **Subgradient methods**: for convex f, a subgradient g at x satisfies f(y) ≥ f(x) + gᵀ(y − x) for all y, and the subdifferential ∂f(x) is the set of all subgradients at x; the subgradient method x_(k+1) = x_k − α_k g_k with diminishing step sizes, whose best iterate converges at rate O(1/√k); the optimality condition 0 ∈ ∂f(x*); subgradients of the absolute value, which are the interval [−1, 1] at zero, and of a maximum of affine functions
- **Projected subgradient method**: x_(k+1) = P_C(x_k − α_k g_k) for a closed convex set C, where P_C is the Euclidean projection onto C, with diminishing steps and bounded iterates giving the same O(1/√k) rate as the unconstrained subgradient method; projected gradient is the smooth case, with a gradient step followed by the projection
- **Mirror descent**: the update x_(k+1) = argmin over x of η⟨g_k, x⟩ + D_ψ(x, x_k), with a Bregman divergence D_ψ generated by a strictly convex ψ; the Euclidean choice recovering subgradient descent, and the negative-entropy choice giving multiplicative weights on the probability simplex, whose dimension dependence is only logarithmic; follow-the-regularized-leader chooses x_(t+1) = argmin over x of ⟨Σₛ≤ₜ gₛ, x⟩ + R(x)/η with a strongly convex regularizer R, the dual form of mirror descent for online linear losses, with regret O(√T) for bounded losses and a bounded domain
- **ADMM (alternating direction method of multipliers)**: for min f(x) + g(z) subject to Ax + Bz = c, the augmented Lagrangian L_ρ = f(x) + g(z) + yᵀ(Ax + Bz − c) + (ρ/2)‖Ax + Bz − c‖²; the iterations x ← argmin over x of L_ρ, z ← argmin over z of L_ρ, then y ← y + ρ(Ax + Bz − c); convergence under convexity for any ρ > 0; used for distributed consensus, where each agent solves a local subproblem and the shared variable is an average
- **Dual-based proximal gradient**: for min f(x) + g(Ax) the Fenchel dual is min over y of f*(−Aᵀy) + g*(y), obtained from the Lagrangian f(x) + yᵀAx − g*(y) by minimizing over x first; when f is strongly convex, f* is smooth, so proximal gradient applies to the dual with the proximal step of g*, and the primal solution is recovered as x = ∇f*(−Aᵀy)
- **Robust and stochastic formulations**: the min–max robust problem min over x of the maximum over an uncertainty set of f(x, u); the stochastic program min over x of E[f(x, ξ)] solved by sample average approximation; chance constraints; the trade-off between conservatism and expected performance
- **Non-convex diagnostics**: the gradient norm and the Hessian's eigenvalues at a stationary point to separate local minima, saddle points and maxima; restarts and sensitivity to initialization as the test of a local answer; a local minimum is not certified global
- **Weight initialization**: the variance of layer activations is kept roughly constant from layer to layer when the weight variance matches the fan-in and fan-out, Glorot (Xavier) initialization with variance 2/(n_in + n_out) for tanh-like units and He initialization with variance 2/n_in for ReLU units; zero initialization fails by symmetry, because every unit in a layer then receives the same gradient; restarts from different initializations as the empirical check for a local answer
- **Derivative-free and least-squares methods**: derivative-free optimization by model-based or pattern search (the Nelder–Mead simplex, coordinate pattern search) when gradients are unavailable or unreliable; nonlinear least squares min ½Σᵢ rᵢ(x)² with the Gauss–Newton step (JᵀJ)p = −Jᵀr, which drops the second-order term, and Levenberg–Marquardt damping (JᵀJ + μI)p = −Jᵀr, which moves between Gauss–Newton for small μ and gradient descent for large μ; Frank–Wolfe (conditional gradient) steps x_(k+1) = (1 − γ)x_k + γs_k, with s_k the minimizer of the linear function ∇f(x_k)ᵀs over the constraint set, which keeps iterates feasible without a projection and converges at rate O(1/k) for smooth convex f; alternating minimization over blocks of variables, each block solved while the others are held fixed, with the objective decreasing at every sweep; this is block coordinate descent, and alternating least squares is the case in which each block is an ordinary least-squares problem, as in matrix factorization; the objective never increases, and when each block has a unique minimizer every limit point is a stationary point of a smooth objective, though a nonconvex problem can stall at a saddle point or a poor stationary point; expectation–maximization is an instance: the E step computes the posterior over latent variables with the parameters held fixed, the M step maximizes the expected complete-data log-likelihood over the parameters with the posterior held fixed, and the likelihood never decreases
- **Interior-point methods**: inequality constraints handled by the log barrier −(1/t)Σ log(−fᵢ(x)); Newton steps on each centering problem as t grows along the central path; polynomial-time bounds for linear and self-concordant convex programs
- **Geometric problems (minimum-volume ellipsoids)**: the smallest-volume ellipsoid enclosing a point set as a convex problem in the ellipsoid's matrix and centre, with the negative log-determinant as the objective; the Löwner–John ellipsoid as its theory; the maximum-volume ellipsoid inside a polyhedron {x : aᵢᵀx ≤ bᵢ} is the ellipsoid {Bu + d : ‖u‖ ≤ 1} that maximizes log det B subject to ‖Baᵢ‖ + aᵢᵀd ≤ bᵢ, which is convex in (B, d); linear classification as an LP feasibility problem, aᵢᵀx + b ≥ 1 for one class and ≤ −1 for the other, and placement as minimizing a sum of distances to fixed points, a convex problem (the Fermat–Weber problem)
- **Pontryagin maximum principle**: a necessary condition for optimal control; the Hamiltonian H = p·f + L with costate p obeying the adjoint equation, and H maximized pointwise over admissible controls; bang-bang controls in time-optimal problems
- **Optimization of functionals (calculus of variations)**: minimizing a functional J[y] = ∫ₐᵇ L(x, y, y′) dx over curves with fixed endpoints; the first variation, whose vanishing for every admissible perturbation gives the Euler–Lagrange equation ∂L/∂y − d/dx(∂L/∂y′) = 0; geodesics and the brachistochrone as worked examples; the Legendre condition ∂²L/∂y′² ≥ 0 as a second-order necessary condition for a minimum; natural boundary conditions when an endpoint is free; isoperimetric constraints handled by a Lagrange multiplier on the constraint functional ∫G = constant

### Numerical computing

- Floating point: IEEE 754, rounding, decimal versus binary; fixed-point formats as an integer with an implied binary point, Qm.n with range and resolution 2⁻ⁿ, where overflow saturates or wraps; the IEEE 754 binary32 and binary64 formats with a sign, a biased exponent and a significand, with machine epsilon 2⁻⁵² ≈ 2.2 × 10⁻¹⁶ for binary64; computed values are compared with a tolerance, |a − b| ≤ atol + rtol·max(|a|, |b|) (the isclose rule), since exact equality fails after rounding, and NaN is the only value unequal to itself, so x ≠ x (or an isnan test) detects it, while ±inf is detected by an isinf test
- Catastrophic cancellation; compensated (Kahan) summation; subtracting two nearly equal floating-point numbers cancels their leading digits, so the relative error of the result can be huge even when each input was accurate, as in (1 + x) − 1 for tiny x, which returns 0; Kahan summation carries the rounding error of each addition in a separate compensation variable, bounding the total error by about 2ε Σ|xᵢ| to first order and independent of the number of terms, where ε is the unit roundoff
- Overflow and underflow; stable reformulations: `log1p`, log-sum-exp, stable softmax; log-sum-exp is computed as log Σᵢ e^(xᵢ) = m + log Σᵢ e^(xᵢ − m) with m = maxᵢ xᵢ, so every exponent is at most 0, and stable softmax uses the same shift, softmaxᵢ = e^(xᵢ − m)/Σⱼ e^(xⱼ − m), which leaves the result unchanged because the factor e^(−m) cancels; log1p(x) computes log(1 + x) accurately for small x, where 1 + x would round
- Conditioning and the stability of algorithms; iterative solvers; the condition number κ(A) = ‖A‖·‖A⁻¹‖ built from induced matrix norms, with the spectral norm ‖A‖₂ equal to the largest singular value, bounding how a relative change in b is amplified into a relative change in x; backward stability, an algorithm being backward stable when its computed result is the exact result for a slightly perturbed input, with relative perturbation of order machine epsilon, so that the forward error is bounded by about κ(A) times that perturbation; Gaussian elimination with partial pivoting is stable in practice, its backward error bounded by a growth factor times machine epsilon, and Householder QR is backward stable
- Numerical differentiation (finite differences, gradient checking) and integration; quadrature rules: the trapezoidal rule with error −(h²/12)(b − a)f″ for a smooth integrand, Simpson's rule with error of order h⁴, Gauss–Legendre quadrature with n nodes exact for polynomials of degree up to 2n − 1, and adaptive quadrature that subdivides where a local error estimate is large; the forward difference (f(x + h) − f(x))/h has error O(h), the central difference (f(x + h) − f(x − h))/(2h) has error O(h²), and the second derivative (f(x + h) − 2f(x) + f(x − h))/h² has error O(h²); round-off error from subtracting nearly equal values grows like ε|f|/h, so the total error is smallest near h ≈ √ε for forward differences and h ≈ ε^(1/3) for central ones, the truncation-versus-round-off trade-off that gradient checks must respect
- Interpolation and curve fitting; error propagation; ℓ1 and Chebyshev (minimax) fitting, where the ℓ1 fit is a linear program and the minimax fit equioscillates between its extreme errors; root finding by bisection on a sign change and by Newton's iteration x_{k+1} = x_k − f(x_k)/f′(x_k), which converges quadratically to a simple root; the secant method x_(k+1) = x_k − f(x_k)(x_k − x_(k−1))/(f(x_k) − f(x_(k−1))), which needs no derivative and converges superlinearly with order (1 + √5)/2; bisection halving the bracket each step, so that reaching tolerance ε from an initial width w takes about log₂(w/ε) steps with the root always bracketed; Brent's method combining bisection with secant-type steps
- Interpolation: the Lagrange form p(x) = Σᵢ yᵢLᵢ(x), with Lᵢ(x) = ∏_{j≠i}(x − xⱼ)/(xᵢ − xⱼ), is the unique polynomial of degree at most n through n + 1 points with distinct nodes; the Newton divided-difference form gives the same polynomial and absorbs a new point at O(n) cost; the interpolation error is f(x) − p(x) = f⁽ⁿ⁺¹⁾(ξ)/(n + 1)! · ∏ⱼ(x − xⱼ) for some ξ in the interval, so equispaced nodes can oscillate near the ends (Runge's phenomenon) and Chebyshev nodes limit the error; linear interpolation between samples at spacing h has error at most (h²/8)·max|f″|; a clamped cubic spline is a C² piecewise cubic through the samples whose second derivatives solve a tridiagonal system in O(n) time, with error O(h⁴)
- Error propagation: for Y = f(X₁, …, Xₙ) with small errors, the first-order Taylor expansion gives Var(Y) ≈ Σᵢ (∂f/∂xᵢ)²·Var(Xᵢ) for independent inputs, and correlated inputs add the cross terms 2Σ_(i<j) (∂f/∂xᵢ)(∂f/∂xⱼ)·Cov(Xᵢ, Xⱼ); the modelling steps are to write the output as a function of the measured inputs, evaluate the partial derivatives at the nominal values, combine the input variances as above, and check that the linearization holds, which needs small relative errors; for one input the rule is Var(f(X)) ≈ f′(μ)²·σ²
- Approximation and fitting: norm approximation minimizes ‖Ax − b‖ in a chosen norm, with the least-squares (ℓ2), ℓ1 and Chebyshev (ℓ∞) cases; regularized approximation adds a penalty on x, minimizing ‖Ax − b‖ + γ‖x‖, which trades fit against the size of x and reduces to ridge regression when both terms are squared; robust approximation minimizes the worst case over an uncertainty set for A, min over x of max over ‖ΔA‖₂ ≤ ρ of ‖(A + ΔA)x − b‖, which equals ‖Ax − b‖ + ρ‖x‖, so robust least squares is regularized least squares
- Reproducibility: seeding pseudo-random generators, deterministic tests; a repository layout that separates raw data, code, configuration and generated results, with pinned dependency lock files, so that a clean checkout regenerates every reported number
- **Projection methods and successive approximation**: fixed-point iteration x_{k+1} = T(x_k) and the contraction mapping theorem, with geometric convergence at the rate of the contraction constant; alternating projections onto convex sets; stopping on residuals rather than on iteration count; the Euclidean projection onto a closed convex set C, P_C(x) = argmin over y in C of ‖y − x‖², is unique and nonexpansive, so projection onto a box, a ball or a halfspace has a closed form; the Chebyshev centre of a polyhedron {x : aᵢᵀx ≤ bᵢ} is the centre of its largest inscribed ball, found from the linear program that maximizes r subject to aᵢᵀx + r‖aᵢ‖ ≤ bᵢ

### Information theory

- Entropy H(X) = −Σ p·log₂ p: the expected surprise −log₂ p(x) of an outcome, measured in bits: H is 0 for a certain outcome and reaches log₂ n for a uniform distribution over n outcomes; by Shannon's source coding theorem it is the lower bound on the mean code length of any lossless code for the source; Shannon's uniqueness theorem says that H is, up to a positive constant, the only function of a finite distribution that is continuous, increases with n for uniform distributions, and satisfies the grouping rule H(p₁, …, pₙ) = H(p₁ + p₂, p₃, …, pₙ) + (p₁ + p₂)·H(p₁/(p₁ + p₂), p₂/(p₁ + p₂))
- Cross-entropy, KL divergence and mutual information; Gibbs' inequality D(p‖q) ≥ 0 with equality only when p = q, and the data-processing inequality, under which a Markov chain X → Y → Z satisfies I(X; Z) ≤ I(X; Y); the chain rules H(X, Y) = H(X) + H(Y | X) and I(X; Y) = H(X) − H(X | Y); conditioning does not increase entropy, H(X | Y) ≤ H(X); the log-sum inequality; and Fano's inequality H(X | Y) ≤ H(P_e) + P_e log(|𝒳| − 1) for any estimator of X from Y with error probability P_e; Gibbs' inequality follows from Jensen's inequality applied to the convex function −log, since D(p‖q) = −Σ p log(q/p) ≥ −log Σ_(p>0) q ≥ 0, and the data-processing inequality follows from the chain rule, since I(X; Y, Z) = I(X; Y) + I(X; Z | Y) = I(X; Z) + I(X; Y | Z) and I(X; Z | Y) = 0 under X → Y → Z, so I(X; Z) ≤ I(X; Y)
- Log loss, perplexity and calibration; calibration as the match between predicted probability and observed frequency, checked with reliability diagrams over bins and the expected calibration error ECE = Σ_b (n_b/n)|acc_b − conf_b|; the Brier score (1/n)Σᵢ(pᵢ − yᵢ)² as a proper scoring rule; temperature scaling, which fits one scalar on held-out data to recalibrate a softmax
- Shannon's source-coding bound; Huffman codes within one bit per symbol of it; the Kraft inequality Σ 2^(−ℓᵢ) ≤ 1 as the condition on codeword lengths ℓᵢ for a prefix code to exist; arithmetic coding, which represents a whole message as one point in a nested sequence of intervals narrowed by each symbol's probability, with a code length within about 2 bits of nH for a message of n symbols, so that it avoids the up-to-one-bit-per-symbol loss of Huffman codes; Huffman's construction repeatedly merges the two least-probable symbols into one node whose probability is their sum, until a single tree remains, and reads each codeword off its root-to-leaf path, giving an optimal prefix code whose expected length lies within one bit of H, built in O(n log n) time with a heap
- Why compressed or well-encrypted data looks uniformly random: a compressor removes the redundancy it can model, so its output carries close to one bit of entropy per bit, and a well-designed cipher is built so that its output has no statistical structure an attacker can exploit; a uniformity test on a file therefore cannot tell good compression from good encryption, and a file that passes is not thereby encrypted
- Channel capacity; the binary symmetric channel with crossover probability p, whose capacity is 1 − H(p) bits per use, and capacity as the maximum of I(X; Y) over input distributions; the channel coding theorem: every rate R < C is achievable, with error probability tending to zero as the block length grows, and no rate above C is, shown by a random-coding argument for achievability and by Fano's inequality for the converse
- **Differential entropy and the Gaussian channel**: h(X) = −∫ f log f for densities, which can be negative and shifts by log|Jacobian| under a change of variables; the Gaussian as the maximum-entropy density for a fixed variance; the capacity C = ½ log(1 + P/N) of the additive Gaussian noise channel; for parallel Gaussian channels with noise variances Nᵢ under a total power budget P, the capacity is Σᵢ ½ log(1 + Pᵢ/Nᵢ) with Pᵢ = max(0, μ − Nᵢ) chosen by water-filling, where the water level μ is set so that Σᵢ Pᵢ = P, so the strongest channels get power first and weak ones are switched off above a threshold; colored Gaussian noise is diagonalized by the eigenvectors of its covariance, which reduces it to parallel channels whose noise variances are the eigenvalues
- **Asymptotic equipartition property**: −(1/n) log p(X₁…Xₙ) → H(X) in probability for i.i.d. sources; the typical set of about 2^(nH) sequences carrying almost all probability; the consequence that about nH bits suffice for lossless coding
- **Rate distortion theory**: R(D) = min I(X; X̂) over test channels whose expected distortion is at most D; the Gaussian source with R(D) = ½ log(σ²/D) for D ≤ σ²; the rate–fidelity trade-off in lossy compression
- **Entropy rates of stochastic processes**: H = lim (1/n) H(X₁…Xₙ) for stationary processes, equal to the limit of H(Xₙ | Xₙ₋₁…X₁); the entropy rate of a stationary Markov chain as the stationary-weighted entropy of its transition rows
- **Network information theory**: multi-user sources and channels; the multiple-access channel capacity region; the broadcast channel; the Slepian–Wolf region for distributed lossless compression with rates H(X|Y), H(Y|X) and H(X, Y); the multiple-access region as the rate pairs with R₁ ≤ I(X₁; Y | X₂), R₂ ≤ I(X₂; Y | X₁) and R₁ + R₂ ≤ I(X₁, X₂; Y) for independent inputs, with the Gaussian sum rate ½ log(1 + (P₁ + P₂)/N)
- **Sparse graph codes (LDPC)**: linear codes defined by a sparse parity-check matrix whose Tanner graph is bipartite; belief-propagation decoding by message passing; the threshold near Shannon capacity under density evolution for irregular degree distributions; the sum-product rule on the factor graph, under which each variable-to-check message is the product of the messages arriving from the other checks and each check-to-variable message sums over the configurations of the other variables, exact on a tree and iterated on a graph with cycles
- **Other code families: convolutional, turbo and fountain codes**: convolutional codes as a finite-state shift register with generator polynomials, decoded by the Viterbi algorithm in time proportional to N·2^(K−1) for a block of N symbols and constraint length K; turbo codes, two convolutional encoders joined by an interleaver and decoded iteratively by exchanging extrinsic log-likelihood ratios; fountain codes (LT and Raptor), which emit an unbounded stream of XOR combinations of source symbols drawn from the robust soliton degree distribution and are decoded by belief propagation, the receiver recovering the source from slightly more than k received symbols
- **Information theory and statistics**: maximum likelihood as minimization of the KL divergence from the empirical distribution, since (1/n)Σᵢ log p_θ(xᵢ) = −H(p̂) − D(p̂ ‖ p_θ), so the maximum-likelihood model is the closest to the data in relative entropy; the Chernoff–Stein lemma, under which, for a null P tested against an alternative Q with the type I error held below a fixed ε, the best type II error decays as 2^(−n D(P ‖ Q)); Sanov's theorem, under which the probability that the empirical distribution of n samples from P falls in a set E decays as 2^(−n min over q in E of D(q ‖ P))

### Signals and sequences

- Sampling and aliasing
- Convolution and correlation in one and two dimensions; separable kernels applied as two one-dimensional passes, costing 2k multiplications per pixel instead of k²; the two-dimensional sum (f ∗ g)[m, n] = Σᵢ Σⱼ f[i, j]·g[m − i, n − j] over the support of the kernel, with zero-padding or reflection at the boundary
- The discrete Fourier transform and the FFT; windowing and the short-time Fourier transform; reconstruction checks, in which the inverse DFT recovers the input to machine precision and Parseval's identity Σₙ|x[n]|² = (1/N)Σₖ|X[k]|² holds, the two checks used to validate a transform implementation; the radix-2 Cooley–Tukey FFT, which splits an N-point DFT (N a power of two) into the DFTs of the even and odd samples and combines them with twiddle factors ω_N^k = e^(−2πik/N) as X[k] = E[k] + ω_N^k O[k] and X[k + N/2] = E[k] − ω_N^k O[k], giving O(N log N) operations; the bit-reversal permutation that reorders the input so that the butterflies can run in place; the short-time Fourier transform X[m, k] = Σₙ x[n]·w[n − mH]·e^(−2πikn/N) takes the DFT of each windowed frame, with hop H between frame starts, and the window length trades time resolution against frequency resolution, since a short window localizes events in time but has a wide spectral main lobe, while a long one resolves nearby frequencies at the cost of blurring transients; synthesis by overlap-add of the inverse DFTs of the frames recovers x when the shifted windows sum to a constant, Σₘ w[n − mH] = c ≠ 0, as a Hann window does at 50% overlap
- Spectrograms and MFCC features; image representation; pre-emphasis y[n] = x[n] − αx[n−1] and framing into short overlapping windows before the spectrum; formants as peaks of the spectral envelope, and the mel filterbank followed by a DCT that yields MFCCs
- Image filters: Sobel edges and morphology; Canny edges from Gaussian smoothing, gradient magnitude and orientation, non-maximum suppression along the gradient and hysteresis thresholds; erosion and dilation as the minimum and maximum over a structuring element, with opening and closing built from them; denoising by Gaussian and median filters, sharpening by unsharp masking, and Fourier filtering by multiplying the 2-D DFT by a transfer function; point transforms s = T(r) applied to each pixel's grey level r, including negation, the log transform, the gamma power law s = c·r^γ, contrast stretching and thresholding; histogram equalization, which maps each grey level through the normalized cumulative histogram, s_k = (L − 1)·Σⱼ≤ₖ nⱼ/n for L grey levels and n pixels; reconstruction from projections recovers a 2-D image from its line integrals at many angles (the Radon transform), by filtered back-projection: each projection is filtered by the ramp |ω| in the frequency domain, smeared back across the image along its angle, and summed, and the Fourier slice theorem makes this exact in the continuous case
- Sequence probability: n-gram models and perplexity; finite-state tokenizers
- **Discrete cosine transform**: DCT-II, X_k = Σₙ x_n cos(π(n + ½)k/N), with its orthonormal scaling, computed through an N-point FFT in O(N log N); energy compaction, whereby a smooth signal's energy concentrates in a few low-frequency coefficients, which is why the 8 × 8 block DCT is the transform inside JPEG; the inverse transform DCT-III, which reconstructs the signal
- **Distribution of a sum of independent random variables**: for independent X and Y, the density of X + Y is the convolution ∫ f_X(x)·f_Y(z − x) dx, and the PMF of a discrete sum is the discrete convolution of the PMFs; the probability generating function of a sum is the product of the PGFs; sums of independent normals are normal, Poisson variables with rates add and gamma variables with a common rate add within their families, and repeated convolution of a density with itself is the mechanism behind the central limit theorem
- **Signal averaging**: averaging N aligned repeats of a signal with independent noise of standard deviation σ leaves the signal and cuts the noise standard deviation to σ/√N, so the signal-to-noise ratio rises by √N; the average is the sample mean with variance σ²/N; averaging power spectra over frames, rather than complex spectra whose phases are not aligned, reduces the variance of a spectral estimate in proportion to the number of frames

### Decision mathematics

- Markov decision processes; the Bellman optimality equation V*(s) = maxₐ Σₛ′ P(s′ | s, a)[r + γV*(s′)], value iteration as its repeated application, converging because the Bellman operator is a γ-contraction; temporal-difference learning, V(s) ← V(s) + α(r + γV(s′) − V(s)), which bootstraps from the estimate at the next state, against Monte Carlo returns that wait for the full episode; Q-learning as its off-policy analogue
- Multi-armed and contextual bandits: epsilon-greedy, UCB, Thompson sampling; the linear contextual bandit with expected reward θᵀx and LinUCB's ridge-regression confidence bound; combinatorial bandits, where each round selects a set of arms under a combinatorial constraint and the reward is a sum of the arms' rewards, with a UCB index per arm and an offline oracle that finds the best feasible set; non-stationary bandits, where the reward distributions drift, handled by sliding-window or discounted UCB that forgets old observations, with regret bounds that grow with the number of breakpoints; the Lai–Robbins lower bound, under which any algorithm that is consistent on every bandit instance has regret at least (1 + o(1)) log T · Σ over suboptimal arms i of Δᵢ / KL(νᵢ ‖ ν*), where Δᵢ is the gap of arm i and ν* the law of the best arm, with KL-UCB matching it asymptotically; the minimax lower bound of order √(KT), obtained from a pair of instances whose arm gaps are of order √(K/T) and that no algorithm can reliably distinguish; Bayesian bandits, in which a prior over the arm parameters is updated to a posterior after each pull and the policy acts on posterior samples, with Thompson sampling as the posterior-sampling rule; ranking bandits, in which the learner shows a ranked list and the reward depends on the clicked position, with the cascading model as the standard case; and partial monitoring, in which the learner observes a feedback signal fixed by a game matrix rather than the loss itself, so the regret depends on the feedback structure and not only on the arm gaps
- **Decision theory (expected loss, Bayes decision rule)**: a loss L(θ, a) and risk R(θ, δ) = E[L(θ, δ(X))]; Bayes risk averaged over a prior; the Bayes decision rule choosing the action of least posterior expected loss; the posterior mean for squared-error loss, the posterior median for absolute loss and the MAP estimate for 0–1 loss; a decision rule δ is admissible when no other rule has risk at least as small for every θ and strictly smaller for some θ, and minimax when its maximum risk over θ, sup over θ of R(θ, δ), is the smallest achievable, with the results that Bayes rules are admissible under mild conditions and that a minimax rule is often the Bayes rule for a least-favourable prior; utility theory, in which a preference relation over lotteries that is complete, transitive, continuous and independent is represented by a utility function, and rational choice maximizes expected utility; the value of information, the expected gain from observing a signal before acting, VOI = E_s[max over a of E[U | s]] − max over a of E[U]; decision networks, which extend Bayesian networks with decision and utility nodes; and partially observable Markov decision processes (POMDPs), in which the agent acts on a belief state, the posterior over hidden states
- **Voting methods, apportionment and fair division**: plurality, the Borda count and the Condorcet winner, with Arrow's impossibility theorem that no ranked voting method over unrestricted preferences with at least three candidates satisfies unanimity, independence of irrelevant alternatives and non-dictatorship together; apportionment of seats among states by Hamilton's largest-remainder method, Jefferson's and D'Hondt's divisor method and Webster's and Sainte-Laguë's divisor method, with the Balinski–Young theorem that no method satisfies quota and house monotonicity together; fair division with envy-free allocations and the last-diminisher procedure for proportional cake cutting
