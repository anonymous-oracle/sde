### Proof and discrete mathematics

- **Proof techniques**
  - Direct proof, contraposition, contradiction and cases; biconditional proofs as two implications, proving P ⇒ Q and Q ⇒ P; existence proofs by explicit construction versus a contradiction showing that no counterexample exists; existence and uniqueness as two separate claims, ∃x P(x) and then ∃!x, where uniqueness shows that any two witnesses are equal, with the Banach fixed-point theorem as a worked example of both
  - Ordinary and strong induction; structural induction over lists and trees
  - The well-ordering principle
  - Invariants as the proof tool for algorithms
- **Logic and sets**
  - Propositions and connectives; implication, converse, inverse and contrapositive; logical equivalence and tautologies
  - Predicates and the quantifiers ∀ and ∃; negating quantified statements
  - Rules of inference: modus ponens, modus tollens, syllogism
  - Set-builder notation, subsets, power sets, Cartesian products; roster notation listing the elements of a finite set; the operations of union, intersection, complement and set difference, De Morgan's laws (A ∪ B)ᶜ = Aᶜ ∩ Bᶜ and (A ∩ B)ᶜ = Aᶜ ∪ Bᶜ, and the proof of a set identity by showing mutual inclusion; the empty set ∅ as the unique set with no elements, a subset of every set; a finite set as one in bijection with {1, …, n} for some n ≥ 0, and an infinite set as one in bijection with no such {1, …, n}, where without the axiom of choice an infinite set need not contain a countably infinite subset
- **Axiomatic set theory and mathematical logic**: the Zermelo–Fraenkel axioms with choice (extensionality, separation, pairing, union, power set, infinity, replacement, foundation and choice); Russell's paradox as the reason naive comprehension is restricted; Cantor's theorem |P(S)| > |S| for every set S; Zorn's lemma as equivalent to the axiom of choice for the existence of maximal elements; the completeness theorem of first-order logic (every semantic consequence is provable) against Gödel's incompleteness theorems, that a consistent, recursively axiomatized theory containing arithmetic is incomplete and cannot prove its own consistency; ordinals as transitive sets well-ordered by membership, with the first infinite ordinal ω, transfinite induction and transfinite recursion, and ordinal addition and multiplication, which are not commutative (1 + ω = ω ≠ ω + 1); cardinals as initial ordinals, with ℵ₀ the cardinality of the naturals and 2^ℵ₀ that of the reals, and the Schröder–Bernstein theorem that injections in both directions give a bijection
- **Relations, functions and counting**
  - Equivalence relations and partitions; partial orders; posets (P, ≤) as sets with a reflexive, antisymmetric and transitive order, drawn as Hasse diagrams; chains and antichains; Dilworth's theorem, that the largest antichain has as many elements as the fewest chains needed to cover the poset; joins, meets and linear extensions (topological sorts of a poset)
  - Injections, surjections and bijections, and what they say about cardinality; composition of functions and the inverse f⁻¹, which exists exactly when f is a bijection and satisfies f⁻¹(f(x)) = x; countable sets such as the integers and the rationals, and Cantor's diagonal argument that the reals are uncountable; a sequence as a function from the natural numbers, with partial sums and the telescoping identity; the graph of f as the set of pairs (x, f(x)), and the graph of f⁻¹ as the reflection of that graph in the line y = x
  - The pigeonhole principle; the sum and product rules
  - Permutations, combinations, the binomial theorem, inclusion–exclusion; permutations with repetition n^k and combinations with repetition C(n + k − 1, k) counted by stars and bars; the general term C(n, r)·aⁿ⁻ʳbʳ of (a + b)ⁿ and the middle term(s), one when n is even and two when n is odd; the binomial theorem proved by induction on n through Pascal's rule C(n, k) = C(n − 1, k − 1) + C(n − 1, k), with a = b = 1 giving the sum of coefficients 2ⁿ; inclusion–exclusion |A₁ ∪ … ∪ Aₙ| = Σᵢ|Aᵢ| − Σᵢⱼ|Aᵢ ∩ Aⱼ| + … + (−1)ⁿ⁺¹|A₁ ∩ … ∩ Aₙ|, proved by counting each element's memberships, with the derangement count Dₙ = n! Σₖ (−1)ᵏ/k! ≈ n!/e as its standard application; permutations as bijections of {1, …, n} with their cycle decomposition, and the sign sgn(σ) = (−1)^(n − c(σ)), where c(σ) counts the cycles of σ, multiplicative under composition, with exactly n!/2 even permutations for n ≥ 2
  - Linear recurrences solved by the characteristic equation; first-order difference systems x_{k+1} = Ax_k with solution x_k = A^k x_0, the matrix form of a linear recurrence; generating functions G(z) = Σₙ aₙzⁿ turning a recurrence into an algebraic equation for G, with the product of two series as their convolution, and the probability generating function G_X(s) = E[s^X], whose derivative at 1 gives the mean, G′(1) = E[X]
  - Arithmetic and geometric progressions: aₙ = a + (n − 1)d with Sₙ = n(2a + (n − 1)d)/2 and arithmetic mean (a + b)/2; aₙ = arⁿ⁻¹ with Sₙ = a(1 − rⁿ)/(1 − r) for r ≠ 1, the sum to infinity S∞ = a/(1 − r) for |r| < 1, and the geometric mean √(ab)
- **Number theory for cryptography**
  - Divisibility; gcd and the Euclidean algorithm with its O(log n) step bound
  - Bézout's identity and the extended Euclidean algorithm
  - Modular arithmetic and modular inverses
  - Primes and the fundamental theorem of arithmetic
  - Fermat's little theorem and Euler's theorem; the Chinese remainder theorem
  - Fast modular exponentiation by repeated squaring
  - The correctness proof of RSA; the discrete-logarithm problem
- **Graph theory**
  - Vertices, edges and degrees; the handshake lemma
  - Paths, cycles and connectivity
  - Trees: n − 1 edges and a unique path between any two vertices
  - Bipartite graphs and 2-colouring
  - Directed acyclic graphs and topological order
  - Euler tours
  - Planarity and colouring: Euler's formula V − E + F = 2 for a connected plane graph, which gives E ≤ 3V − 6 for simple planar graphs with at least three vertices, so K₅ and K₃,₃ are not planar; Kuratowski's theorem that a graph is planar exactly when it contains no subdivision of K₅ or K₃,₃; the chromatic number χ(G) with the greedy bound χ(G) ≤ Δ + 1 in any vertex order; Brooks' theorem that χ(G) ≤ Δ for a connected graph unless G is complete or an odd cycle; and the four colour theorem for planar graphs
- **Circular permutations**: (n − 1)! arrangements of n distinct objects around a circle, since rotations are identified; fixing one object to break the rotational symmetry; reflections identify further arrangements only for necklaces and bracelets
- **Multinomial theorem and expansion**: (x₁ + … + xₖ)ⁿ as a sum over compositions of n with coefficients n!/(n₁! … nₖ!); the coefficient as the number of ways to split n labelled items into k labelled groups; the count of terms C(n + k − 1, k − 1); extracting a single coefficient
- **Catalan numbers**: Cₙ = C(2n, n)/(n + 1) counting correct bracketings, binary trees with n internal nodes and lattice paths that never cross the diagonal; the recurrence Cₙ₊₁ = Σᵢ CᵢCₙ₋ᵢ; growth as 4ⁿ/(n^{3/2}√π)
- **Burnside's lemma and orbit counting**: for a finite group G acting on a set X, the orbits as the equivalence classes of the action; the orbit–stabilizer theorem |G·x| · |G_x| = |G|; Burnside's lemma, that the number of orbits equals (1/|G|) Σ over g in G of |Fix(g)|, where Fix(g) is the set of points that g leaves unchanged; counting necklaces of n beads in k colours by summing over the rotations, which gives (1/n) Σ over d dividing n of φ(d)·k^(n/d) with Euler's φ; the cycle-index form of Pólya's enumeration theorem

### Probability

- **Foundations**
  - Sample spaces, events and the axioms; the Kolmogorov axioms: nonnegativity, P(Ω) = 1, and countable additivity for pairwise disjoint events
  - Joint and marginal probability; marginalizing a joint table; joint densities f(x, y) for continuous variables, with marginals f_X(x) = ∫ f(x, y) dy, conditional densities f(y | x) = f(x, y)/f_X(x), and independence equivalent to f(x, y) = f_X(x)·f_Y(y)
  - Conditional probability, independence and Bayes' theorem derived from the definition
  - Random variables, expectation, linearity of expectation (no independence needed), variance and covariance
  - Indicator variables
- **Distributions**
  - Probability mass, density and cumulative distribution functions
  - Bernoulli, binomial, geometric, Poisson, uniform, exponential and normal, with their means and variances; the beta distribution on [0, 1] with density x^(α−1)(1 − x)^(β−1)/B(α, β) and mean α/(α + β), and its conjugacy with the binomial: a Beta(α, β) prior with k successes in n trials gives the posterior Beta(α + k, β + n − k); the normal law derived twice, by Gauss, who showed that if the arithmetic mean is the maximum-likelihood location of independent errors for every sample, then the log-density is quadratic, and by Herschel and Maxwell, who showed that if the two coordinates of a point in the plane are independent and the joint density depends only on the distance from the origin, then each coordinate is normal
  - The memoryless property
  - Sampling from distributions; Monte Carlo estimation; survey sampling designs: stratified sampling with proportional allocation, whose variance is no larger than simple random sampling's, and cluster sampling, whose variance grows with the correlation inside each cluster; estimands as the population quantity a design targets (a mean, a total or a difference in means), distinct from the estimator, the rule applied to the sample, and the estimate, its value; design-based inference, in which the randomness comes from the sampling design, with the Horvitz–Thompson total Σ yᵢ/πᵢ unbiased when every unit has a known inclusion probability πᵢ > 0
  - Sampling without replacement and the hypergeometric distribution: drawing n items from a population of N with K successes gives P(X = k) = C(K, k)·C(N − K, n − k)/C(N, n), with mean nK/N and variance n(K/N)(1 − K/N)(N − n)/(N − 1), the finite-population correction (N − n)/(N − 1) that makes its variance smaller than the binomial's; the binomial as the limit when N is large
  - Location-scale families: a family of densities f_(μ,σ)(x) = (1/σ) f((x − μ)/σ) generated by one standard density f, with μ the location and σ > 0 the scale; the normal and the Laplace as location-scale families, the exponential as a scale family with its location fixed at 0; the standardized variable Z = (X − μ)/σ with the reference density, which makes estimators of μ and σ equivariant under the change of units
  - The multivariate normal N(μ, Σ) with density (2π)^(−d/2)|Σ|^(−1/2) exp(−½(x − μ)ᵀΣ⁻¹(x − μ)) for a positive definite covariance Σ; closed under affine maps, so that AX + b is normal with mean Aμ + b and covariance AΣAᵀ; its marginals are normal; its conditional mean E[X₁ | X₂] = μ₁ + Σ₁₂Σ₂₂⁻¹(x₂ − μ₂) is linear in the observed coordinates; uncorrelated coordinates are independent
- **The birthday bound**
  - P(some collision) ≈ 1 − e^(−n(n−1)/2N); collisions become likely near n ≈ √N
  - Why a random 64-bit ID collides after about 2³² draws
- **Tail bounds and order statistics**
  - Markov, Chebyshev and Chernoff bounds; the union bound
  - The law of large numbers and the central limit theorem; convergence in probability (the weak law) versus almost sure convergence (the strong law), which is the stronger mode; convergence in distribution, Fₙ(x) → F(x) at every continuity point of F, the sense in which the central limit theorem holds; the chain of implications almost sure convergence ⇒ convergence in probability ⇒ convergence in distribution, whose converses fail in general
  - The maximum of n samples: P(max ≤ t) = F(t)ⁿ; the k-th order statistic X₍ₖ₎ with P(X₍ₖ₎ ≤ t) = Σ from j = k to n of C(n, j)·F(t)ʲ·(1 − F(t))ⁿ⁻ʲ
  - Fan-out tails: a request waiting on 100 sub-requests, each slow with probability 1%, is slow with probability 1 − 0.99¹⁰⁰ ≈ 63%
  - Hedged requests: a second copy after the p95 delay makes the tail P(X > t)² for about 5% extra load; tied requests cancel the loser
  - Why averages hide tails, and why a p99 cannot be averaged across hosts
- **Balls into bins**
  - n requests at random onto n servers: maximum load Θ(log n / log log n)
  - The power of two choices: the less loaded of d ≥ 2 random servers drops the maximum load to ln ln n / ln d + O(1)
  - Why "least connections" over stale load reports herds, and why two random choices tolerate staleness
- **Stochastic processes**
  - Markov chains and stationary distributions; the transition matrix P, the stationary equation πP = π, and the n-step probabilities given by P^n, whose rows converge to the stationary law for an irreducible aperiodic chain
  - Poisson processes: the counting process N(t) with independent increments and N(t + s) − N(t) ~ Poisson(λs), so that P(N(t) = k) = e^(−λt)(λt)ᵏ/k!; interarrival times exponential with mean 1/λ and memoryless; superposition adds rates, and thinning keeps each event independently with probability p
  - Random processes as indexed families of random variables X(t) or Xₙ with their sample paths; strict and wide-sense stationarity, the wide-sense case requiring a constant mean and an autocorrelation R(s, t) = E[X(s)X(t)] that depends only on t − s; ergodicity, under which a time average along one sample path converges to the ensemble mean; the Wiener–Khinchin theorem, under which the power spectral density is the Fourier transform of the autocorrelation
- **Plausible reasoning and Cox's desiderata**: degrees of plausibility ordered qualitatively; Cox's desiderata of consistency and rationality forcing the sum and product rules up to a monotone reparametrization; probability as the unique consistent extension of deductive logic to uncertain propositions
- **Law of total probability**: P(B) = Σᵢ P(B | Aᵢ)P(Aᵢ) over a partition of the sample space; the continuous form with integration against a density; its role as the denominator of Bayes' theorem when conditioning on competing causes
- **Transformations of random variables**: the change-of-variable formula f_Y(y) = f_X(g⁻¹(y))·|d g⁻¹(y)/dy| for monotone maps; the CDF method for general maps; summing over branches for non-monotone maps; the Jacobian determinant for vector transformations
- **Log-normal and multinomial distributions**: a log-normal variable exp(X) with X normal, median e^μ and mean e^(μ + σ²/2), arising from multiplicative effects; the multinomial as n trials over k categories with probability n!/(∏ nᵢ!)·∏ pᵢ^nᵢ; negative covariance between category counts
- **Maximum-entropy distributions**: maximizing entropy under moment constraints with Lagrange multipliers, giving exponential-family densities; the uniform law with no constraint, the geometric law for a fixed mean on the non-negative integers, the normal for a fixed mean and variance, the exponential for a fixed mean on the positive reals; the maximum-entropy principle as the rule for choosing a prior or model consistent with known constraints and nothing more
- **Jeffreys and ignorance priors**: non-informative priors for parameters about which nothing is known; the Jeffreys prior proportional to the square root of the Fisher information, invariant under reparametrization; the flat prior for a location parameter and 1/σ for a scale parameter; a flat prior in one parametrization becoming informative in another
- **Paradoxes of probability (Borel–Kolmogorov)**: conditioning on an event of probability zero depends on the limiting sequence of events chosen, so the same conditioning event on a sphere yields different conditional densities in different coordinates; nonconglomerability and the marginalization paradox as related failures of naive conditioning; Bertrand's paradox: a chord of a circle chosen at random has probability 1/3 of exceeding the side of the inscribed equilateral triangle if its random endpoints are chosen, 1/2 if its distance from the centre is chosen at random and 1/4 if its midpoint is uniform in the disc, the three answers differing only in the invariant measure used to pick the chord, so the question must state the random mechanism
- **Rule of succession and exchangeable sequences**: a sequence is exchangeable when its joint law is invariant under permutation of its terms; de Finetti's representation as a mixture of independent and identically distributed sequences; Laplace's rule (k + 1)/(n + 2) after k successes in n trials under a uniform prior

### Statistics and experimental design

- **Estimation**
  - Estimators, bias and variance; maximum-likelihood estimation; Fisher information; consistency (θ̂ₙ → θ in probability as n grows) and asymptotic normality of the maximum-likelihood estimator; the mean squared error as bias² + variance, and the trade of added bias for lower variance; Fisher information I(θ) = E[(∂ log f(X; θ)/∂θ)²] = −E[∂² log f(X; θ)/∂θ²] under regularity conditions, additive over independent observations so that n of them carry n·I(θ), with the Bernoulli case I(p) = 1/(p(1 − p))
  - The sample mean, its standard error σ/√n, and confidence intervals; the median and mode as the other measures of central tendency, the median being the 0.5 quantile and the trimmed mean its outlier-resistant compromise
  - Bayesian inference: prior and posterior updates; the naive Bayes classifier, whose posterior P(y | x) ∝ P(y)·∏ᵢ P(xᵢ | y) assumes the features are independent given the class
  - The bootstrap and the jackknife; the parametric bootstrap, which resamples from a fitted distribution instead of the data, and the jackknife's leave-one-out estimate of bias; percentile intervals as the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles of the bootstrap distribution of the estimate, and the bias-corrected and accelerated (BCa) interval, which shifts those percentile cut-points by a bias constant and an acceleration constant computed from the jackknife
- **Hypothesis testing**
  - Hypothesis tests, p-values and their common misreadings
  - Power and sample size; the sample size of an A/B test, n = 2σ²(z₁₋α/₂ + z₁₋β)²/δ² per arm for a difference δ in means at two-sided level α and power 1 − β, with each unit assigned to one variant by hashing its identifier into buckets so that its variant stays fixed across sessions; canary releases, which send a change to a small traffic slice, watch guardrail metrics against the control and roll back on a breach; and shadow traffic, which runs the new path on copies of requests without serving its output to users
  - Practical significance and effect size: a standardized effect such as Cohen's d = (μ₁ − μ₂)/σ, read against the conventional benchmarks of 0.2 (small), 0.5 (medium) and 0.8 (large), and reported beside the p-value; a confidence interval for the difference in means or proportions as the range of effect sizes compatible with the data; the minimum detectable effect, the smallest effect that a test of given power and sample size can reliably find; a statistically significant difference too small to matter in the product's units, and the reverse
  - Multiple testing and the false discovery rate
  - Sequential testing and the peeking problem; CUPED variance reduction
  - Chi-squared tests: the goodness-of-fit statistic Σᵢ (Oᵢ − Eᵢ)²/Eᵢ over k cells, approximately χ² with k − 1 − m degrees of freedom when m parameters are estimated from the data; the test of independence in an r × c contingency table, with expected counts Eᵢⱼ = (row total)(column total)/n and (r − 1)(c − 1) degrees of freedom; the rule that expected counts should be at least about 5
- **Quantiles**
  - Estimating quantiles from histograms
  - Why per-host p99 values do not average to the fleet p99
- **Correlation and causation**
  - Confounders
  - Causal DAGs and adjustment; propensity scores and inverse-propensity weighting; the backdoor criterion, under which a set Z of covariates adjusts for confounding when it blocks every backdoor path from treatment to outcome and contains no descendant of the treatment
  - Difference-in-differences; uplift modelling
- **Association between variables**: the correlation matrix; Pearson correlation for linear association versus Spearman rank correlation for monotone association and outlier resistance; Kendall tau from concordant and discordant pairs; correlation is not causation
- **Sufficiency and the factorization theorem**: a statistic T(X) is sufficient for θ when the conditional law of the data given T does not depend on θ; the factorization criterion f(x; θ) = g(T(x); θ)h(x); the sample mean of a normal sample as sufficient for its mean; a sufficient statistic loses no information about θ
- **Basu's theorem and complete sufficient statistics**: completeness, meaning E[g(T)] = 0 for all θ forces g(T) = 0 almost surely; a complete sufficient statistic is independent of every ancillary statistic; the sample mean and sample variance of a normal sample are independent; an ancillary statistic is one whose distribution does not depend on θ, such as the sample range of a normal sample with known variance, which is ancillary for the mean, so that Basu's theorem makes it independent of the complete sufficient sample mean
- **Exponential families and multiparameter models**: densities in the form exp(η(θ)·T(x) − A(θ))h(x); natural parameters, and the log-partition function A whose derivatives give the mean and variance of T; the normal, Bernoulli and Poisson as members; multiparameter maximum likelihood with the Fisher information matrix
- **Method of moments**: equating sample moments to population moments and solving for the parameters; consistency from the law of large numbers; the gamma distribution as a worked example; efficiency compared with maximum likelihood
- **Delta method**: the asymptotic normal law of g(θ̂) from a central limit theorem for θ̂ through a first-order Taylor expansion, with variance g′(θ)²σ²/n; variance-stabilizing transformations; the second-order case when g′(θ) = 0
- **Cramér–Rao bound**: Var(θ̂) ≥ 1/(n I(θ)) for unbiased estimators, from the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality applied to the score; efficient estimators attain it and the maximum-likelihood estimator attains it asymptotically; the Bernoulli mean as a worked case
- **Student's t and F distributions**: t as a standard normal divided by the square root of a chi-square over its degrees of freedom, with heavier tails at small n; F as a ratio of independent scaled chi-squares, governing variance ratios and F-tests; the chi-square distribution as a sum of k squared independent standard normals, with mean k and variance 2k, and (n − 1)s²/σ² distributed χ²(n−1) for a normal sample, the fact from which t and F are built
- **One-sample and two-sample t-tests**: t = (x̄ − μ₀)/(s/√n) with n − 1 degrees of freedom; Welch's test for unequal variances; paired differences; the assumptions of independence and an approximately normal sample mean
- **z-test**: z = (x̄ − μ₀)/(σ/√n) when σ is known or n is large; the two-proportion z-test for conversion rates, with a pooled variance under the null
- **Type I and Type II errors**: rejecting a true null with probability α, the significance level; failing to reject a false one with probability β; power 1 − β; the trade-off at a fixed sample size and the Neyman–Pearson lemma for the most powerful test at a given α
- **Analysis of variance (ANOVA)**: splitting the total sum of squares into between-group and within-group parts; the F ratio of mean squares under the null of equal means; one-way and two-way designs; post hoc comparisons with multiple-comparison correction
- **Mann–Whitney U test**: a rank test for two independent samples, where U counts the pairs in which one sample exceeds the other; the normal approximation for large samples with a tie correction; insensitive to outliers because only ranks enter
- **Wilcoxon signed-rank test**: paired differences ranked by absolute size, with the sum of ranks of positive differences as the statistic; its null distribution under symmetry about zero; corrections for ties and zero differences
- **Skewness and kurtosis**: standardized third and fourth central moments, skewness E[(X − μ)³]/σ³ and excess kurtosis E[(X − μ)⁴]/σ⁴ − 3; what they say about asymmetry and tail weight; sample estimates of them being unstable at small n
- **Nonparametric inference**: estimating a distribution without a parametric family; the empirical CDF Fₙ(x) = (1/n)Σ 1{Xᵢ ≤ x} and the Dvoretzky–Kiefer–Wolfowitz bound on its uniform error; kernel density estimation and the bias–variance trade-off in its bandwidth; permutation tests that shuffle group labels to build the null distribution of a statistic under exchangeability
- **Exploratory data analysis**: the five-number summary (minimum, Q1, median, Q3, maximum) drawn as a box plot, with the 1.5 × IQR fence (IQR = Q3 − Q1) for flagging outliers; residual plots against fitted values to check linearity and constant variance; quantile–quantile plots against a reference distribution to compare tail shapes
- **Sampling distributions**: a random sample X₁…Xₙ as n independent draws from one distribution, and a statistic as a function of the sample; the sampling distribution of a statistic as its law over repeated samples; for the sample mean, E[x̄] = μ and Var(x̄) = σ²/n, exactly normal when the population is normal and approximately normal for large n by the central limit theorem; the t statistic (x̄ − μ)/(s/√n) as the standardized sample mean when the scale is estimated, and the standard error as the standard deviation of a sampling distribution
- **Kernel methods**: a positive-definite kernel k(x, y) that equals an inner product in some feature space (Mercer's theorem); the kernel trick of computing those inner products without forming the feature space; the representer theorem, under which a regularized empirical-risk minimizer lies in the span of the kernel functions at the training points, so that the problem reduces to the n × n Gram matrix K; kernel ridge regression with coefficients α = (K + λI)⁻¹y, costing O(n³) to solve; the Gaussian kernel exp(−‖x − y‖²/(2σ²)) and its bandwidth as the bias–variance knob shared with kernel density estimation
- **Moment generating functions**: M_X(t) = E[e^(tX)], whose k-th derivative at 0 gives the k-th moment E[X^k]; uniqueness, so that an MGF finite on an interval determines the distribution; M_(X+Y)(t) = M_X(t)·M_Y(t) for independent X and Y, which is how sums of normals and Poissons are identified; cumulants as the coefficients of log M_X(t)

### Queueing and performance modelling

- **Operational laws** (no distributional assumption)
  - The utilization law U = X·S
  - The forced-flow law Xₖ = Vₖ·X
  - Little's law L = λW, with its sample-path proof
  - The bottleneck law: maximum throughput is 1 / max(Vₖ·Sₖ)
  - The interactive response-time law for closed systems: R = N/X − Z
- **Queueing models**
  - Arrival and service processes
  - M/M/1 from its birth–death chain: πₙ = (1 − ρ)ρⁿ, mean number in system ρ/(1 − ρ), mean time 1/(μ − λ)
  - The knee: 2 service times at ρ = 0.5, 10 at 0.9, 100 at 0.99; utilization ceilings of 70–80% in capacity planning
  - M/M/k and the Erlang C formula; why one shared queue for k servers beats k separate queues
  - M/G/1 and the Pollaczek–Khinchine formula, E[W_q] = ρ·E[S]·(1 + C²)/(2(1 − ρ)); why service-time variability costs as much as load
- **Estimation as a method**
  - Fermi estimation: decompose, estimate each factor within a factor of 3, multiply
  - Sensitivity: the widest-ranging factor dominates; estimate it two ways
  - Capacity planning and back-of-the-envelope arithmetic in design work

- Solving Ax = b by Gaussian elimination and LU factorization; Cramer's rule as determinant ratios xᵢ = det(Aᵢ)/det(A), where Aᵢ replaces column i with b, factorial in cost by cofactor expansion; row operations; determinants (ad − bc for 2 × 2) and invertibility; the n × n determinant by cofactor expansion along a row, with the 3 × 3 case a(ei − fh) − b(di − fg) + c(dh − eg) and the sum over permutations; the adjugate with A⁻¹ = adj(A)/det(A); row substitution, replacing one row by itself plus a multiple of another, which leaves the solution set and the determinant unchanged; row echelon form and reduced row echelon form reached by the same row operations, with the pivots counting the rank; forward elimination costing about 2n³/3 flops followed by back substitution, and partial pivoting with the factorization PA = LU for stability

- Eigenvalues and eigenvectors; power iteration; Hotelling deflation A − λ₁v₁v₁ᵀ, which removes a found eigenpair of a symmetric matrix so that power iteration finds the next; spectral methods as the eigen-based family behind PCA and spectral clustering, where the graph Laplacian L = D − W has eigenvectors for its smallest eigenvalues that embed the vertices so that k-means on them separates clusters
- The singular value decomposition, principal component analysis and low-rank approximation
- Distance and similarity: Euclidean, Manhattan, cosine; the metric axioms (non-negativity, identity of indiscernibles, symmetry and the triangle inequality); the Minkowski distance (Σᵢ|xᵢ − yᵢ|ᵖ)^(1/p) for p ≥ 1, with p = 2 giving Euclidean, p = 1 Manhattan, and the limit p → ∞ giving the Chebyshev distance, the largest coordinate difference; a norm inducing the metric d(x, y) = ‖x − y‖, so the norm axioms (positive definiteness, absolute homogeneity, triangle inequality) imply the metric axioms; cosine similarity x·y ÷ (‖x‖‖y‖) and the angular distance arccos(cos θ), which is a metric, whereas 1 − cos θ fails the triangle inequality; on unit vectors ‖x − y‖² = 2(1 − cos θ), so the Euclidean and cosine rankings agree; the O(d) cost per pair, and how each measure responds to scaling and translation
- Column space and null space; the Moore–Penrose pseudo-inverse for over- and under-determined systems; hyperplanes and the distance of a point to a hyperplane; the four fundamental subspaces (column space, null space, row space and left null space) with dimensions r, n − r, r and m − r for a rank-r matrix of size m × n
- **Symmetric and skew-symmetric matrices**: real symmetric matrices have real eigenvalues and orthogonal eigenvectors by the spectral theorem; skew-symmetric matrices have purely imaginary eigenvalues; every square matrix splits uniquely into ½(A + Aᵀ) plus ½(A − Aᵀ); the spectral theorem, A = QΛQᵀ = Σᵢ λᵢqᵢqᵢᵀ with orthonormal eigenvectors qᵢ, so that a symmetric matrix is a sum of rank-one projections weighted by its eigenvalues, and a self-adjoint operator on a finite-dimensional inner product space is diagonalized by an orthonormal eigenbasis in the same way
- **Positive definite matrices and minima**: xᵀAx > 0 for every nonzero x, equivalently positive eigenvalues, positive pivots or a Cholesky factorization; the quadratic ½xᵀAx − bᵀx minimized where Ax = b; a positive definite Hessian as the second-order test for a strict minimum
- **Complex matrices and similarity transformations**: the conjugate transpose, Hermitian and unitary matrices; similar matrices B = P⁻¹AP share eigenvalues and the characteristic polynomial; diagonalizability as similarity to a diagonal matrix; powers A^k = P D^k P⁻¹ computed from the diagonal form D = P⁻¹AP; complex vectors with the Hermitian inner product ⟨x, y⟩ = x*y = Σ x̄ᵢyᵢ and norm ‖x‖ = √(x*x), and the spectral theorem for Hermitian matrices, with real eigenvalues and orthonormal eigenvectors, so that A = UΛU* for a unitary U
- **Jordan form and Lyapunov stability**: Jordan blocks as the canonical form of defective matrices, built from generalized eigenvectors; the system x′ = Ax is asymptotically stable exactly when every eigenvalue has negative real part, and the Lyapunov equation AᵀP + PA = −Q certifies it; the matrix exponential e^(At) = Σ (At)^k / k! as the solution of x′ = Ax, computed from the Jordan form
- **QR factorization and the QR algorithm**: A = QR with orthogonal Q and upper-triangular R, computed by Gram–Schmidt, Householder reflections or Givens rotations; least squares by solving Rx = Qᵀb without forming AᵀA; the QR algorithm iterating QR factorizations, with shifts, to reveal eigenvalues
- **Sparse and structured matrix problems**: compressed storage of nonzeros; fill-in during elimination and reordering to limit it; banded, triangular, Toeplitz and circulant structure giving fast solves; iterative methods preferred for large sparse systems
- **Multilinear forms and complexification**: a k-linear form is linear in each argument separately, with bilinear forms as matrices, B(x, y) = xᵀMy, and alternating multilinear forms such as the determinant, which as a function of the n columns of an n × n matrix is the alternating n-linear form, unique up to a scalar; the complexification V_ℂ = V ⊗ ℂ of a real vector space, over which a real matrix's eigenvalues are taken, with complex conjugation of coordinates making the complex eigenvalues of a real matrix come in conjugate pairs
- **Matrix norms**: the Frobenius norm ‖A‖_F = √(Σᵢⱼ aᵢⱼ²) = √(trace(AᵀA)); induced norms ‖A‖ = max over ‖x‖ = 1 of ‖Ax‖, with ‖A‖₁ the maximum absolute column sum and ‖A‖∞ the maximum absolute row sum; the spectral norm ‖A‖₂ equal to the largest singular value; the nuclear norm as the sum of singular values; the Eckart–Young theorem that the truncated SVD is the best rank-k approximation in both the spectral and the Frobenius norm
- **Matrix functions**: f(A) defined through the spectrum, f(A) = P f(D) P⁻¹ for diagonalizable A, and for a Jordan block J = λI + N with N nilpotent, f(J) = Σₖ f⁽ᵏ⁾(λ)Nᵏ/k!, so that the Jordan form extends any sufficiently smooth scalar function to a matrix; the matrix square root and logarithm and their non-uniqueness when several eigenvalues share a branch; the Cauchy integral f(A) = (1/2πi)∮ f(z)(zI − A)⁻¹ dz over a contour enclosing the spectrum; the matrix exponential as the case f(z) = eᶻ

### Calculus and optimization

- **Single-variable calculus**
  - Limits and continuity; the derivative as a limit
  - Product, quotient and chain rules
  - The first-order Taylor approximation, and why a small gradient step decreases a smooth function
  - The integral as accumulated area; probability densities
  - First-order differential equations; Euler and Runge–Kutta (RK4) methods; multistep methods such as Adams–Bashforth, which reuse past slopes and need one new function evaluation per step, with implicit backward differentiation formulas for stiff systems; the local truncation error and the global error one order lower; the forward-time centred-space scheme for the heat equation u_t = αu_xx, stable when r = αΔt/Δx² ≤ 1/2, and the CFL bound on the time step for advection; the Crank–Nicolson scheme for the heat equation, the average of the explicit and implicit schemes, second-order accurate in time and unconditionally stable, solved by a tridiagonal system at each step; von Neumann stability analysis, which substitutes a Fourier mode e^(ikx) and requires the amplification factor to satisfy |G| ≤ 1 for every wavenumber
- **Multivariable and matrix calculus**
  - Partial derivatives and the gradient
  - Gradients of vector functions, Jacobians, the matrix chain rule; the inverse function theorem, under which a C¹ map with invertible Jacobian at a point is locally invertible there, with the derivative of the inverse equal to the inverse of the Jacobian; the implicit function theorem, under which F(x, y) = 0 defines y as a C¹ function of x near a point where ∂F/∂y is invertible, with dy/dx = −(∂F/∂y)⁻¹(∂F/∂x)
  - The Hessian
  - Vector calculus: divergence ∇·F = Σᵢ ∂Fᵢ/∂xᵢ, measuring local source strength; curl ∇ × F in three dimensions, measuring local rotation; ∇ × ∇f = 0 and ∇ · (∇ × F) = 0; the divergence theorem and Stokes' theorem as the integral forms of these local statements
- **Optimization**
  - Convex sets and functions; every local minimum of a convex function is global; the separating hyperplane theorem, under which disjoint convex sets are split by a hyperplane aᵀx ≤ b on one side and aᵀx ≥ b on the other; the dual cone K* = {y : yᵀx ≥ 0 for all x in K}; quasiconvex functions, whose sublevel sets are convex; the conjugate f*(y) = sup over x of (yᵀx − f(x)); convexity and concavity, with a concave function defined as one whose negative is convex; the first-order characterization f(y) ≥ f(x) + ∇f(x)ᵀ(y − x) for differentiable convex f and the second-order test that the Hessian is positive semidefinite; Jensen's inequality f(E X) ≤ E f(X) for convex f
  - Gradient descent and its convergence rates; stochastic gradient descent; the L-smoothness inequality ‖∇f(x) − ∇f(y)‖ ≤ L‖x − y‖ and the μ-strong convexity, under which gradient descent with step 1/L contracts the gap by a factor (1 − μ/L) per step, f(x_k) − f* ≤ (1 − μ/L)^k (f(x₀) − f*), while convex L-smooth functions give the O(1/k) rate; steepest descent as gradient descent with the step chosen by exact line search, whose iterates zig-zag between orthogonal directions on an ill-conditioned quadratic, each step shrinking the A-norm error by (κ − 1)/(κ + 1) with κ the condition number
  - Momentum and adaptive optimizers
  - Regularization (L1, L2); coordinate descent; ridge regression β̂ = (XᵀX + λI)⁻¹Xᵀy, which shrinks each coefficient along the principal directions of X by the factor d²/(d² + λ) for singular value d, and the lasso's L1 penalty, which sets some coefficients exactly to zero and so selects variables; dropout as random masking of units with keep probability p during training, scaled by 1/p so that test-time activations need no change, a cheap average over an ensemble of thinned networks
  - Constrained optimization: Lagrange multipliers and KKT conditions; the Lagrangian dual function g(λ) = inf over x of L(x, λ) for λ ≥ 0, weak duality g(λ) ≤ f(x*), and strong duality under Slater's condition; the KKT conditions for minimizing f(x) subject to gᵢ(x) ≤ 0 and hⱼ(x) = 0: stationarity ∇f + Σᵢ λᵢ∇gᵢ + Σⱼ νⱼ∇hⱼ = 0, primal feasibility, dual feasibility λᵢ ≥ 0, and complementary slackness λᵢgᵢ(x) = 0; necessary at a local minimum under a constraint qualification such as Slater's, and sufficient for convex problems
  - Linear programming; the assignment problem and the Hungarian method; quadratic programming, minimizing ½xᵀQx + cᵀx subject to linear constraints with Q positive semidefinite; geometric programming with posynomial objectives, which become convex under the substitution x = e^y; the simplex method, which moves between adjacent vertices of the feasible polyhedron by pivots that exchange one basic variable for another and improve the objective, stopping when every reduced cost is nonnegative, exponential in the worst case (Klee–Minty) yet fast in practice; quadratic programming solved through its KKT system, which is linear in x and the multipliers when only equalities are imposed, with active-set and interior-point methods for inequality constraints
  - Generalization versus optimization; approximation error
  - Generalized inequalities and proper cones: a proper cone K is closed, convex, pointed and has nonempty interior, and x ⪯_K y means y − x ∈ K; the nonnegative orthant gives the componentwise order and the positive semidefinite cone gives the Loewner order, A ⪯ B when B − A is positive semidefinite; the dual cone K* = {y : ⟨y, x⟩ ≥ 0 for all x in K}, which equals K for the orthant and for the positive semidefinite cone; vector optimization with respect to K, whose solutions are the minimal elements of the order, the Pareto-optimal points
- **Newton's method**: the step x ← x − H⁻¹∇f(x) from the second-order Taylor model; quadratic convergence near a minimum with a positive definite Hessian; O(n³) per step to solve the Hessian system; quasi-Newton updates (BFGS) that build the Hessian from gradient differences; with equality constraints Ax = b, the Newton step from the KKT block system whose matrix has H and Aᵀ in the first block row and A and 0 in the second; the secant condition B_(k+1)s_k = y_k with s_k = x_(k+1) − x_k and y_k = ∇f(x_(k+1)) − ∇f(x_k); the BFGS update B_(k+1) = B_k − (B_k s_k s_kᵀ B_k)/(s_kᵀ B_k s_k) + (y_k y_kᵀ)/(y_kᵀ s_k), which keeps B positive definite when y_kᵀ s_k > 0 and gives superlinear local convergence; limited-memory L-BFGS, storing the last m pairs (s, y) in O(mn) memory
- **Line search and trust regions**: Armijo's sufficient-decrease condition f(x + αd) ≤ f(x) + c₁α∇f(x)ᵀd, found by backtracking; the Wolfe conditions adding the curvature condition ∇f(x + αd)ᵀd ≥ c₂∇f(x)ᵀd, with the strong Wolfe form required by quasi-Newton updates; trust-region methods minimizing the quadratic model m_k(p) = f(x_k) + ∇f(x_k)ᵀp + ½pᵀB_k p over a ball ‖p‖ ≤ Δ_k, comparing the actual with the predicted reduction ρ_k = (f(x_k) − f(x_k + p_k))/(m_k(0) − m_k(p_k)) to accept the step and to grow or shrink Δ_k; the Cauchy point, the minimizer of the model along steepest descent within the ball, which guarantees convergence to a stationary point
- **Conjugate gradient**: solving Ax = b for symmetric positive definite A with A-conjugate search directions in a Krylov subspace; termination in at most n steps in exact arithmetic; convergence governed by the square root of the condition number; preconditioning; the Krylov subspace K_k(A, b) = span{b, Ab, …, A^(k−1)b}, from which the k-th iterate is drawn and in which it minimizes the error in the A-norm; the Krylov methods built on the same subspaces: Lanczos iteration, which builds an orthonormal basis of K_k(A, b) for symmetric A with a tridiagonal projection, MINRES for symmetric indefinite systems, and GMRES, which for nonsymmetric A minimizes the residual over the Krylov subspace at each step using Arnoldi orthogonalization
- **Proximal methods**: minimizing f(x) + g(x) with a nonsmooth g through the proximal operator prox_g(v) = argmin over x of g(x) + ½‖x − v‖²; proximal gradient iterations; soft-thresholding as the proximal operator of the L1 norm
- **Subgradient methods**: for convex f, a subgradient g at x satisfies f(y) ≥ f(x) + gᵀ(y − x) for all y, and the subdifferential ∂f(x) is the set of all subgradients at x; the subgradient method x_(k+1) = x_k − α_k g_k with diminishing step sizes, whose best iterate converges at rate O(1/√k); the optimality condition 0 ∈ ∂f(x*); subgradients of the absolute value, which are the interval [−1, 1] at zero, and of a maximum of affine functions
- **Mirror descent**: the update x_(k+1) = argmin over x of η⟨g_k, x⟩ + D_ψ(x, x_k), with a Bregman divergence D_ψ generated by a strictly convex ψ; the Euclidean choice recovering subgradient descent, and the negative-entropy choice giving multiplicative weights on the probability simplex, whose dimension dependence is only logarithmic
- **ADMM (alternating direction method of multipliers)**: for min f(x) + g(z) subject to Ax + Bz = c, the augmented Lagrangian L_ρ = f(x) + g(z) + yᵀ(Ax + Bz − c) + (ρ/2)‖Ax + Bz − c‖²; the iterations x ← argmin over x of L_ρ, z ← argmin over z of L_ρ, then y ← y + ρ(Ax + Bz − c); convergence under convexity for any ρ > 0; used for distributed consensus, where each agent solves a local subproblem and the shared variable is an average
- **Robust and stochastic formulations**: the min–max robust problem min over x of the maximum over an uncertainty set of f(x, u); the stochastic program min over x of E[f(x, ξ)] solved by sample average approximation; chance constraints; the trade-off between conservatism and expected performance
- **Non-convex diagnostics**: the gradient norm and the Hessian's eigenvalues at a stationary point to separate local minima, saddle points and maxima; restarts and sensitivity to initialization as the test of a local answer; a local minimum is not certified global
- **Weight initialization**: the variance of layer activations is kept roughly constant from layer to layer when the weight variance matches the fan-in and fan-out, Glorot (Xavier) initialization with variance 2/(n_in + n_out) for tanh-like units and He initialization with variance 2/n_in for ReLU units; zero initialization fails by symmetry, because every unit in a layer then receives the same gradient; restarts from different initializations as the empirical check for a local answer
- **Derivative-free and least-squares methods**: derivative-free optimization by model-based or pattern search (the Nelder–Mead simplex, coordinate pattern search) when gradients are unavailable or unreliable; nonlinear least squares min ½Σᵢ rᵢ(x)² with the Gauss–Newton step (JᵀJ)p = −Jᵀr, which drops the second-order term, and Levenberg–Marquardt damping (JᵀJ + μI)p = −Jᵀr, which moves between Gauss–Newton for small μ and gradient descent for large μ; Frank–Wolfe (conditional gradient) steps x_(k+1) = (1 − γ)x_k + γs_k, with s_k the minimizer of the linear function ∇f(x_k)ᵀs over the constraint set, which keeps iterates feasible without a projection and converges at rate O(1/k) for smooth convex f; alternating minimization over blocks of variables, each block solved while the others are held fixed, with the objective decreasing at every sweep
- **Interior-point methods**: inequality constraints handled by the log barrier −(1/t)Σ log(−fᵢ(x)); Newton steps on each centering problem as t grows along the central path; polynomial-time bounds for linear and self-concordant convex programs
- **Geometric problems (minimum-volume ellipsoids)**: the smallest-volume ellipsoid enclosing a point set as a convex problem in the ellipsoid's matrix and centre, with the negative log-determinant as the objective; the Löwner–John ellipsoid as its theory
- **Pontryagin maximum principle**: a necessary condition for optimal control; the Hamiltonian H = p·f + L with costate p obeying the adjoint equation, and H maximized pointwise over admissible controls; bang-bang controls in time-optimal problems
- **Optimization of functionals (calculus of variations)**: minimizing a functional J[y] = ∫ₐᵇ L(x, y, y′) dx over curves with fixed endpoints; the first variation, whose vanishing for every admissible perturbation gives the Euler–Lagrange equation ∂L/∂y − d/dx(∂L/∂y′) = 0; geodesics and the brachistochrone as worked examples; the Legendre condition ∂²L/∂y′² ≥ 0 as a second-order necessary condition for a minimum; natural boundary conditions when an endpoint is free; isoperimetric constraints handled by a Lagrange multiplier on the constraint functional ∫G = constant

### Numerical computing

- Floating point: IEEE 754, rounding, decimal versus binary; fixed-point formats as an integer with an implied binary point, Qm.n with range and resolution 2⁻ⁿ, where overflow saturates or wraps; the IEEE 754 binary32 and binary64 formats with a sign, a biased exponent and a significand, with machine epsilon 2⁻⁵² ≈ 2.2 × 10⁻¹⁶ for binary64

- Overflow and underflow; stable reformulations: `log1p`, log-sum-exp, stable softmax
- Conditioning and the stability of algorithms; iterative solvers; the condition number κ(A) = ‖A‖·‖A⁻¹‖ built from induced matrix norms, with the spectral norm ‖A‖₂ equal to the largest singular value, bounding how a relative change in b is amplified into a relative change in x; backward stability, an algorithm being backward stable when its computed result is the exact result for a slightly perturbed input, with relative perturbation of order machine epsilon, so that the forward error is bounded by about κ(A) times that perturbation; Gaussian elimination with partial pivoting is stable in practice, its backward error bounded by a growth factor times machine epsilon, and Householder QR is backward stable
- Numerical differentiation (finite differences, gradient checking) and integration; quadrature rules: the trapezoidal rule with error −(h²/12)(b − a)f″ for a smooth integrand, Simpson's rule with error of order h⁴, Gauss–Legendre quadrature with n nodes exact for polynomials of degree up to 2n − 1, and adaptive quadrature that subdivides where a local error estimate is large

- **Projection methods and successive approximation**: fixed-point iteration x_{k+1} = T(x_k) and the contraction mapping theorem, with geometric convergence at the rate of the contraction constant; alternating projections onto convex sets; stopping on residuals rather than on iteration count

### Information theory

- Entropy H(X) = −Σ p·log₂ p
- Cross-entropy, KL divergence and mutual information; Gibbs' inequality D(p‖q) ≥ 0 with equality only when p = q, and the data-processing inequality, under which a Markov chain X → Y → Z satisfies I(X; Z) ≤ I(X; Y); the chain rules H(X, Y) = H(X) + H(Y | X) and I(X; Y) = H(X) − H(X | Y); conditioning does not increase entropy, H(X | Y) ≤ H(X); the log-sum inequality; and Fano's inequality H(X | Y) ≤ H(P_e) + P_e log(|𝒳| − 1) for any estimator of X from Y with error probability P_e
- Log loss, perplexity and calibration; calibration as the match between predicted probability and observed frequency, checked with reliability diagrams over bins and the expected calibration error ECE = Σ_b (n_b/n)|acc_b − conf_b|; the Brier score (1/n)Σᵢ(pᵢ − yᵢ)² as a proper scoring rule; temperature scaling, which fits one scalar on held-out data to recalibrate a softmax
- Shannon's source-coding bound; Huffman codes within one bit per symbol of it; the Kraft inequality Σ 2^(−ℓᵢ) ≤ 1 as the condition on codeword lengths ℓᵢ for a prefix code to exist; arithmetic coding, which represents a whole message as one point in a nested sequence of intervals narrowed by each symbol's probability, with a code length within about 2 bits of nH for a message of n symbols, so that it avoids the up-to-one-bit-per-symbol loss of Huffman codes
- Why compressed or well-encrypted data looks uniformly random
- Channel capacity; the binary symmetric channel with crossover probability p, whose capacity is 1 − H(p) bits per use, and capacity as the maximum of I(X; Y) over input distributions; the channel coding theorem: every rate R < C is achievable, with error probability tending to zero as the block length grows, and no rate above C is, shown by a random-coding argument for achievability and by Fano's inequality for the converse
- **Differential entropy and the Gaussian channel**: h(X) = −∫ f log f for densities, which can be negative and shifts by log|Jacobian| under a change of variables; the Gaussian as the maximum-entropy density for a fixed variance; the capacity C = ½ log(1 + P/N) of the additive Gaussian noise channel
- **Asymptotic equipartition property**: −(1/n) log p(X₁…Xₙ) → H(X) in probability for i.i.d. sources; the typical set of about 2^(nH) sequences carrying almost all probability; the consequence that about nH bits suffice for lossless coding
- **Rate distortion theory**: R(D) = min I(X; X̂) over test channels whose expected distortion is at most D; the Gaussian source with R(D) = ½ log(σ²/D) for D ≤ σ²; the rate–fidelity trade-off in lossy compression
- **Entropy rates of stochastic processes**: H = lim (1/n) H(X₁…Xₙ) for stationary processes, equal to the limit of H(Xₙ | Xₙ₋₁…X₁); the entropy rate of a stationary Markov chain as the stationary-weighted entropy of its transition rows
- **Network information theory**: multi-user sources and channels; the multiple-access channel capacity region; the broadcast channel; the Slepian–Wolf region for distributed lossless compression with rates H(X|Y), H(Y|X) and H(X, Y); the multiple-access region as the rate pairs with R₁ ≤ I(X₁; Y | X₂), R₂ ≤ I(X₂; Y | X₁) and R₁ + R₂ ≤ I(X₁, X₂; Y) for independent inputs, with the Gaussian sum rate ½ log(1 + (P₁ + P₂)/N)
- **Sparse graph codes (LDPC)**: linear codes defined by a sparse parity-check matrix whose Tanner graph is bipartite; belief-propagation decoding by message passing; the threshold near Shannon capacity under density evolution for irregular degree distributions; the sum-product rule on the factor graph, under which each variable-to-check message is the product of the messages arriving from the other checks and each check-to-variable message sums over the configurations of the other variables, exact on a tree and iterated on a graph with cycles
- **Other code families: convolutional, turbo and fountain codes**: convolutional codes as a finite-state shift register with generator polynomials, decoded by the Viterbi algorithm in time proportional to N·2^(K−1) for a block of N symbols and constraint length K; turbo codes, two convolutional encoders joined by an interleaver and decoded iteratively by exchanging extrinsic log-likelihood ratios; fountain codes (LT and Raptor), which emit an unbounded stream of XOR combinations of source symbols drawn from the robust soliton degree distribution and are decoded by belief propagation, the receiver recovering the source from slightly more than k received symbols
- **Information theory and statistics**: maximum likelihood as minimization of the KL divergence from the empirical distribution, since (1/n)Σᵢ log p_θ(xᵢ) = −H(p̂) − D(p̂ ‖ p_θ), so the maximum-likelihood model is the closest to the data in relative entropy; the Chernoff–Stein lemma, under which, for a null P tested against an alternative Q with the type I error held below a fixed ε, the best type II error decays as 2^(−n D(P ‖ Q)); Sanov's theorem, under which the probability that the empirical distribution of n samples from P falls in a set E decays as 2^(−n min over q in E of D(q ‖ P))

### Signals and sequences

- Sampling and aliasing
- Convolution and correlation in one and two dimensions; separable kernels applied as two one-dimensional passes, costing 2k multiplications per pixel instead of k²; the two-dimensional sum (f ∗ g)[m, n] = Σᵢ Σⱼ f[i, j]·g[m − i, n − j] over the support of the kernel, with zero-padding or reflection at the boundary

- Sequence probability: n-gram models and perplexity; finite-state tokenizers
- **Discrete cosine transform**: DCT-II, X_k = Σₙ x_n cos(π(n + ½)k/N), with its orthonormal scaling, computed through an N-point FFT in O(N log N); energy compaction, whereby a smooth signal's energy concentrates in a few low-frequency coefficients, which is why the 8 × 8 block DCT is the transform inside JPEG; the inverse transform DCT-III, which reconstructs the signal
- **Distribution of a sum of independent random variables**: for independent X and Y, the density of X + Y is the convolution ∫ f_X(x)·f_Y(z − x) dx, and the PMF of a discrete sum is the discrete convolution of the PMFs; the probability generating function of a sum is the product of the PGFs; sums of independent normals are normal, Poisson variables with rates add and gamma variables with a common rate add within their families, and repeated convolution of a density with itself is the mechanism behind the central limit theorem
- **Signal averaging**: averaging N aligned repeats of a signal with independent noise of standard deviation σ leaves the signal and cuts the noise standard deviation to σ/√N, so the signal-to-noise ratio rises by √N; the average is the sample mean with variance σ²/N; averaging power spectra over frames, rather than complex spectra whose phases are not aligned, reduces the variance of a spectral estimate in proportion to the number of frames

### Decision mathematics

- Markov decision processes; the Bellman optimality equation V*(s) = maxₐ Σₛ′ P(s′ | s, a)[r + γV*(s′)], value iteration as its repeated application, converging because the Bellman operator is a γ-contraction; temporal-difference learning, V(s) ← V(s) + α(r + γV(s′) − V(s)), which bootstraps from the estimate at the next state, against Monte Carlo returns that wait for the full episode; Q-learning as its off-policy analogue
- Multi-armed and contextual bandits: epsilon-greedy, UCB, Thompson sampling; the linear contextual bandit with expected reward θᵀx and LinUCB's ridge-regression confidence bound; combinatorial bandits, where each round selects a set of arms under a combinatorial constraint and the reward is a sum of the arms' rewards, with a UCB index per arm and an offline oracle that finds the best feasible set; non-stationary bandits, where the reward distributions drift, handled by sliding-window or discounted UCB that forgets old observations, with regret bounds that grow with the number of breakpoints; the Lai–Robbins lower bound, under which any algorithm that is consistent on every bandit instance has regret at least (1 + o(1)) log T · Σ over suboptimal arms i of Δᵢ / KL(νᵢ ‖ ν*), where Δᵢ is the gap of arm i and ν* the law of the best arm, with KL-UCB matching it asymptotically; the minimax lower bound of order √(KT), obtained from a pair of instances whose arm gaps are of order √(K/T) and that no algorithm can reliably distinguish
- **Decision theory (expected loss, Bayes decision rule)**: a loss L(θ, a) and risk R(θ, δ) = E[L(θ, δ(X))]; Bayes risk averaged over a prior; the Bayes decision rule choosing the action of least posterior expected loss; the posterior mean for squared-error loss, the posterior median for absolute loss and the MAP estimate for 0–1 loss
- **Voting methods, apportionment and fair division**: plurality, the Borda count and the Condorcet winner, with Arrow's impossibility theorem that no ranked voting method over unrestricted preferences with at least three candidates satisfies unanimity, independence of irrelevant alternatives and non-dictatorship together; apportionment of seats among states by Hamilton's largest-remainder method, Jefferson's and D'Hondt's divisor method and Webster's and Sainte-Laguë's divisor method, with the Balinski–Young theorem that no method satisfies quota and house monotonicity together; fair division with envy-free allocations and the last-diminisher procedure for proportional cake cutting
